Abacus

JAH-PC-90000001 · historic · c. 2700–2300 BCE

Rows of beads on rods are slid by hand to add, subtract, multiply, and divide. Each column stands for a place value, so the beads remember the running total.

One of the oldest known calculating tools. Early forms appeared in ancient Mesopotamia; bead frames were later used in Rome, in China as the suanpan, and in Japan as the soroban.

Antikythera Mechanism

JAH-PC-90000002 · historic · c. 2nd–1st century BCE

A box of bronze gears that predicted the positions of the sun and moon, the dates of eclipses, and the cycles of the ancient games. Turning one crank moved pointers across dials on both faces.

Recovered from a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera in the early 1900s. It is the most complex geared mechanism known from the ancient world, and its purpose was decoded only with modern X-ray imaging.

Pascaline

JAH-PC-90000003 · historic · 1642

A brass box where numbered wheels are turned with a stylus to add and subtract. Carry from one wheel to the next happens automatically inside the gearing.

Invented by French mathematician Blaise Pascal in 1642 to help his father with tax arithmetic. Fewer than sixty were built, and surviving machines sit in museums in Paris and elsewhere.

Leibniz Stepped Reckoner

JAH-PC-90000004 · historic · 1673

Cylindrical drums with teeth of increasing length turn number wheels to multiply and divide, not just add. The operator sets the number on sliders and cranks through the calculation.

Invented by German polymath Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in 1673. The stepped drum became the standard multiplying mechanism for nearly two centuries of later machines.

Difference Engine

JAH-PC-90000005 · historic · Designed 1820s

A gear machine designed to compute mathematical tables by the method of finite differences, stamping results ready for printing. It used only addition and carriage, never multiplication.

Designed by English mathematician Charles Babbage in the 1820s. The full engine was never built in his lifetime; working replicas were completed from his drawings in London in the 1990s.

Analytical Engine

JAH-PC-90000006 · historic · Designed 1830s–1840s

A design with a mill for arithmetic, a store for memory, and punched cards to direct the sequence of operations — the same three parts a modern computer has. Programs could branch and loop.

Designed by Charles Babbage in the 1830s and 1840s with notes published by Ada Lovelace, who wrote a program for it. It was never built, but its architecture described the stored-program concept a century early.

Jacquard Loom

JAH-PC-90000007 · historic · 1804

A weaving loom whose pattern is set by a chain of punched cards, one card per row of the fabric. Each card's holes decide which threads lift, weaving complex images automatically.

Invented by French weaver Joseph-Marie Jacquard in 1804. Its punched cards were the direct ancestor of the cards that programmed early computers.

Hollerith Tabulating Machine

JAH-PC-90000008 · historic · 1890

Cards punched with people's data are read electrically by pins dipping through the holes into mercury cups. Dials count each category as cards feed through by hand and later by machine.

Invented by American engineer Herman Hollerith and used in the 1890 United States Census. His company grew into part of what became IBM.

Zuse Z3

JAH-PC-90000009 · historic · 1941

A machine of telephone relays that read programs from punched film. It worked in binary floating point and could run stored instruction sequences automatically.

Built by German engineer Konrad Zuse and completed in 1941. The original was destroyed in wartime bombing; a reconstruction stands in a Munich museum.

Harvard Mark I

JAH-PC-90000010 · historic · 1944

A 51-foot machine of switches, relays, and shafts that computed ballistics and physics tables automatically. It could multiply in about six seconds and printed its answers on punch cards or type.

Built by IBM for Harvard University under Howard Aiken and completed in 1944. It ran for the United States Navy during the war.

Colossus

JAH-PC-90000011 · historic · 1944

Two thousand vacuum tubes processed punched tape at high speed to find the settings of an enemy cipher machine. It compared patterns statistically and printed its scores for analysts.

Built at Bletchley Park in England by engineer Tommy Flowers and first working in 1944. It attacked the Lorenz cipher; the work stayed secret for decades.

ENIAC

JAH-PC-90000012 · historic · 1945

Seventeen thousand vacuum tubes performed additions at electronic speed, programmed by plugging cables into panels like a telephone switchboard. A calculation that took days by hand finished in seconds.

Built at the University of Pennsylvania by J. Presper Eckert and John Mauchly and dedicated in 1946 after wartime development beginning in 1943. It filled a large room and drew enormous power.

Manchester Baby

JAH-PC-90000013 · historic · 1948

A small machine that held both its instructions and its data in the same electronic memory — the stored-program idea. Its first program, in June 1948, found the highest factor of a number.

Built at the University of Manchester by Frederic Williams and Tom Kilburn. That first run proved the stored-program concept that every modern computer uses.

EDSAC

JAH-PC-90000014 · historic · 1949

A mercury-delay-line memory machine that ran real scientific jobs for university researchers, including programs for biology and radio astronomy. Users prepared programs on punched tape.

Built at the University of Cambridge by Maurice Wilkes and running in 1949. It was the first computer to offer a regular computing service to outside users.

UNIVAC I

JAH-PC-90000015 · historic · 1951

The first computer sold as a commercial product in the United States, famous for predicting a presidential election result on television. It read metal magnetic tape and printed its output.

Built by Eckert and Mauchly and delivered in 1951, with the first machine going to the United States Census Bureau. It made the computer a public idea overnight.

IBM 701

JAH-PC-90000016 · historic · 1952

IBM's first commercial scientific computer, renting to aircraft companies and government labs. It used electrostatic storage tubes for memory and magnetic tape for data.

Announced by IBM in 1952 and first installed in 1953. It pulled IBM — then a punch-card company — into the electronic computer age.

IBM 650

JAH-PC-90000017 · historic · 1954

A drum-memory machine that became the most popular computer of its decade, used for payroll, grades, and science alike. Programs were loaded from punched cards.

Announced by IBM in 1953 and shipping from 1954. More than two thousand were installed — enormous numbers for the time.

TX-0

JAH-PC-90000018 · historic · 1956

One of the first computers built with transistors instead of vacuum tubes, which made it far smaller and cooler. It gave hackers a machine they could use directly, one at a time.

Built at MIT Lincoln Laboratory in 1956. Its interactive style shaped the hacker culture at MIT that later produced early video games.

PDP-1

JAH-PC-90000019 · historic · 1960

A compact machine with a round cathode-ray display and a light pen, sold to labs that wanted hands-on computing. Programmers wrote the first video game, Spacewar!, on it.

Built by Digital Equipment Corporation and shipped in 1960. Its low price for the time put real computing in university labs.

IBM System/360

JAH-PC-90000020 · historic · 1964

A line of compatible computers from small business machines to giant mainframes, all running the same programs. It introduced the 8-bit byte as the industry standard.

Announced by IBM in 1964. It was one of the largest private engineering projects of its era and made IBM dominant for decades.

PDP-8

JAH-PC-90000021 · historic · 1965

A machine the size of a small refrigerator that a single department could afford and keep. It sold by the tens of thousands and carried computing into labs and factories.

Built by Digital Equipment Corporation in 1965. Its simple 12-bit design became one of the best-selling computers of its generation.

Intel 4004

JAH-PC-90000022 · historic · 1971

The whole central processor of a computer on one chip — four bits wide, running at under a megahertz. It was made for a calculator and ended up starting the microprocessor age.

Released by Intel in 1971, designed by Federico Faggin, Ted Hoff, and Stanley Mazor. Its success led directly to the 8008, 8080, and the PC era.

Intel 8008

JAH-PC-90000023 · historic · 1972

An 8-bit processor on one chip that could address far more memory than the 4004. It powered early hobby machines and terminals.

Released by Intel in 1972. Hobbyists built some of the first personal computers around it before the 8080 arrived.

Altair 8800

JAH-PC-90000024 · historic · 1975

A kit computer with toggle switches on the front and no keyboard or screen at first. Hobbyists flipped in programs bit by bit and watched the lights answer.

Built by MITS and announced in 1975. Its fame drew a young Paul Allen and Bill Gates, who wrote a BASIC interpreter for it.

Apple I

JAH-PC-90000025 · historic · 1976

A bare circuit board sold without case, keyboard, or power supply — the buyer finished the computer. It displayed text on an ordinary television.

Designed by Steve Wozniak and sold by Apple Computer in 1976. Only about two hundred were made, and surviving boards are museum treasures.

TRS-80 Model I

JAH-PC-90000026 · historic · 1977

A complete home computer sold through Radio Shack stores with keyboard, screen, and cassette storage. Ordinary families could buy one off the shelf.

Launched by Tandy Corporation in 1977. It was one of the 1977 trio of machines that opened the home-computer market.

Apple II

JAH-PC-90000027 · historic · 1977

A home computer with color graphics, sound, and slots for expansion cards. It ran the first spreadsheet program, which gave businesses a reason to buy it.

Launched by Apple Computer in 1977 and designed by Steve Wozniak. It sold for years and funded Apple's growth.

Osborne 1

JAH-PC-90000028 · historic · 1981

A sewing-machine-sized computer with a tiny 5-inch screen and a handle, sold with a bundle of business software. It was luggable before laptops existed.

Released by Osborne Computer Corporation in 1981. It proved there was a market for computers you could carry.

BBC Micro

JAH-PC-90000029 · historic · 1981

A sturdy school computer made for the BBC's computer literacy project, with excellent BASIC and ports for experiments. A generation learned to program on it.

Built by Acorn Computers for the BBC and released in 1981. It dominated British schools through the 1980s.

IBM PC 5150

JAH-PC-90000030 · historic · 1981

A business machine with an open design that other companies could copy, which created the clone industry. Its slot architecture and DOS software became the world standard.

Released by IBM in 1981. Because IBM used off-the-shelf parts and published the design, compatible machines flooded the market.

ZX Spectrum

JAH-PC-90000031 · historic · 1982

A tiny rubber-keyed computer that plugged into the TV and loaded games from cassette tape in minutes. Millions of kids learned to code on it.

Released by Sinclair Research in the United Kingdom in 1982. Its low price made it one of Europe's best-selling home computers.

Commodore 64

JAH-PC-90000032 · historic · 1982

Sixty-four kilobytes of memory, famous sound and sprite graphics, and a huge game library. It became the best-selling single computer model of all time.

Released by Commodore in 1982. It stayed in production for over a decade and anchored the 1980s home-computer boom.

Macintosh 128K

JAH-PC-90000033 · historic · 1984

The first affordable computer with a graphical screen, windows, and a mouse for ordinary buyers. What you saw on screen was what printed.

Released by Apple Computer in 1984 with a famous television advertisement. It set the pattern for every computer desktop that followed.

Atari ST

JAH-PC-90000034 · historic · 1985

A sixteen-bit machine with a crisp monochrome display option and built-in MIDI ports that made it the studio standard for musicians. It brought real power to a home price.

Released by Atari in 1985. Its MIDI ports made it the backbone of countless recording studios.

Amiga 1000

JAH-PC-90000035 · historic · 1985

A machine with custom chips for graphics and sound that could do animation and stereo music years ahead of rivals. Video producers used it for titling and effects.

Released by Commodore in 1985. Its custom chipset made it a legend for games, art, and desktop video.

Motorola 68000

JAH-PC-90000036 · historic · 1979

A processor with a clean 32-bit design inside that powered the Macintosh, the Amiga, the Atari ST, and early workstations. Programmers loved its tidy instruction set.

Released by Motorola in 1979. It carried the personal-computer world from 8 and 16 bits toward 32-bit computing.

x86-64 Desktop PC

JAH-PC-90000037 · modern · 2003–present

The standard home and office computer: a 64-bit processor, gigabytes of memory, and slots for graphics and storage. It runs everything from games to engineering software.

The 64-bit x86 design became the common desktop standard in the early 2000s. It remains the platform most personal software is written for.

ARM Smartphone SoC

JAH-PC-90000038 · modern · 2010s–present

A single chip holding the processor, graphics, radio, and memory controller for a phone. It sips power so the battery lasts all day while running demanding apps.

ARM-based chips took over smartphones in the 2010s with designs from many makers. The whole phone computer fits on one fingernail-sized piece of silicon.

Apple Silicon Mac

JAH-PC-90000039 · modern · 2020–present

A Mac where the processor, graphics, and memory live on one efficient chip. It runs cool and quiet while beating older multi-chip designs.

Apple moved the Mac to its own ARM-based chips beginning in 2020. The unified memory design lets the processor and graphics share data without copying.

RISC-V Developer Board

JAH-PC-90000040 · modern · 2010s–present

A computer built on the free and open RISC-V instruction set, so anyone can study and build on its design. It runs Linux and standard software.

The RISC-V instruction set was created at UC Berkeley and published openly starting in 2010. Open hardware lets small teams build custom processors.

GPU Compute Node

JAH-PC-90000041 · modern · 2010s–present

A server packed with graphics processors that do thousands of simple calculations at once. It trains AI models and renders films in hours instead of months.

General-purpose GPU computing took off in the late 2000s and 2010s. Modern AI training runs almost entirely on such machines.

Single-Board Computer

JAH-PC-90000042 · modern · 2012–present

A complete computer on one small board with ports for screen, USB, and electronics projects. It runs a full operating system from a memory card.

Popularized by the Raspberry Pi, first released in 2012. Such boards put real computers in classrooms, hobby benches, and products worldwide.

Microcontroller Board

JAH-PC-90000043 · modern · 2000s–present

A tiny chip with processor, memory, and input-output pins that runs a single program forever — inside toys, thermostats, and robots. It costs little and sips power.

Microcontroller boards made embedded programming a hobby in the 2000s. Billions of such chips ship every year inside everyday objects.

Game Console SoC

JAH-PC-90000044 · modern · 2013–present

A custom chip combining a strong processor and graphics to run detailed games on the television. It is tuned for one job: fast, beautiful play.

Modern consoles since 2013 use custom single-chip designs. They deliver supercomputer-class graphics at a home price.

Datacenter Server Blade

JAH-PC-90000045 · modern · 2000s–present

A slim computer that slides into a rack with dozens of siblings, serving websites and files around the clock. It is built for reliability, not looks.

Blade servers became standard in datacenters in the 2000s. The internet's services run on racks of such machines.

AI Accelerator Pod

JAH-PC-90000046 · modern · 2016–present

A chip designed for one thing: the matrix math that AI runs on. It does in seconds what would take a regular processor hours.

Specialized AI chips appeared in the mid-2010s as deep learning grew. They now power most large-scale AI training and search.

FPGA Development Board

JAH-PC-90000047 · modern · 1980s–present

A chip whose logic can be rewired after manufacturing, so it becomes any digital circuit the designer describes. It sits between software and custom chips.

Field-programmable gate arrays have been sold since the 1980s. They prototype new processors and accelerate special jobs.

Supercomputer Cluster Node

JAH-PC-90000048 · modern · 1990s–present

Thousands of computers linked by fast networks act as one machine for weather, physics, and AI. Each node is ordinary; the teamwork is extraordinary.

Cluster supercomputers grew through the 1990s and 2000s. The fastest machines on Earth are built this way.

Edge AI Device

JAH-PC-90000049 · modern · 2018–present

A small device that runs AI right where it sits — a camera that counts cars, a speaker that hears commands — with no internet needed. Private and instant.

Edge AI hardware spread in the late 2010s. Running models locally keeps data private and answers instant.

Automotive Control Unit

JAH-PC-90000050 · modern · 1980s–present

Dozens of small rugged computers inside a car manage the engine, brakes, and airbags. They must never crash, so they are built to extreme reliability standards.

Electronic control units spread through cars from the 1980s onward. A modern car carries dozens of them.

Satellite Flight Computer

JAH-PC-90000051 · modern · 1960s–present

A radiation-hardened computer that flies the spacecraft, aims the antennas, and runs the science instruments. It must work for years with no repair shop.

Hardened computers have flown since the early space age. They trade speed for the ability to survive cosmic rays.

Network Appliance

JAH-PC-90000052 · modern · 1990s–present

A specialized computer that forwards data packets between networks at wire speed. It is the reason your messages find their way.

Routers became standalone products in the 1990s as the internet grew. Every network depends on them.

Home Storage Server

JAH-PC-90000053 · modern · 2000s–present

A box of hard drives on the home network that keeps every photo and file safe with automatic copies. It replaces renting cloud storage.

Network storage boxes became consumer products in the 2000s. They guard family data at home instead of on distant servers.

Laptop SoC System

JAH-PC-90000054 · modern · 1990s–present

A full computer folded into a portable shape: efficient processor, battery, screen, and keyboard in one. It is the most common personal computer form today.

Laptops overtook desktops in sales in the 2000s. Modern designs run all day on efficient chips.

Smartwatch Computer

JAH-PC-90000055 · modern · 2015–present

A complete computer on the wrist that tracks health, shows messages, and runs apps on a tiny efficient chip. It charges rarely and works all day.

Modern smartwatches matured in the mid-2010s. Their tiny chips do serious work on a watch battery.

E-Reader Board

JAH-PC-90000056 · modern · 2007–present

A low-power computer built around a paper-like display that holds thousands of books. It reads for weeks on one charge.

E-ink readers became mainstream in the late 2000s. The display sips power because it only uses energy when the page changes.

Superconducting Qubit Processor

JAH-PC-90000057 · quantum · 2010s–present

Qubits made of superconducting circuits chilled near absolute zero, controlled by microwave pulses. They run quantum algorithms that no regular computer can simulate at scale.

Superconducting qubits were developed in university and company labs through the 2000s and 2010s. They are the most widely built gate-based quantum processors today.

Trapped-Ion Processor

JAH-PC-90000058 · quantum · 1990s–present

Individual charged atoms float in electromagnetic traps, and lasers flip their quantum states. Ions of the same kind are perfectly identical, which makes them excellent qubits.

Trapped-ion quantum computing grew from 1990s atomic-physics experiments. Commercial trapped-ion machines are sold as cloud services today.

Photonic Quantum Processor

JAH-PC-90000059 · quantum · 2010s–present

Qubits carried by single particles of light race through chips of waveguides and detectors. Photons barely interact with noise, and the chips can work closer to room temperature.

Photonic quantum computing advanced rapidly in the 2010s and 2020s. Light-based machines have demonstrated landmark quantum experiments.

Quantum Annealer

JAH-PC-90000060 · quantum · 2011–present

A machine that solves optimization puzzles by letting quantum bits naturally settle into their lowest-energy arrangement. It is built for one kind of math, done extremely well.

Commercial quantum annealers have been sold since 2011. They tackle scheduling, routing, and sampling problems.

Neutral-Atom Array

JAH-PC-90000061 · quantum · 2010s–present

Hundreds of neutral atoms are held in place by focused laser beams like tweezers, then excited to giant Rydberg states to interact. The array can rearrange itself for each problem.

Neutral-atom quantum machines emerged in the late 2010s from atomic-physics labs. They now offer hundreds of qubits as cloud services.

Silicon Spin Qubit

JAH-PC-90000062 · quantum · 2010s–present

Single electrons trapped in tiny silicon dots use their spin as a qubit. Because they are made with chip-factory methods, they could one day sit beside regular transistors.

Silicon spin qubits were demonstrated in research labs in the 2010s. Their compatibility with chip manufacturing is their great promise.

NMR Quantum Computer

JAH-PC-90000063 · quantum · 1997–2000s

The first machines to run real quantum algorithms used the atomic nuclei inside liquid molecules, read by the same physics as hospital MRI scanners. A famous factoring demo ran on seven qubits.

Liquid-state NMR quantum computing was demonstrated in 1997 and factored the number 15 in 2001. It was a landmark proof that quantum algorithms work.

Diamond NV Quantum Register

JAH-PC-90000064 · quantum · 2000s–present

Atomic-scale defects in diamond hold quantum states that survive at room temperature. They sense magnetic fields with exquisite precision as well as compute.

Nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamond have been studied since the 2000s. They are prized for working without deep freezing.

Cold-Atom Quantum Simulator

JAH-PC-90000065 · quantum · 2010s–present

Clouds of atoms chilled to near absolute zero mimic the quantum behavior of materials and molecules. Instead of calculating nature, the machine becomes a small piece of it.

Cold-atom simulators grew from Bose-Einstein condensation research in the 2000s. They now model quantum materials in the lab.

Rydberg Atom Processor

JAH-PC-90000066 · quantum · 2018–present

Atoms puffed up to huge Rydberg states interact powerfully over long distances, making fast quantum gates. Laser tweezers place each atom exactly where the problem needs it.

Rydberg-atom processors became commercial in the late 2010s and early 2020s. Their reconfigurable layouts suit optimization and simulation.

Hybrid QPU-CPU Appliance

JAH-PC-90000067 · quantum · 2020s–present

A box that pairs a small quantum processor with a regular computer, splitting each job: the classical side prepares and the quantum side solves the hard kernel. They iterate together.

Hybrid quantum-classical systems became the standard way to use quantum hardware in the 2020s. The classical computer handles everything the quantum chip cannot.

Topological Qubit Research

JAH-PC-90000068 · quantum · 2010s–present (research)

An experimental approach where quantum information is stored in the braided paths of exotic particles, protected from noise by topology itself. It is still laboratory research.

Topological quantum computing has been pursued in labs since the 2000s, with major claims and retractions along the way. It remains an active research frontier.

Silicon CMOS Substrate

JAH-PC-90000069 · materials · 1950s–present

Purified sand becomes the crystal wafer on which billions of transistors are printed. Complementary pairs of transistors switch with almost no wasted power.

Silicon transistors date to the 1950s, and the CMOS process made them dominant by the 1980s. Nearly every chip in the world is still silicon.

Germanium Transistor

JAH-PC-90000070 · materials · 1947–1960s

The very first transistors were built on germanium, which switches at lower voltage than silicon. It ruled the early years before silicon took over.

The point-contact transistor of 1947 at Bell Labs used germanium. Silicon replaced it for most uses by the 1960s, but germanium lives on in fast and infrared devices.

Gallium Arsenide Chip

JAH-PC-90000071 · materials · 1970s–present

A compound semiconductor that moves electrons far faster than silicon, used in phone radios, lasers, and satellites. It costs more but wins where speed matters.

Gallium arsenide devices became practical in the 1970s. They power the radio front-ends of mobile phones and high-efficiency solar cells.

Graphene Transistor

JAH-PC-90000072 · materials · 2004–present (research)

A sheet of carbon one atom thick that conducts electricity better than copper and is stronger than steel. Transistors built on it could switch at extreme speeds.

Graphene was isolated in 2004 at the University of Manchester, work that won the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics. Transistor research continues in labs worldwide.

Diamond NV-Center Substrate

JAH-PC-90000073 · materials · 2000s–present (research)

Diamond crystals with single-atom defects hold quantum states at room temperature. They sense magnetic fields atom by atom and can serve as qubits.

Nitrogen-vacancy research grew through the 2000s. Room-temperature quantum behavior makes diamond unique among qubit materials.

Memristor Crossbar Array

JAH-PC-90000074 · materials · 2008–present (research)

A grid of resistors that remember their resistance, so the memory itself can do the math. Multiplying matrices happens in the wires, not in a processor.

The memristor was proposed in theory in 1971 and built at HP Labs in 2008. Crossbar arrays are a leading candidate for in-memory AI computing.

Silicon Photonic Chip

JAH-PC-90000075 · materials · 2000s–present

Light guides etched into silicon carry data as photons instead of electrons, moving information faster with less heat. It already links datacenters; logic is next.

Silicon photonics matured in the 2000s and 2010s for fiber-optic links. Researchers now build photonic processors for AI math.

DNA Storage-Compute

JAH-PC-90000076 · materials · 2012–present (research)

Digital files encoded into synthetic DNA strands store staggering amounts of data in a speck of material. Chemical reactions can also run simple computations on the strands.

DNA data storage was demonstrated by researchers in 2012 and has stored books, images, and video since. DNA computing experiments date to the 1990s.

Spintronic MRAM Device

JAH-PC-90000077 · materials · 2000s–present

Memory cells that store bits in the magnetic direction of electron spins, keeping data with no power. It is fast like RAM and permanent like a disk.

Spin-transfer-torque MRAM entered products in the 2010s. It promises computers that never need to boot because memory never forgets.

Carbon Nanotube Transistor

JAH-PC-90000078 · materials · 1998–present (research)

Transistors made from rolled sheets of carbon atoms, thinner and potentially faster than silicon channels. Whole tiny computers have been built from them in labs.

Carbon nanotube transistors were demonstrated in 1998, and a complete nanotube computer ran in 2013 at Stanford. Manufacturing at scale remains research.

Silicon Carbide Power Device

JAH-PC-90000079 · materials · 1990s–present

A hard crystal that switches high voltages and shrugs off heat that would kill silicon. It drives electric cars and solar inverters efficiently.

Silicon carbide power devices became commercial in the 1990s and 2000s. They are now standard in electric-vehicle drivetrains.

Gallium Nitride Device

JAH-PC-90000080 · materials · 2000s–present

A crystal that switches power at extreme speed, shrinking phone chargers to pocket size. It also makes the blue and white LEDs that light the world.

Gallium nitride power devices became consumer products in the 2010s, and GaN LEDs won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics. Fast charging is its famous trick.

2D TMD Transistor (MoS2)

JAH-PC-90000081 · materials · 2011–present (research)

Transistors built from a single layer of molybdenum disulfide, a crystal that is a natural semiconductor unlike graphene. Stacked layers could build 3D chips.

Single-layer MoS2 transistors were demonstrated in 2011. Two-dimensional semiconductors are a leading research path beyond silicon.

Phase-Change Memory Compute

JAH-PC-90000082 · materials · 2000s–present

A material that flips between glassy and crystalline states to store bits, and can do arithmetic in the flip itself. It bridges memory and processor.

Phase-change memory shipped in products in the 2010s. Researchers use its gradual switching for brain-like computing.

Ferroelectric FET

JAH-PC-90000083 · materials · 2010s–present (research)

A transistor whose gate remembers its electric polarization, storing a bit without power and switching at low voltage. It could make memory as fast as logic.

Ferroelectric transistors based on hafnium oxide advanced rapidly in the 2010s. They are candidates for ultra-low-power memory.

Superconducting Logic (RSFQ)

JAH-PC-90000084 · materials · 1980s–present (research)

Logic built from superconducting loops that pass single quantum pulses of magnetic flux, switching hundreds of times faster than silicon while barely warming.

Rapid single-flux-quantum logic was developed from the 1980s onward. It remains the fastest known digital logic family, needing deep cold.

Plasmonic Waveguide Chip

JAH-PC-90000085 · materials · 2000s–present (research)

Light waves squeezed along metal surfaces carry signals in spaces far smaller than a wavelength. It promises optics at electronic sizes.

Plasmonics grew as a research field in the 2000s. Squeezing light below the diffraction limit could merge photonics with nanoscale chips.

Semiconductor Superlattice

JAH-PC-90000086 · materials · 1970s–present (research)

Alternating atomic layers of different semiconductors create artificial crystals with engineered electronic properties. They make precise lasers and detectors.

Superlattices were proposed in 1970 and grown by molecular-beam epitaxy from the 1970s onward. Quantum-cascade lasers are their famous product.

Hybrid Classical-Quantum System

JAH-PC-90000087 · mixes · 2020s–present

A regular computer and a quantum processor work as one team on each problem. The classical side handles data and control; the quantum side attacks the hard core.

Hybrid workflows became the standard pattern for using quantum hardware in the 2020s. Every major quantum cloud runs this way.

Neuromorphic-Digital Hybrid

JAH-PC-90000088 · mixes · 2010s–present

A chip of artificial neurons that sips power sits beside a regular processor that handles exact math. Senses go to the neurons; spreadsheets go to the CPU.

Brain-inspired chips like TrueNorth and Loihi appeared in the 2010s. Hybrids pair them with conventional processors.

Analog-Digital Hybrid Computer

JAH-PC-90000089 · mixes · 1950s–1970s

Analog circuits integrate and differentiate continuously while a digital side sets up the problem and reads the answer. Old hybrids simulated aircraft and rockets this way.

Analog-digital hybrids were workhorses of aerospace simulation in the 1960s. Digital computers eventually absorbed their role.

FPGA-CPU Hybrid

JAH-PC-90000090 · mixes · 2010s–present

A regular processor shares its board with a rewirable logic chip that becomes custom hardware for the hot loop. Software speed where it counts, hardware speed where it matters.

CPU-FPGA hybrids became common in datacenters and embedded systems in the 2010s. The FPGA accelerates exactly the bottleneck.

Co-Packaged Photonic-Electronic

JAH-PC-90000091 · mixes · 2020s–present

Photonic chips that move data as light sit in the same package as the electronic chips that think. Data flies between them at light speed with little heat.

Co-packaged optics moved from research toward products in the 2020s. Datacenters need it as copper links run out of steam.

CPU-GPU Heterogeneous Node

JAH-PC-90000092 · mixes · 2000s–present

A few clever CPU cores direct thousands of simple GPU cores, splitting every job by talent. This pairing powers gaming, AI, and science computing.

Heterogeneous computing became mainstream in the 2000s with programmable GPUs. Nearly every fast computer today is a hybrid this way.
