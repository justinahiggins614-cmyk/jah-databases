Beeswax Candles simple · candles · beginner

Beeswax candles are made by dipping, molding, or rolling sheets of beeswax around a wick. Beeswax burns longer and with less smoke than many other candle waxes, holds its shape in warm rooms, and gives off a faint honey scent. A dipped candle is built up layer by layer, a molded candle is poured into a form, and a rolled candle is made by wrapping a sheet of wax around a wick, so one material gives three very different-looking candles.

Materials

  • Pure beeswax (filtered, yellow or white)
  • Cotton or hemp candle wicks, sized to the candle diameter
  • Double boiler or wax melter with thermometer
  • Candle molds or dipping frame, or beeswax sheets for rolled candles
  • Heat-safe pouring jug
  • Wick tabs and a wick holder or chopsticks
  • Kitchen scale

Method

  1. Choose a wick rated for the finished candle diameter; a wick that is too small tunnels and a wick that is too large smokes.
  2. Melt the beeswax in a double boiler to 70 to 75 degrees Celsius and skim any surface foam.
  3. For dipped candles, dip the weighted wick, let it cool for a few minutes, and repeat 20 to 40 times until the candle reaches the wanted diameter.
  4. For molded candles, thread the wick through the mold, seal the base, pour the wax, and top up twice as the wax cools and shrinks.
  5. For rolled candles, lay a beeswax sheet flat, place the wick along one short edge, and roll firmly and evenly.
  6. Trim the wick to about 6 mm, level the base on a warm flat surface, and cure the candle for a day before burning.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Never leave a burning candle unattended, and keep candles away from curtains and drafts.

Yield

Six pillar candles, 7 cm across and 15 cm tall, use about 3.5 kg of wax including a waste allowance; see the demo.

Variations

  • Dipped tapers in pairs for the table.
  • Molded pillars with a wide, slow flame.
  • Rolled honeycomb sheets in natural gold or ivory.
  • Scent-free plain beeswax, since the wax itself carries the scent.

Working demo program

import math

TITLE = "Beeswax Candles"
COUNT = 6
DIAMETER_CM = 7.0
HEIGHT_CM = 15.0
BURN_G_PER_HOUR = 7.0
WASTE_PCT = 10.0


def main():
    r = DIAMETER_CM / 2.0
    vol = math.pi * r * r * HEIGHT_CM
    weight = vol * 0.96
    total = weight * COUNT * (1.0 + WASTE_PCT / 100.0)
    burn = weight / BURN_G_PER_HOUR
    print(TITLE + " - candle wax calculator")
    print("One candle: %.1f g of wax" % weight)
    print("Burn time per candle: %.1f hours" % burn)
    print("Wax for %d candles (with %d%% waste allowance): %.1f g" % (COUNT, int(WASTE_PCT), total))

Beeswax Food Wraps simple · food storage · beginner

Beeswax food wraps are cotton cloth coated with beeswax, sometimes blended with a little plant resin and oil, that soften with the warmth of the hands and seal around bowls, fruit, and bread. They replace single-use plastic wrap for most cold and room-temperature foods, they wash clean in cool water, and they last for months before the coating wears thin and the wrap can be re-waxed or composted.

Materials

  • Plain cotton cloth, washed and dried
  • Food-grade beeswax pellets (E901)
  • Pine resin and jojoba oil (optional, for extra grip)
  • Baking paper (parchment)
  • Iron or oven
  • Scissors or pinking shears
  • Old baking tray

Method

  1. Cut the cloth to the wanted size, allowing a little extra for hems if the edges are to be hemmed.
  2. Sprinkle grated or pelleted food-grade beeswax evenly over the cloth, with resin and a few drops of oil if using them.
  3. Cover with a second sheet of baking paper and iron on medium heat, or bake at 100 degrees Celsius for a few minutes, until the wax melts evenly into the cloth.
  4. Lift the wrap while still warm, hold it up until it sets, and check for dry patches; add a little more wax and reheat any patch that looks bare.
  5. Cool flat, then wash in cool water with mild soap and air-dry.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Food-grade separation: only food-grade beeswax (E901) touches food. Wax of unknown grade, colored craft wax, and wax used for candles, polish, or display models is never used on food, and food equipment is never shared with non-food wax work. Wraps are not used on raw meat or fish, and they are never put in a microwave, oven, or dishwasher, where heat melts the coating.

Yield

One set of small, medium, and large wraps uses about 43 g of wax; see the demo.

Variations

  • Small wraps for half lemons and jars.
  • Medium wraps for sandwiches and bowls.
  • Large wraps for loaves and platters.
  • Re-waxing a tired wrap with fresh pellets instead of throwing it away.

SIZES = [("small", 20, 20), ("medium", 30, 30), ("large", 40, 40)]
WAX_G_PER_100CM2 = 12.0
CLOTH_G_PER_100CM2 = 9.0


def main():
    print(TITLE + " - food wrap wax calculator")

        print("%s wrap %dx%d cm: area %d sq cm, cloth %.1f g, wax %.1f g" % (name, w, h, area, cloth, wax))
    print("Wax for one set of three wraps: %.1f g" % total_wax)
    print("Wraps from a 500 g block of wax: %d" % int(500.0 / (total_wax / 3.0)))

Cheese Wax Coatings simple · food coatings · beginner

Cheese wax is a coating of beeswax, often blended with a little plant oil, brushed or dipped onto a cheese wheel to protect it while it ages. The coating slows moisture loss, keeps mold off the rind, and can be peeled away before eating. Hard and semi-hard cheeses take wax well, and the traditional colors of cheese wax, red or yellow, come from food-grade color added to the wax, not from the wax itself.

Materials

  • Food-grade beeswax (E901)
  • Food-grade plant oil (a small amount, for flexibility)
  • Cheese wheel, rind dry and clean
  • Natural-bristle brush or dipping pot
  • Thermometer
  • Food-grade color (optional)

Method

  1. Age the cheese until the rind is dry and firm, and brush off any surface mold before waxing.
  2. Melt food-grade beeswax with a little oil to 70 to 80 degrees Celsius, adding food-grade color if wanted.
  3. Brush on a thin, even coat, let it set, then brush a second coat, paying attention to the edges where the wheel meets its flat faces.
  4. Or dip the wheel briefly, let it drain, and dip again for a second coat once the first has set.
  5. Store the waxed wheel cool and humid for aging, and peel the wax away before cutting and eating.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Food-grade separation: only food-grade beeswax (E901) touches food. Wax of unknown grade, colored craft wax, and wax used for candles, polish, or display models is never used on food, and food equipment is never shared with non-food wax work. Wax that has coated a cheese is never re-melted for food use, and moldy wax is discarded.

Yield

A 20 cm wheel takes about 130 g of wax for two coats; see the demo.

Variations

  • Red or yellow wax for farmhouse styles.
  • Clear natural wax that shows the rind beneath.
  • Wax over cloth binding for long-aged wheels.

Fruit and Confection Coatings simple · food coatings · beginner

A thin coating of food-grade beeswax (E901) on fruit and confections keeps moisture in, slows shriveling, and gives a gentle shine. Apples, citrus, and firm candies are dipped or brushed with melted wax, cooled until the coat sets, and polished lightly. Only food-grade wax is ever used for this purpose: wax sold for candles, polish, or crafts is not food-grade, and it is never used on anything meant to be eaten.

Materials

  • Food-grade beeswax (E901) only
  • Fruit or confections, clean and fully dry
  • Dipping pot or brush
  • Thermometer
  • Cooling rack

Method

  1. Wash and dry the fruit or confections completely; any water under the wax turns cloudy and loosens the coat.
  2. Melt food-grade beeswax to 65 to 75 degrees Celsius in a clean pot used only for food wax.
  3. Dip each piece briefly or brush it evenly, let the excess drip off, and set it on a rack to cool.
  4. Apply a second thin coat if the first looks patchy, then polish lightly with a soft dry cloth once set.
  5. Store coated fruit cool, and peel or wash the coating off before eating if preferred.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Food-grade separation: only food-grade beeswax (E901) touches food. Wax of unknown grade, colored craft wax, and wax used for candles, polish, or display models is never used on food, and food equipment is never shared with non-food wax work. This record covers food-grade wax only; non-food-grade wax is excluded from food contact entirely.

Yield

Twelve apples take about 29 g of food-grade wax; see the demo.

Variations

  • Dipped apples for market stalls.
  • Brushed citrus for gift boxes.
  • Thin coats on firm caramels and nut clusters.

Lip Balm and Salves simple · cosmetics · beginner

Lip balm and salves are simple beeswax mixtures: wax gives the balm its body and staying power, a butter such as shea or cocoa butter makes it creamy, and a plant oil such as jojoba or sweet almond oil makes it spread easily. Melted together, poured into tubes or tins, and cooled, the mixture sets into a firm balm that melts on contact with warm skin. The ratio of wax to butter to oil sets the firmness, so more wax gives a harder balm and more oil gives a softer one.

Materials

  • Beeswax (cosmetic grade)
  • Shea butter or cocoa butter
  • Jojoba oil or sweet almond oil
  • Lip balm tubes or small tins
  • Double boiler
  • Kitchen scale
  • Optional: a drop of food-grade flavor oil

Method

  1. Weigh the wax, butter, and oil in the ratio 1 part wax to 1 part butter to 2 parts oil for a standard balm.
  2. Melt them together in a double boiler, stirring until the mixture is completely liquid and clear.
  3. Remove from the heat, stir in any flavor oil, and pour carefully into tubes or tins.
  4. Let the balms cool undisturbed until fully set, then cap them and label them with the date and ingredients.
  5. Store in a cool place; balm softens in heat and melts in a hot pocket or car.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Keep balm ingredients cosmetic-grade, patch-test any new balm on a small area of skin first, and never share lip balm tubes. Honey in balm is a flavor ingredient; this record makes no health claim for it.

Yield

A 120 g batch fills 25 standard tubes; see the demo.

Variations

  • A firmer balm for winter, with more wax.
  • A softer balm for summer tubes, with more oil.
  • Tinted balm with a pinch of cosmetic-grade mica.
  • Unscented balm for sensitive skin.

    print("Wax share of recipe: %.0f%%" % (WAX_G / total * 100.0))
    print("Usable after %d%% loss: %.1f g" % (int(LOSS_PCT), usable))
    print("Standard tubes filled (%.1f g each): %d" % (TUBE_G, int(usable / TUBE_G)))

Lotions and Creams compounded · cosmetics · intermediate

Emulsified wax creams are lotions and creams in which beeswax helps hold oil and water together. The wax is melted with the oil phase, the water phase is heated separately, and the two are blended with stirring until they cool into a smooth cream. Beeswax thickens the cream, gives it a protective feel on the skin, and helps it stay mixed instead of separating. A borax-free cream relies on the wax and an emulsifying wax or plant emulsifier to hold the emulsion.

Materials

  • Beeswax (cosmetic grade)
  • Plant oil (jojoba, sweet almond, or olive)
  • Distilled water or floral water
  • Plant emulsifier or emulsifying wax
  • Heat-safe beakers or jars
  • Stick blender or whisk
  • Preservative suited to creams (follow its maker's instructions)
  • Jars, sterilized

Method

  1. Heat the wax, oil, and emulsifier together to about 70 degrees Celsius until fully melted.
  2. Heat the water phase separately to the same temperature.
  3. Pour the water phase into the oil phase slowly, stirring constantly, and blend with a stick blender in short bursts until the mixture thickens and turns opaque.
  4. Stir while cooling, add preservative and any fragrance at the maker's recommended temperature, and pour into sterilized jars.
  5. Label with date and ingredients, and keep the cream cool; discard any cream that smells off or separates badly.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Creams with a water phase need a proper preservative and clean equipment, or they grow microbes; never sell or share unpreserved cream, and patch-test new creams on a small area of skin.

Yield

A 200 g batch is 30 g wax, 50 g oil, and 120 g water phase; see the demo.

Variations

  • A rich night cream with more wax and oil.
  • A light day lotion with more water.
  • A hand cream with extra butter for dry hands.
  • An unscented cream for sensitive skin.

Wood Polish and Leather Conditioner compounded · household · intermediate

Beeswax wood polish and leather conditioner are made by melting beeswax into a plant oil, sometimes with a little solvent-free thinner such as citrus oil, and cooling the mixture into a soft paste. Rubbed into wood, the paste feeds the surface and leaves a soft shine; rubbed into leather, it conditions the fibers and adds water resistance. The wax layer is thin and breathable, unlike a hard varnish, so it suits cutting boards, furniture, and leather goods that are handled often.

Materials

  • Beeswax
  • Food-safe plant oil (for boards) or leather-safe oil (for leather)
  • Citrus oil or turpentine substitute (optional, for workability)
  • Double boiler
  • Tins or jars
  • Soft cloths

Method

  1. Melt one part beeswax with three to four parts oil in a double boiler, stirring until clear.
  2. Remove from the heat, stir in a small amount of citrus oil if using it, and pour into tins.
  3. Let the paste cool until firm, then rub a thin layer onto clean, dry wood or leather with a soft cloth.
  4. Let it soak for twenty minutes, buff off the excess, and repeat for a deeper finish.
  5. Reapply when the surface looks dry or dull, usually every few months for furniture and boards.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Polish for food-contact boards uses food-safe ingredients only. Keep oil-soaked cloths flat to dry, never bunched up, since some oils can heat as they dry. Test polish on a hidden spot of leather first.

Yield

A 240 g batch covers about 12 square metres; see the demo.

Variations

  • Paste polish for furniture.
  • Liquid polish with more oil for quick wiping.
  • Board butter for cutting boards and wooden spoons.
  • Leather balm with a softer, oil-rich mix.

Encaustic Painting compounded · art · advanced

Encaustic painting is painting with hot, pigmented beeswax. The artist melts beeswax blended with a little tree resin, mixes in dry pigment, and paints the molten medium onto a rigid panel, fusing each layer with heat so it bonds to the layer below. The finished painting has a luminous, slightly translucent surface that can be carved, scraped, and polished, and it is one of the oldest painting methods still in use, with examples surviving from ancient Egypt.

Materials

  • Beeswax
  • Damar resin
  • Dry pigments (artist grade)
  • Rigid panels (wood, primed)
  • Heated palette and brushes kept for encaustic
  • Heat gun or torch for fusing
  • Ventilation and a respirator for pigment work

Method

  1. Melt eight parts beeswax with two parts damar resin to make encaustic medium, and keep it at about 90 degrees Celsius on a heated palette.
  2. Mix dry pigment into small amounts of medium to make colored paints, keeping each color in its own heated tin.
  3. Paint onto a rigid panel in thin layers, fusing each layer with a heat gun until the surface just glistens, so the layers bond.
  4. Build up texture with palette knives, carve lines with tools while the wax is warm, and polish the cooled surface with a soft cloth.
  5. Work in a ventilated room and never overheat the wax, which smokes above about 120 degrees Celsius.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Encaustic work needs ventilation, since overheated wax and some pigments give off fumes; never heat wax above its smoking point, and keep the heat gun moving so no spot scorches.

Yield

One 30 by 40 cm panel at 1 mm thickness needs about 115 g of medium; see the demo.

Variations

  • Thin translucent glazes over a drawing.
  • Thick impasto layers carved with tools.
  • Photo transfer sealed under clear medium.
  • Mixed media with collage sealed in wax.

Lost-Wax Casting compounded · craft · advanced

Lost-wax casting is a way to turn a wax model into a metal object. The maker shapes the object in beeswax, covers it in a heat-proof mold material, melts the wax out, and pours molten metal into the empty mold. Jewelers and sculptors use it because wax can be carved, filed, and smoothed far more easily than metal, and every detail of the wax survives in the finished piece. Beeswax is often blended with a little resin or microcrystalline wax for carving firmness.

Materials

  • Beeswax or beeswax carving blend
  • Carving tools, files, and sandpaper
  • Investment (plaster-silica mold material) or sand mold
  • Sprue wax and a flask
  • Kiln for burnout
  • Metal for casting
  • Safety gear: face shield, gloves, apron

Method

  1. Carve or model the object in wax, slightly larger than the finished size to allow for metal shrinkage.
  2. Attach a wax sprue to carry metal in and air out, and fix the model to a sprue base.
  3. Invest the model in mold material, let it set, and burn out the wax in a kiln following the investment maker's schedule.
  4. Pour the molten metal, let it cool, break away the investment, and cut off the sprue.
  5. File, sand, and polish the casting to its finished surface.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Molten metal work needs full protective gear, a kiln area clear of flammables, and training before a first pour; never pour metal into a damp mold, which can spit violently.

Yield

A 2.5 cubic cm model weighs about 2.4 g in wax; see the demo.

Variations

  • Jewelry rings, pendants, and brooches.
  • Small sculpture and figurines.
  • Dental and precision parts in the wider craft tradition.
  • Reproductions of carved originals.

Batik Resist Dyeing compounded · textile art · intermediate

Batik is a resist-dyeing method in which hot beeswax is drawn onto cloth to protect parts of the fabric from dye. The cloth is dyed, the wax is removed with heat and washing, and the waxed areas keep their original color, so layered waxing and dyeing builds up patterns in several colors. Beeswax cracks when bent, and dye seeps into the cracks to make the fine crackle lines that are a signature of batik.

Materials

  • Beeswax, often blended with paraffin for easier cracking (labeled as a blend)
  • Cotton or silk cloth, washed
  • Tjanting tool or brushes for applying wax
  • Fabric dyes
  • Double boiler
  • Iron and newspaper for wax removal
  • Rubber gloves

Method

  1. Wash and dry the cloth, then draw the design lightly in pencil.
  2. Melt the wax blend and draw or paint it onto every area that should stay the current color.
  3. Dye the cloth in the lightest color first, rinse, and dry it.
  4. Wax over the areas that should stay that color, dye in the next darker color, and repeat for each layer.
  5. Remove the wax by ironing the cloth between sheets of newspaper, then wash the cloth in hot soapy water.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Dye work needs gloves and ventilation, and dye baths are never poured into food pots or sinks used for food preparation without thorough cleaning.

Yield

Three metres of cloth 1.1 m wide needs about 198 g of resist wax; see the demo.

Variations

  • Single-color resist with crackle lines.
  • Multi-layer batik in three or more colors.
  • Batik on silk scarves.
  • Batik wall hangings with wax-drawn outlines.

Sealing Wax compounded · sealing · beginner

Sealing wax is used to close jars, bottles, letters, and cheese wheels with a tamper-evident seal. Traditional sealing wax is beeswax blended with resin for hardness and color, melted and dripped or pressed onto the closure, sometimes stamped with a seal while still soft. On jars and bottles the wax keeps air out and shows at a glance whether the container has been opened; on letters it is a decorative closure and a mark of the sender.

Materials

  • Beeswax
  • Tree resin for hardness
  • Food-grade color or mica (optional)
  • Seal stamp
  • Spoon or melting ladle
  • Jars, bottles, or envelopes

Method

  1. Melt beeswax with resin in a ratio of about two parts wax to one part resin until smooth.
  2. For jars and bottles, dip the closed lid or cork briefly, or spoon melted wax over the closure and smooth it.
  3. For letters, drip a small pool of wax onto the flap, press the seal stamp firmly into it, and hold still until the wax sets.
  4. For cheese aging seals, coat the cut face or plug with wax as in the cheese coating method.
  5. Cool fully before handling or stacking, since warm seals dent easily.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Sealing wax for food jars uses food-grade ingredients only, and sealing wax is never used to hide a failed jar seal: jars must seal properly on their own first.

Yield

One 100 g stick makes 40 letter seals; see the demo.

Variations

  • Jar and bottle seals for preserves.
  • Letter seals with a personal stamp.
  • Cheese aging seals on cut wheels.
  • Decorative seals on gift wrapping.

Thread and Bookbinding Wax compounded · bookbinding · beginner

Bookbinders and hand sewers run thread through beeswax before stitching, which smooths the fibers, stops the thread from tangling, and strengthens the stitch. A cake of wax is drawn along the thread, the thread is pulled through a cloth to even the coating, and the waxed thread sews more cleanly through signatures and leather. The same wax seals thread ends and conditions the cords used in bookbinding.

Materials

  • Pure beeswax cake
  • Linen or cotton bookbinding thread
  • Soft cloth for smoothing
  • Needles and awl
  • Bookbinding press or boards

Method

  1. Cut the thread to a working length, no longer than an arm's span, to avoid tangling.
  2. Draw the thread firmly across the beeswax cake two or three times, coating it evenly.
  3. Pull the waxed thread through a folded soft cloth to remove excess wax and press the coating into the fibers.
  4. Thread the needle and sew the signatures, keeping the tension even so the waxed thread does not cut the paper.
  5. Seal the final knot with a touch of wax rubbed warm between the fingers.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Bookbinding wax is craft wax, not food wax, and the two are kept apart. Keep awls and needles sheathed when not in use.

Yield

One 50 g cake waxes 125 m of thread; see the demo.

Variations

  • Waxed thread for hand sewing.
  • Saddle-stitched journals.
  • Coptic bindings with exposed spine stitching.
  • Leather cord conditioning.

Furniture and Ski Wax Blends complex · household · intermediate

Furniture and ski waxes are blends, and their labels must say what is actually in them. A furniture wax may be mostly beeswax with a little carnauba wax for hardness and plant oil for workability; a ski wax may blend beeswax with paraffin for glide. A blend that contains paraffin or tallow is never presented as pure beeswax, and the composition is stated on the tin. The beeswax in a blend still does real work: it adds body, water resistance, and a soft shine.

Materials

  • Beeswax
  • Carnauba wax (for furniture blends)
  • Paraffin (for ski blends only, labeled as such)
  • Plant oil
  • Double boiler
  • Tins
  • Cloths and ski scraper

Method

  1. For furniture wax, melt six parts beeswax with two and a half parts carnauba and one and a half parts oil until clear.
  2. For ski wax, melt seven parts beeswax with three parts paraffin, following the ski maker's guidance for the snow temperature.
  3. Pour into tins and cool until firm.
  4. For furniture, rub on thinly with a cloth, let it haze, and buff to a shine.
  5. For skis, rub or iron on thinly, let it cool, and scrape off the excess before skiing.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Never label a paraffin or tallow blend as beeswax, and never use a ski blend containing paraffin on food-contact surfaces. Keep waxing cloths flat to dry.

Yield

A 300 g furniture batch is 180 g beeswax, 75 g carnauba, and 45 g oil; see the demo.

Variations

  • Hard furniture paste with carnauba.
  • Soft furniture cream with more oil.
  • Warm-weather ski wax with more beeswax.
  • Cold-weather ski wax with more paraffin, labeled as such.

Beeswax Modeling and Sculpture complex · art · intermediate

Beeswax modeling is sculpture in wax: the maker warms the wax until it is soft, shapes it by hand or with tools, and lets it harden into a finished model. Wax models are used as studies for bronze casting, as display pieces, and as teaching models, since wax takes fine detail and can be reworked endlessly by warming it again. Modeling wax is often beeswax blended with a little resin or clay for firmness at room temperature.

Materials

  • Beeswax or modeling-wax blend
  • Modeling tools and palette knives
  • Armature wire and foil for large pieces
  • Warm water bath or heat lamp for softening
  • Smooth work surface

Method

  1. Warm the wax until it is soft but not liquid, working small pieces in the hands and larger pieces over a warm surface.
  2. Build large forms over a wire and foil armature so the wax shell stays thin and light.
  3. Model the form with tools, adding detail while the wax is warm and smoothing joins with a warm tool.
  4. Let the piece cool fully, then refine the surface with scrapers and a soft cloth.
  5. Keep finished models out of heat and sunlight, since wax softens and slumps in warmth.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Display wax models are never food, and modeling wax is kept apart from food-grade wax. Keep models out of hot cars and sunny windows.

Yield

A sphere 5 cm in radius plus a 10 by 6 by 4 cm block use about 731 g of wax; see the demo.

Variations

  • Small figurines and studies.
  • Teaching models of anatomy or nature.
  • Maquettes for bronze casting.
  • Display models for shops and museums.

Beeswax in Soap and Cosmetics Base complex · cosmetics · intermediate

Beeswax in soap and cosmetics acts as a hardener and a texture base: a small amount of wax in a soap recipe makes a harder, longer-lasting bar, and in creams and balms it gives body and staying power. Soap makers add beeswax at about five percent of the oils, melting it with the other oils before adding the lye solution, and they recalculate the recipe whenever the wax share changes, since wax changes how much lye the batch needs. The wax angle is what this record covers; full soap method is covered by the soap-making databases it links to.

Materials

  • Beeswax (cosmetic grade)
  • Soap oils (olive, coconut, palm-free alternatives as preferred)
  • Lye and distilled water (for soap)
  • Soap mold
  • Stick blender
  • Scale accurate to 1 g

Method

  1. Formulate the recipe with beeswax at about five percent of the total oils, and run it through a soap calculator to set the lye amount.
  2. Melt the beeswax with the other oils until fully liquid.
  3. Add the lye solution carefully, following standard cold-process safety, and blend to trace.
  4. Pour into a mold, insulate, and let the soap set for a day before unmolding and cutting.
  5. Cure the bars for four to six weeks before use.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Lye is caustic: wear gloves and eye protection, mix lye into water (never water into lye), and work in a ventilated room. Soap with lye needs a full cure before use, and any soap recipe change needs a fresh soap-calculator check.

Yield

A 1,000 g oil batch with five percent wax makes 10 bars of 100 g; see the demo.

Variations

  • Hard bars with more wax for long life.
  • Cream soaps with a lower wax share.
  • Shaving soap bases with wax for glide.
  • Cosmetic cream bases with wax as the body.

Beeswax Polish for Tools and Instruments complex · household · advanced

Beeswax polish for tools and instruments protects metal and wood together: a thin coat on a tool handle keeps the wood from drying and cracking, a coat on a plane sole or saw blade reduces friction, and a coat on a wooden instrument body gives a soft, breathable shine that does not dampen the sound the way a thick varnish can. Makers rub the paste on thinly, let it haze, and buff it off, repeating the coat on handles that see hard use.

Materials

  • Beeswax
  • Plant oil or food-safe oil
  • Citrus oil (optional)
  • Tins
  • Fine steel wool (0000 grade, for preparation)
  • Soft cloths

Method

  1. Clean the tool or instrument, remove rust or grime from metal parts, and smooth rough wood with fine steel wool.
  2. Rub a thin coat of beeswax paste onto wood handles and bodies, and a very thin coat onto clean metal surfaces.
  3. Let the coat haze for twenty minutes, then buff with a soft cloth until the surface feels smooth, not sticky.
  4. Reapply on handles every few months, and after any work that strips the finish.
  5. Keep polish off strings, frets, and moving parts where wax would gum up the action.

Safety

Hot wax handling: melt beeswax in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame and never in a pot used for food afterward unless it is food-grade equipment kept for that purpose. Keep a lid nearby to smother any flare, keep water away from hot wax, and keep children and pets out of the work area. Beeswax melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius and can burn skin badly while liquid. Keep polish away from open flames and hot workshop equipment, test on a hidden spot of any valuable instrument first, and keep steel wool away from sparks and heat.

Yield

One 80 g tin polishes 16 tools; see the demo.

Variations

  • Handle polish for chisels and planes.
  • Saw-blade wax for smoother cuts.
  • Instrument-body polish for wooden instruments.
  • Leather tool-roll conditioner.
