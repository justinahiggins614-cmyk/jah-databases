Exercise During Cancer Treatment JAH-HCCE-000001 supported for a specific symptom

Moving your body during cancer treatment means gentle, regular activity that fits what you can do that day, such as short walks, light stretching, or supervised exercise sessions. It is planned around treatment days, rest days, and how tired or sore you feel, and it can be changed up or down without guilt when treatment side effects flare.

What is claimed: Exercise is claimed by some advocates to fight cancer directly, boost the immune system enough to shrink tumours, and replace drug treatment.

Exercise helps many people feel less tired and keep more strength and mobility during treatment. It is not a treatment for the cancer itself, and it works best as part of the care plan your oncology team already has for you, with activities chosen for your type of treatment and your physical limits.

What studies actually show: Trials show the strongest, most consistent benefit is for fatigue, physical function, mood, and sleep during treatment. No randomised trial shows exercise alone shrinking tumours or replacing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, or other conventional treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Exercise is generally low risk when it is adapted to the person, but bone metastases, low blood counts, heart or lung problems, dizziness, neuropathy, and recent surgery all change what is safe. Stop and seek care for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, or new severe bone pain, and clear any new program with the oncology team first.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Mindfulness and Meditation for Distress JAH-HCCE-000002 supported for a specific symptom

Mindfulness means paying gentle attention to the present moment, often through guided breathing, body scans, or short seated practices. Meditation sessions for people in cancer care are usually short, guided, and focused on noticing thoughts and feelings without trying to force them away.

What is claimed: Mindfulness is sometimes claimed to heal cancer by reducing stress hormones, reprogramming the mind, or activating self-healing that removes tumours.

Many people find these practices help them cope with worry, low mood, and the stress of appointments and waiting for results. The practice does not change the cancer, but it can change how heavy distress feels day to day, and it can be done at home, in a chair, or even in a waiting room.

What studies actually show: Studies support mindfulness for distress, anxiety, and mood symptoms in people with cancer. They do not show that mindfulness shrinks tumours, improves survival, or replaces psychological or medical treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Mindfulness is low risk for most people, but intense silent retreats or unguided trauma-focused meditation can worsen anxiety or distress in some people, especially those with post-traumatic stress. Anyone with severe anxiety, depression, or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional care promptly, not rely on meditation alone.

Acupuncture for Nausea and Pain JAH-HCCE-000003 supported for a specific symptom

Acupuncture involves a trained practitioner placing very thin needles at specific points on the body, usually for twenty to thirty minutes, while the person rests. In cancer care it is most often used to try to ease nausea and vomiting after chemotherapy, and sometimes for pain.

What is claimed: Acupuncture is sometimes claimed to unblock energy channels to destroy cancer, rebalance the body so tumours dissolve, and replace chemotherapy.

Some people feel calmer and less nauseated after sessions, and some notice less pain for a short time. Results vary a lot from person to person, and acupuncture is used alongside anti-nausea medicines, not instead of them.

What studies actually show: The best-supported use is easing chemotherapy-related nausea and vomiting as an add-on to standard anti-nausea drugs. Evidence for pain relief is mixed and short-term. There is no credible evidence that acupuncture treats cancer itself.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Acupuncture must be avoided or adapted with low platelets, low white-cell counts, bleeding risk, skin infection, or lymphoedema in the treated limb, and only sterile single-use needles from a licensed practitioner should be used. Tell the practitioner about all medicines, especially blood thinners, and report fever, bleeding, or worsening pain promptly.

Ginger for Chemotherapy Nausea JAH-HCCE-000004 mixed

Ginger is a root used as food and in teas, capsules, and extracts. In cancer care it is most often tried for nausea and vomiting during chemotherapy, usually as an extra measure alongside the anti-nausea medicines the oncology team prescribes.

What is claimed: Ginger is sometimes claimed to be a natural chemotherapy that kills cancer cells, cleanses the body of treatment toxins, and makes anti-nausea drugs unnecessary.

Some people find ginger tea or capsules settle their stomach a little, while others notice no difference at all. Because nausea has many causes, ginger helps some people, some of the time, and it never replaces prescribed anti-nausea treatment.

What studies actually show: Laboratory studies show ginger compounds affect cancer cells in dishes, which is not evidence of benefit in people. Human trials show mixed, modest results for nausea only, and no evidence that ginger treats cancer or replaces anti-nausea medicines.

People on anticoagulants, with bleeding disorders, or close to surgery should discuss ginger supplements with their clinician, and ginger should never replace prescribed anti-nausea medicines.

Sleep Hygiene for Treatment-Related Insomnia JAH-HCCE-000006 supported for a specific symptom

Sleep hygiene means the daily habits that protect sleep: a regular wake time, a dark and cool bedroom, no bright screens late at night, less caffeine late in the day, and a short wind-down routine. During cancer treatment, hospital stays, steroids, pain, and worry often break sleep apart, so these habits need to be rebuilt deliberately.

What is claimed: Sleep hygiene is sometimes claimed to let the immune system destroy cancer overnight, reset the body so treatment becomes unnecessary, and cure insomnia in every case.

Better sleep habits help many people fall asleep faster and wake less often, which leaves more energy for the next day. Sleep hygiene alone does not fix severe insomnia, and it works best combined with other care when sleep problems persist.

What studies actually show: Behavioural sleep care improves sleep symptoms for many people with cancer, especially as part of CBT-I. It does not treat cancer, and sleep hygiene alone is often insufficient for moderate or severe insomnia.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Sleep restriction, a CBT-I technique, can worsen daytime sleepiness and is unsafe for people who drive, operate machinery, or have epilepsy or bipolar disorder unless supervised. Sleep medicines and supplements interact with cancer treatments, so they should never be started without clinician approval.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Psychosocial Support JAH-HCCE-000007 supported for a specific symptom

Psychosocial support means help for the emotional and practical side of cancer: talking with a counsellor, joining a support group, getting help with money or transport worries, and having family support arranged. It recognises that cancer affects feelings, relationships, and daily life, not just the body.

What is claimed: Psychosocial support is sometimes claimed to cure cancer through positive thinking, or to be unnecessary because a strong attitude alone determines outcome.

People who receive good psychosocial support often feel less distressed, less alone, and better able to cope with treatment decisions and side effects. It does not treat the cancer, but it treats the suffering that comes with it, which matters just as much to daily life.

What studies actually show: Support consistently improves distress, mood, and quality of life, and can improve treatment adherence. It does not shrink tumours or replace medical treatment, and no attitude, positive or otherwise, determines whether cancer responds to treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Support groups and counselling are low risk, but unmoderated groups can spread misinformation about unproven treatments, and anyone with severe depression, anxiety, or thoughts of self-harm needs prompt professional care. Peer advice should never replace clinician guidance about treatment.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. High-Dose Vitamin C JAH-HCCE-000008 unproven

High-dose vitamin C means vitamin C given in very large amounts, usually by intravenous infusion in a clinic, far above what food or ordinary vitamin tablets provide. It is promoted as a cancer treatment, and some clinics sell it for that purpose.

What is claimed: High-dose vitamin C is claimed to kill cancer cells selectively, replace chemotherapy, and cure advanced cancer, sometimes on the basis of laboratory studies or individual stories.

Vitamin C is an essential nutrient, and low levels are common in illness, but taking very large amounts has not been shown to treat cancer in people. Small early studies raised hopes, but larger, better studies have not confirmed a benefit for survival or tumour shrinkage.

What studies actually show: Laboratory and animal findings have not translated into proven benefit in people. Randomised evidence does not support vitamin C as a cancer treatment, and no survival or tumour-shrinkage benefit is established.

Evidence grade: unproven.

Safety and interactions: High-dose vitamin C can interact with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, because antioxidants may protect tumour cells as well as healthy cells from treatments that work by damaging cancer cells. It can also cause kidney stones, interfere with some laboratory tests, and is dangerous for people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency or kidney disease. Never take high-dose vitamin C during treatment without oncology approval.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Essiac Tea JAH-HCCE-000009 unproven

Essiac is a herbal tea blend, traditionally made from burdock root, sheep sorrel, slippery elm, and Indian rhubarb, sold as a cancer remedy. It is usually brewed at home and drunk daily, and its sellers often share personal stories of recovery.

What is claimed: Essiac is claimed to cure cancer, detoxify the body, and work where conventional treatment has failed, often through testimonials and historical anecdotes.

Stories are not the same as proof. No reliable human study has shown that Essiac treats cancer, shrinks tumours, or helps people live longer, and the blend has changed over time, so different products contain different ingredients.

What studies actually show: There is no reliable human evidence that Essiac treats cancer or improves survival. Laboratory results are inconsistent and do not establish benefit in people, and testimonials cannot separate the tea's effect from concurrent treatment or natural variation.

Evidence grade: unproven.

Safety and interactions: Essiac herbs can cause diarrhoea, nausea, and allergic reactions, and herbal blends can interact with cancer medicines and with blood clotting. Because products are unstandardised, the dose of active constituents is unknown, and people should never delay or replace conventional treatment with Essiac.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Laetrile (Amygdalin) JAH-HCCE-000010 harmful-interaction risk

Laetrile, also called amygdalin or vitamin B17, is a substance found in apricot kernels and other fruit kernels, sold in tablets or given by injection as a supposed cancer treatment. It is not a vitamin, despite the B17 name, and it is not an approved cancer treatment.

What is claimed: Laetrile is claimed to be a hidden natural vitamin treatment for cancer, suppressed by medicine, that selectively kills cancer cells while leaving healthy cells untouched.

Laetrile does not treat cancer, and it can poison people. The substance can release cyanide in the body, and people have become seriously ill or died after taking it, especially when combined with high-dose vitamin C or eaten as large amounts of apricot kernels.

What studies actually show: Controlled human study found no anti-tumour benefit from laetrile, and the selective-killing theory is not supported by human pharmacology. Laetrile is disproven as a cancer treatment and carries a real poisoning risk.

Evidence grade: harmful-interaction risk.

Safety and interactions: Laetrile can cause cyanide poisoning, which can be fatal, and the risk rises with vitamin C supplements and with eating apricot kernels. Symptoms include headache, dizziness, confusion, fast breathing, and collapse; anyone with these symptoms after laetrile or kernel use needs emergency care immediately. Laetrile must never be used during or instead of conventional treatment.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Alkaline Diet JAH-HCCE-000011 disproven

The alkaline diet claims that eating mostly alkaline foods, such as vegetables and certain fruits, and avoiding acidic foods, such as meat and dairy, makes the body less acidic so cancer cannot grow. Some versions also sell alkaline water, powders, or strict meal plans.

What is claimed: The alkaline diet is claimed to change blood pH, starve cancer of acidity, and cure cancer through food choices alone.

The idea sounds simple, but the body does not work that way. Blood acidity is tightly controlled by the lungs and kidneys, and food does not meaningfully change blood pH in a healthy person, so the diet's central claim fails at its first step.

What studies actually show: The blood-pH claim is disproven by basic physiology, and no human evidence shows an alkaline diet treats cancer or improves survival. Eating more vegetables is generally healthy, but it is not a cancer treatment.

Evidence grade: disproven.

Safety and interactions: Strict alkaline diets can cut protein, calories, and calcium at exactly the time treatment demands adequate nutrition, leading to weight loss, muscle loss, and weakness. Alkaline powders and extreme restrictions can also disturb electrolytes. Any restrictive diet during treatment should be reviewed by the oncology dietitian.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Fasting-Mimicking Diet Around Treatment JAH-HCCE-000012 mixed

A fasting-mimicking diet means eating very little for a few days around chemotherapy, following a planned low-calorie, low-protein pattern, in the hope that healthy cells become protected while cancer cells become more vulnerable. It is different from simply skipping meals, because it is timed around treatment and carefully planned.

What is claimed: Fasting-mimicking diets are claimed to protect healthy cells completely, make chemotherapy far more powerful, starve cancer, and replace standard treatment.

This idea is still being tested. Some small early studies found it feasible and hinted at fewer side effects, but the studies are small, not all patients can do it safely, and it has not been shown to improve survival or shrink tumours better than standard care.

What studies actually show: Animal findings are promising but human evidence is early and mixed, with small trials, feasibility limits, and no proven survival benefit. Fasting around treatment is investigational and should only be considered inside a supervised trial or with explicit oncology approval.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Fasting during cancer treatment can cause dangerous weight loss, muscle loss, low blood sugar, electrolyte problems, and fainting, and it is especially risky for people who are already losing weight, have diabetes, or are frail. Never fast around chemotherapy without direct oncology and dietitian supervision.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Cannabis and CBD for Symptoms JAH-HCCE-000013 mixed

Cannabis and CBD products are used by some people with cancer to try to ease nausea, poor appetite, pain, anxiety, or sleep problems. Cannabis contains THC, which causes a high, while CBD products are usually sold as not causing a high, though their contents vary widely.

What is claimed: Cannabis and CBD are claimed to kill cancer cells, shrink tumours in people, and replace chemotherapy, often by quoting laboratory studies on isolated cells.

Cannabis-based medicines help some people with nausea and appetite, but results are mixed, products differ greatly, and side effects such as dizziness, confusion, and anxiety are common. Cannabis is not a cancer treatment, even though some laboratory studies are often quoted as if they were proof.

What studies actually show: Cannabinoid medicines have a modest, mixed evidence base for nausea and some symptoms in selected patients. Laboratory cell-killing at high concentrations does not prove benefit in people, and no human evidence establishes cannabis or CBD as a cancer treatment.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Cannabis can cause dizziness, confusion, anxiety, paranoia, and impaired driving, and cannabinoids interact with many medicines through liver enzymes, changing levels of chemotherapy and supportive drugs. CBD products are poorly regulated, may contain undeclared THC, and should never replace prescribed anti-nausea or pain medicines without clinician discussion.

Turmeric and Curcumin JAH-HCCE-000014 mixed

Turmeric is a yellow spice used in cooking, and curcumin is its main active compound, sold in capsules and extracts. It is popular as an anti-inflammatory supplement, and some people with cancer take it hoping to reduce inflammation, pain, or treatment side effects.

What is claimed: Turmeric and curcumin are claimed to kill cancer cells, reverse inflammation that causes cancer, and work as a natural chemotherapy without side effects.

Turmeric as food is safe for most people, but curcumin capsules are poorly absorbed, products vary, and human evidence for benefit in cancer is limited and mixed. It is not a cancer treatment, and high-dose supplements can interfere with treatment.

What studies actually show: Laboratory effects at concentrations not reached in people do not prove human benefit. Human trials show mixed, small symptom-level signals at best, and no evidence that curcumin treats cancer.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Curcumin supplements can increase bleeding risk, interact with blood thinners and with chemotherapy drugs, worsen gallbladder problems, and cause stomach upset. Piperine, added to some products to increase absorption, further increases interaction risk. Discuss any curcumin supplement with the oncology team, especially during chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Mistletoe Extract JAH-HCCE-000015 mixed

Mistletoe extract is made from the mistletoe plant and given by injection, usually under the skin, in some European cancer care settings. It is promoted as a treatment that boosts the immune system, improves quality of life, and helps people tolerate chemotherapy better.

Massage Therapy for Pain and Anxiety JAH-HCCE-000016 supported for a specific symptom

Massage therapy in cancer care means gentle, adapted touch from a therapist trained to work with people who have cancer, with lighter pressure, shorter sessions, and avoidance of sensitive or treated areas. It is meant to help the person relax and feel more comfortable, not to treat the disease.

What is claimed: Massage is sometimes claimed to move toxins out of tumours, break up cancer, or spread healing energy that cures disease.

Many people feel less pain and anxiety for a short time after massage, and they often sleep better that night. The benefit is real but temporary, which is why massage is offered as ongoing comfort care rather than a one-time fix.

What studies actually show: Trials support massage for short-term pain and anxiety relief in people with cancer. They do not show that massage affects tumours, spreads or removes cancer, or improves survival, and massage must never be applied forcefully to tumours or fragile bones.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Massage must be adapted or avoided with low platelets, blood clots, bone metastases, open wounds, radiation burns, or lymphoedema in the treated area, and deep pressure is unsafe in these situations. Only therapists trained in oncology massage should treat people during active cancer care.
