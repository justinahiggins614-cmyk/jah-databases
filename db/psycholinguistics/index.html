Language Comprehension JAH-PSYL-000001 · compounded

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

Language comprehension is the mental process by which a listener or reader turns a stream of sounds or written marks into meaning. It runs from the earliest acoustic or visual analysis, through recognising words, to building a sentence structure and finally integrating the message with what the listener already knows about the world. Comprehension is fast and largely automatic: ordinary listeners understand connected speech at roughly 150 words per minute and can follow much faster rates, yet they usually cannot report the exact words they heard moments earlier, only the meaning they took from them.

History

The scientific study of comprehension began with the behaviourist work of the 1950s and the psycholinguistic revolution of the 1960s, when Chomsky's generative grammar and Miller's information-processing work made the mind's sentence processing a legitimate object of experiment. Reaction-time studies, eye-tracking and later brain imaging turned comprehension into a measurable process.

Description

Language comprehension is the mental process by which a listener or reader turns a stream of sounds or written marks into meaning. It runs from the earliest acoustic or visual analysis, through recognising words, to building a sentence structure and finally integrating the message with what the listener already knows about the world.

Comprehension is fast and largely automatic: ordinary listeners understand connected speech at roughly 150 words per minute and can follow much faster rates, yet they usually cannot report the exact words they heard moments earlier, only the meaning they took from them.

The Problem

How does a listener go from a continuous stream of speech sounds to a sentence meaning in a fraction of a second, and what information is used at each step?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: segment the sound stream. Speech has no reliable pauses between words, so the listener uses phonetic cues, stress and knowledge of the language's sound patterns to carve the stream into words. Step 2: access word meanings. Each recognised word activates its meaning and its grammatical properties in the mental lexicon. Step 3: build structure. The parser assigns a syntactic structure to the words as they arrive, word by word, rather than waiting for the sentence to end. Step 4: integrate meaning. The sentence meaning is combined with discourse context and world knowledge to form a mental model of what is being described. Step 5: monitor and repair. When later words contradict the first parse, the listener re-analyses, which is why garden-path sentences feel briefly confusing.

The Answer

Listeners build meaning incrementally, using sound, word, grammar and world knowledge together, and they revise their first interpretation when new words demand it. Comprehension is therefore a constructive process, not a passive recording of words.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

The model assumes a typical adult listener with normal hearing and a familiar language. It does not describe comprehension in noise, in an unfamiliar accent, or in a second language, where extra stages and costs appear, and it does not settle whether grammar and meaning are processed by fully separate modules.

Math Proof

Let R be the rate of speech in words per minute and C the comprehension accuracy. For ordinary connected speech, R ≈ 150 wpm with C near ceiling; as R rises toward 300 wpm, C falls measurably. Fixation duration F on a word increases with its surprisal S: F ≈ 200 + k·S milliseconds, where k is a positive constant.

Science Proof

Eye-tracking shows readers fixate on a word for about 200-250 milliseconds and move on, with longer fixations on rare or unpredictable words, which demonstrates incremental processing. Event-related brain potentials show a distinct N400 response to words that do not fit the sentence meaning, and a P600 response to grammatical surprises, confirming that meaning and grammar are processed on different schedules.

Speech Production JAH-PSYL-000002 · compounded

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

Speech production is the process by which a speaker turns an intention into articulated sound. It runs from deciding what to say, through choosing words and arranging them in a sentence, to planning the sounds and moving the lips, tongue and vocal folds to produce them. Production is remarkably fast and fluent: speakers produce about 120-150 words per minute in conversation, make roughly one detectable error per thousand words, and can begin speaking before they have finished planning the whole sentence.

History

Modern study of production began with analyses of speech errors collected by Meringer and Mayer in the 1890s and revived by Fromkin in the 1970s, and it was systematised by Levelt's 1989 model, which divided production into conceptualisation, formulation and articulation and has guided research ever since.

Description

Speech production is the process by which a speaker turns an intention into articulated sound. It runs from deciding what to say, through choosing words and arranging them in a sentence, to planning the sounds and moving the lips, tongue and vocal folds to produce them.

Production is remarkably fast and fluent: speakers produce about 120-150 words per minute in conversation, make roughly one detectable error per thousand words, and can begin speaking before they have finished planning the whole sentence.

The Problem

How does a speaker get from a thought to a stream of sounds, and how are words chosen and ordered so quickly and with so few errors?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: conceptualisation. The speaker forms a pre-verbal message: what to say and what to leave unsaid. Step 2: formulation. The message is given grammatical form: words are selected from the mental lexicon, their sounds retrieved, and a sentence frame is built. Step 3: articulation. The phonetic plan is executed by the speech muscles. Step 4: self-monitoring. The speaker listens to their own speech and corrects errors, usually within a fraction of a second. Crucially, these stages overlap: a speaker begins articulating the first words while still formulating later ones, which is why fluent speech is possible at all.

The Answer

Speech production is a staged, overlapping pipeline from message to articulation, with built-in self-monitoring. Errors are rare precisely because each stage checks its own output, and most errors are caught and repaired by the speaker.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

The model describes typical adult production in a first language. It does not cover stuttering, aphasic production, or second-language production, where stages can break down or run more slowly, and the exact timing of overlap between stages is still debated.

Math Proof

Let E be errors per 1,000 words. In ordinary conversation E ≈ 1. Let W be words per minute, W ≈ 135. Errors per minute ≈ W·E/1000 ≈ 0.135, i.e. about one error every seven to eight minutes of talking.

Science Proof

Speech-error corpora show that errors respect linguistic structure: sounds exchange with sounds, words with words, and morphemes with morphemes, never at random. Picture-naming experiments show that word selection is affected by how frequent and how recently used a word is, and tip-of-the-tongue states show that a word's meaning can be retrieved while its sound form is temporarily blocked, which supports separate stages for meaning and sound.

First Language Acquisition JAH-PSYL-000003 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

First language acquisition is the process by which children learn the language spoken around them, reaching full conversational ability in a few years without formal teaching. Children move from babbling to first words at about 12 months, to two-word combinations at about 18-24 months, and to complex sentences by about four years. Acquisition is strikingly uniform across languages and cultures: children everywhere pass through the same broad milestones in the same order, even though the languages they learn differ greatly in sound, word order and grammar.

History

Systematic study began with diary studies in the 19th century and became a science with Roger Brown's 1973 study of three children's grammatical development, followed by the nativist programme of Chomsky, who argued that children bring innate grammatical knowledge to the task, and the usage-based accounts that stress learning from input.

Description

First language acquisition is the process by which children learn the language spoken around them, reaching full conversational ability in a few years without formal teaching. Children move from babbling to first words at about 12 months, to two-word combinations at about 18-24 months, and to complex sentences by about four years.

Acquisition is strikingly uniform across languages and cultures: children everywhere pass through the same broad milestones in the same order, even though the languages they learn differ greatly in sound, word order and grammar.

The Problem

How do children learn a whole language in a few years from the speech they hear, with no instruction in grammar and with input that is often incomplete or ungrammatical?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: listen and babble. In the first year infants tune their hearing to the sounds of their language and babble with its rhythms. Step 2: first words. Around 12 months children produce their first recognisable words, usually names for people and objects. Step 3: vocabulary growth. The lexicon grows slowly, then rapidly after about 50 words, in what is called the vocabulary spurt. Step 4: two-word combinations. Around 18-24 months children combine words, showing they grasp word order. Step 5: grammar. Between two and four years children acquire the morphology and sentence structures of their language, including over-regularisations such as 'goed', which show they are applying rules rather than merely imitating.

The Answer

Children acquire language in a fixed sequence of milestones driven by both the input they hear and their own developing capacities, and their errors show rule-governed learning rather than copying.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

Milestone ages are averages with wide variation: healthy children can be months early or late. The account does not settle the nativist versus usage-based debate, and it describes typical development, not language delay or disorder.

Math Proof

Let M1 = 12 months (first words), M2 = 21 months (two-word combinations, midpoint of 18-24), M3 = 48 months (complex sentences). Gaps: M2 − M1 = 9 months; M3 − M2 = 27 months. Vocabulary at 24 months is roughly 300 words; at 48 months roughly 1,500.

Science Proof

Diary and experimental studies across many languages show the same milestone order, and the over-regularisation of irregular forms is strong evidence that children extract rules. The 'poverty of the stimulus' argument notes that children master structures they could not have learned from the examples alone, which supports some innate contribution, though how large it is remains contested.

Code-Switching in the Mind JAH-PSYL-000006 · compounded

Code-switching is the practice of alternating between two languages within a conversation or even within a single sentence, as when a bilingual speaker says 'I went to la tienda to buy milk'. Far from being confusion, it follows rules: switches happen at points where the grammars of the two languages fit together, and speakers use switching to mark emphasis, identity, humour or a change of topic. Psycholinguistic research treats code-switching as a window on bilingual control: it shows how the mind selects a language moment by moment and what it costs to switch.

The study of code-switching grew from sociolinguistic work in the 1970s, notably Gumperz on conversational switching, and Poplack's 1980 equivalence constraint, which proposed that switches occur only where the two grammars align. Aphasia JAH-PSYL-000007 · compounded

Aphasia is a language disorder caused by damage to the language areas of the brain, most often by stroke. It is not a loss of intelligence: people with aphasia usually know what they want to say but cannot find the words, build the sentences, or understand what they hear, depending on where the damage is. The classic distinction is between Broca's aphasia, with halting, effortful speech but relatively preserved understanding, and Wernicke's aphasia, with fluent but empty speech and poor understanding. About one million people in the United States live with aphasia.

History

Aphasia was first described scientifically by Paul Broca in 1861 and Carl Wernicke in 1874, who linked specific language deficits to specific brain areas. Modern imaging has refined the picture, showing that language depends on networks rather than single spots, but the Broca-Wernicke distinction remains clinically useful.

Lexical access is the process of retrieving a word from the mental lexicon — the mind's dictionary — whether to understand it when heard or read, or to produce it when speaking or writing. History

The study of lexical access began with reaction-time experiments in the 1960s and 1970s, including the lexical decision task, in which participants judge whether a string of letters is a real word. Frequency effects and priming effects became the core findings of the field.

Description

Lexical access is the process of retrieving a word from the mental lexicon — the mind's dictionary — whether to understand it when heard or read, or to produce it when speaking or writing. The lexicon is estimated to hold tens of thousands of words for an adult, yet retrieval usually takes only a few hundred milliseconds.

Access is affected by word frequency: common words are recognised faster than rare words, and by context: a word that fits the sentence is recognised faster than one that does not.

The Problem

How does the mind find the right word among tens of thousands in a few hundred milliseconds, and what makes some words easier to find than others?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: activate candidates. When a word is heard or seen, all similar-sounding or similar-looking words are briefly activated. Step 2: select. Frequency, context and recent use raise the activation of the target word, and the most activated candidate wins. Step 3: retrieve properties. The word's meaning, grammar and sound form become available for use. Step 4: measure. In a lexical decision task, frequent words are judged about 100 milliseconds faster than rare words, and a word primed by a related word is judged faster still.

The Answer

Lexical access is a competitive activation process: many candidates are activated at once, and frequency, context and priming decide the winner, which is why common and expected words are found fastest.

Limits

Sentence parsing is the process by which a listener or reader assigns grammatical structure to a sentence as it arrives, deciding which words go together and who did what to whom. Parsing must work on incomplete information, because words arrive one at a time, and it must be fast: ordinary reading assigns structure in a few hundred milliseconds per word. History

Description

Language comprehension in aging is the study of how understanding spoken and written language changes as people grow older. Vocabulary and general knowledge are usually preserved or even improve with age, while processing speed slows: older adults understand sentences well but take longer, and they have more difficulty with complex sentences and with speech in noisy settings.

The pattern shows a split between what is known and how fast it can be used: knowledge grows across the lifespan, while the speed and working-memory capacity that support rapid comprehension decline gradually.

The Problem

How does language comprehension change with age, and which abilities are preserved and which decline?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: test vocabulary. Older adults score as well as or better than younger adults on vocabulary tests, and their knowledge of word meanings is preserved. Step 2: test speed. Older adults are slower to recognise words and to understand sentences, especially complex ones with long dependencies. Step 3: test in noise. Understanding speech in background noise is harder for older adults, partly because of hearing changes and partly because of slower processing. Step 4: test discourse. Older adults follow stories and conversations well and often compensate with knowledge and context, so everyday comprehension remains good.

The Answer

In aging, language knowledge is preserved while processing speed and working-memory support decline, so comprehension stays good for ordinary language but slows and weakens for complex sentences and noisy conditions.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

These are group averages for healthy aging; individuals vary widely, and hearing loss, health and education all matter. This record describes normal aging, not dementia or any disorder, and makes no diagnosis.

Math Proof

Processing speed declines by roughly 20-30 percent between young adulthood and age 70, while vocabulary scores remain stable or rise by a few percent. A sentence that takes a young adult 2 seconds to understand may take an older adult about 2.5 seconds.

Science Proof

Comparisons of younger and older adults consistently show preserved vocabulary with slower processing, and the pattern is replicated across languages. The decline is gradual and variable: healthy aging differs greatly from dementia, in which language itself breaks down.
