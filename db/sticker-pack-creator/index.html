A sticker that tries to say three things says nothing: at sticker size, the reader's eye has nowhere to land, the details blur together, and the one idea that mattered gets lost. A sticker works only when one idea is biggest, one idea is first, and everything else serves it. A creator that asks for the one idea first, makes it the biggest thing on the sticker, and refuses to let anything else grow as large, turns a crowded sticker into a clear one: the reader sees the idea at a glance, understands it in a second, and remembers it afterward. The discipline is simple, and it is the difference between a sticker and a small mess.

Worked example: a sticker for a garden club carries one idea, a single bright sunflower, biggest on the sticker, with the club's name small below it.

History

Proposed solution

Dependencies

A sticker with a ragged shape tears when it is peeled: thin points snap off, narrow gaps rip, and the sticker that looked good on screen arrives in pieces. The maker draws by feel, and the feel is often wrong, because a shape that looks fine on screen can have corners too sharp to cut and gaps too narrow to peel. A creator that checks every shape before export, rounds corners that are too sharp, widens gaps that are too narrow, and shows the cut line plainly, turns a fragile shape into a clean one: the sticker peels neatly, the edges hold, and the maker sees the cut line before printing. The check is automatic, and the fix is shown, not hidden.

Worked example: a star sticker has its sharp points rounded just enough to cut cleanly, the cut line is shown in the preview, and the printed sticker peels neatly.

History

Clean cutting is as old as die-cut labels, where a shape that could not be cut was redrawn before the run. The creator checks before printing, the way a good printer always did.

A field guide index to the twenty-three records in this database: a map of the whole set, ordered simple to compounded to complex, so a reader can find any record by number and by name. This is a design index for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

This database holds twenty-three full records about the sticker pack creator, ordered from simple to compounded to complex, and a reader arriving without a map cannot tell which record to read first, which records belong together, or where the harder designs begin. The field guide index solves that by listing every record by number and by name, in order, with a one-line account of what each record covers and which rung of the ladder it sits on. Without the index, the simple records and the complex records blur together, and a reader who needs the basics may open a complex design first, while a reader who wants the hardest designs may never find them. The index is the map that keeps the ladder readable, and it is the record that turns twenty-three separate designs into one set a reader can walk through from start to finish.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order; Give each record a one-line account of what it covers; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.

Dependencies

The twenty-three records themselves, listed in order; the ladder ranks simple, compounded, and complex; agreement that an index lists, and never replaces, the records it points to.

Feasibility

buildable-now — An index is a list with one-line accounts, written with tools that exist today; only care in listing stands in the way.
