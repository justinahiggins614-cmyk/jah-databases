Two Photos, One Honest Comparison Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: buildable-now Honest before-and-after comparison with matched photos is established practice today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a before-and-after image creator that puts two photos of the same place side by side, with honest labels that say what changed and what did not. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

Two photos shown as before and after are easy to fake: different angles, different light, different crops, and the change looks bigger than it is, or the change is hidden entirely. The viewer cannot tell what really changed, because the two photos were not taken the same way. A creator that asks for two photos of the same place, taken from the same spot, labels each one plainly with its date, and says what changed and what did not, turns two photos into an honest comparison: the viewer sees the same place twice, the change is real, and the labels can be checked. The honesty is the point, and the creator enforces it.

Worked example: a garden is shown in spring and in summer, from the same spot, with dates labeled, and the caption says the beds grew and the fence did not change.

History

Before and after pictures are as old as photography, from garden records kept season by season to buildings photographed before and after repair. The creator keeps the honesty those old records had: same place, same spot, plain labels.

Proposed solution

Ask for two photos of the same place, from the same spot; Label each one plainly with its date; Say what changed, in plain words; Say what did not change, in plain words; Refuse a comparison where the two photos do not match.

Dependencies

Two photos of the same place from the same spot; dates labeled plainly; a change named and an unchanged part named; a refusal for mismatched photos.

Feasibility: research-stage Public research on automatic photo alignment exists and is published, but no settled method for aligning everyday photos reliably, with the alignment shown and judged by a non-expert, exists at the needed scale yet.

A design for a before-and-after image creator that aligns two photos of the same place, so the same window, the same tree, and the same corner sit in the same spot in both pictures. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

Two photos of the same place rarely line up: one was taken a step to the left, one is tilted, one is closer, and the viewer spends their attention on the shift instead of the change. A creator that finds the same points in both photos, such as a window corner or a tree, and moves, tilts, and scales the second photo until those points sit in the same spot, turns two mismatched photos into one aligned comparison: the place stays still, and only the change moves. The alignment is checked before the comparison is shown, so the viewer sees the change, not the shift.

Worked example: two photos of a garden are aligned by the fence corner and the tree, the second photo is tilted and scaled until the points match, and the change in the beds is clear.

History

Aligning two pictures of the same place is an old surveyor's skill, from maps drawn from two viewpoints to photographs overlaid to show change. The creator aligns by shared points, the way a surveyor always did.

Proposed solution

Find the same points in both photos: corners, windows, trees; Move, tilt, and scale the second photo until those points match; Check the alignment before showing the comparison; Show the alignment with the points marked, so it can be judged; Warn when the photos cannot be aligned, and name why.

Dependencies

Shared points found in both photos; moving, tilting, and scaling to match them; an alignment check; a plain warning when alignment fails.

A design for a before-and-after image creator that shows the change with a slider the viewer drags, revealing the after photo over the before photo. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

Two photos shown side by side are hard to compare: the viewer's eye jumps between them, small changes are missed, and the change is never seen in one place. A creator that stacks the two photos and lets the viewer drag a slider across them, revealing the after photo over the before photo, turns comparing into seeing: the viewer drags, the change appears under their finger, and the difference is visible in one place, at one moment. The slider is simple, it works on any screen, and it shows the change better than two separate pictures ever could.

Worked example: a garden slider is dragged from left to right, the summer beds appear over the spring beds, and the change is visible in one place.

History

Sliders that reveal a change are an old idea, from printed cards with a flap that lifted to show the after to windows with a curtain that drew back. The creator puts the flap on a screen, and lets the viewer drag it.

Proposed solution

Stack the two photos, after over before, aligned; Put a slider across them that the viewer can drag; Reveal the after photo where the slider has passed, and the before where it has not; Let the viewer drag back and forth, as often as they like; Keep the labels visible, so the viewer always knows which side is which.

Dependencies

Two aligned photos stacked; a draggable slider; labels kept visible on both sides.

Feasibility

A field guide index to the twenty-three records in this database: a map of the whole set, ordered simple to compounded to complex, so a reader can find any record by number and by name. This is a design index for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

This database holds twenty-three full records about the before-and-after image creator, ordered from simple to compounded to complex, and a reader arriving without a map cannot tell which record to read first, which records belong together, or where the harder designs begin. The field guide index solves that by listing every record by number and by name, in order, with a one-line account of what each record covers and which rung of the ladder it sits on. Without the index, the simple records and the complex records blur together, and a reader who needs the basics may open a complex design first, while a reader who wants the hardest designs may never find them. The index is the map that keeps the ladder readable, and it is the record that turns twenty-three separate designs into one set a reader can walk through from start to finish.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order; Give each record a one-line account of what it covers; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.

Dependencies

The twenty-three records themselves, listed in order; the ladder ranks simple, compounded, and complex; agreement that an index lists, and never replaces, the records it points to.

Feasibility

buildable-now — An index is a list with one-line accounts, written with tools that exist today; only care in listing stands in the way.
