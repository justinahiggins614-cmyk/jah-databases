Density and Flow Fundamentals Rank 2 · simple

Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion never designs to a peak flow; it designs to a safe density with margin, and treats the peak as a warning, not a target. Companion to the public record of the same title: Density is people per square metre, flow is people passing a line per unit time, and speed is distance per unit time, and the fundamental relationship used in crowd models is that flow through a width equals density multiplied by speed multiplied by that width, up to the point where rising density slows speed. At low density people walk near their free speed, about 1.3 metres per second in published pedestrian models; as density rises toward two people per square metre, speed falls; above about four people per square metre, movement becomes difficult and dangerous, and published crowd-safety guidance treats sustained densities in that range as a stop condition, not a design target. These thresholds are model guidance from published crowd science, not measurements from any particular Mecca event, and they vary with age, mobility, luggage, heat and slope. Flow capacity therefore has a peak: widening a corridor helps only until the downstream exit or the next bottleneck limits flow, which is why bottleneck analysis is a separate record in this pair. The demo below computes flow at three densities with falling speed, showing the peak and the fall, which is the central lesson of crowd flow.

Bottleneck Analysis Rank 8 · compounded

Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion lists every route section in order with its capacity, and a route with an unlisted section is treated as unanalysed. Companion to the public record of the same title: A bottleneck is any point where demand exceeds the capacity of the next section, so a queue forms upstream, and bottleneck analysis finds those points before a crowd does. In pilgrimage movements, bottlenecks commonly occur at gates, stairways, escalators, corridor narrowings, ticket and security checks, vehicle-to-foot transfers, and any place where a wide flow meets a narrow exit. Analysis proceeds by listing every section in order, stating its capacity in people per hour from width, speed and density models, and comparing it with the demand arriving from upstream, with the smallest capacity governing the whole route. A bottleneck can also move: clearing one gate can create a queue at the next stairway, so analysis must cover the whole route, not one point. Published crowd-safety guidance treats bottleneck analysis as a design-stage duty, not a post-incident explanation, and this record's demo is a model of that analysis, not a survey of any real route. The demo below lists model section capacities along a route and finds the bottleneck, the section with the smallest capacity.

Emergency Evacuation Modeling Rank 9 · compounded

Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion never quotes an evacuation time without its pre-movement time, because a movement-only time understates the real clearance time. Companion to the public record of the same title: Emergency evacuation modeling estimates how long it takes a crowd to leave a space through available exits, and it is used to test whether a venue, tent district or station can be cleared before a hazard, such as fire or a structural failure, makes escape impossible. The basic model divides the number of people by the total exit flow, where exit flow is exit width multiplied by flow per metre of width, and then adds detection, alarm and pre-movement time, the time before people start moving, which published models show can exceed movement time itself. Evacuation models must also account for people with reduced mobility, for smoke or heat that closes an exit, and for the fact that crowds do not split evenly across exits unless signage and staff direct them. This record's demo is a model, not an evacuation plan for any real place, and a real plan needs surveyed exit widths, real population counts and drills, none of which are claimed here. Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion plans crowd density lower on hotter days, so heat is a planning input that changes the crowd plan, not a footnote to it. Companion to the public record of the same title: Heat and crowding interact: heat slows walking speed, increases rest stops and water needs, raises the risk of heat illness, and makes a dense crowd harder to move, while a dense crowd makes heat worse by trapping warm air, blocking shade and wind, and delaying access to water, shade and medical help. Simulation Methods Rank 12 · complex

Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion requires every simulation to state its seed, its inputs and its validation, and an unvalidated simulation is labeled as a hypothesis. Companion to the public record of the same title: Simulation methods for crowds range from simple flow models, which treat a crowd as a fluid with density and speed, through cellular and agent models, which give each simulated person rules for speed, spacing and destination, to full-scale exercises and drills, which test a plan with real people at reduced scale. Agent models, notably the social-force model in published literature, let researchers test gate widths, schedules and signage before building, and Monte Carlo simulation, which runs a model many times with varied inputs, shows how sensitive a plan is to a late train, a closed exit or a hotter day. Simulation is only as good as its inputs and its validation: a model fitted to one venue does not automatically describe another, and a model that has never been compared with observed counts is a hypothesis, not evidence. Capacity Planning Formulas Rank 14 · complex

Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion sizes every space, route and queue from the same three formulas, so a capacity claim can be checked in three lines. Companion to the public record of the same title: Capacity planning for a crowd space starts from three formulas, all models, that together size a space, a route and a schedule. First, space capacity: area divided by area per person at the chosen safe density, which gives the number of people a space can hold. Second, route capacity: flow per metre of width multiplied by width multiplied by time, which gives the number of people a route can pass in an hour. Third, Little's law for queues: the number of people in a system equals arrival rate multiplied by time in the system, which sizes waiting areas from arrival rates and waiting times. Planning uses safe densities, not maximum densities, and adds margin for heat, reduced mobility and unexpected closures, so a plan that exactly fits its formulas with no margin is treated as fragile. This record's demo applies all three formulas to one model case, so a reader can see how space, route and queue planning join into one capacity plan. Crowd-Safety Glossary Rank 15 · complex

Signature original companion record (not a public record). The Signature companion keeps its glossary as a controlled vocabulary: a new term is added only with its definition, unit and first model. Companion to the public record of the same title: This glossary defines the terms used across the crowd-science pair in plain language, so a reader needs no prior crowd-science vocabulary. Density is people per square metre. Flow is people passing a line per unit time, often per metre of width per second. Speed is metres per second. Bottleneck is the section with the smallest capacity on a route. Level of service is a graded scale from free movement to crush conditions, from published pedestrian literature. Each term is a model term: it describes a quantity in a model of crowd behaviour, and using the term correctly means stating its value, its unit and its model, never quoting a number bare.
