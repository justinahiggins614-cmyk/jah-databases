Admiration: Definition Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Established: this facet is documented in the published emotion-science literature (Ekman, Plutchik, Russell, and the appraisal tradition), and the record reports what that literature states.

Admiration is the feeling of delighted approval and wonder that rises when a person meets excellence in another: a skill, a character, a work, or a deed that seems worthy of praise.

What exactly is admiration, and where does it sit among the positive social emotions? The question matters because admiration is easily confused with its neighbours: it resembles awe, but it does not need vastness; it resembles love, but it does not need attachment; it resembles envy, but it carries no wish to take anything away. The literature treats admiration as a positive emotion directed at the excellence of another person or thing, an emotion that moves the admiring person toward emulation rather than possession. This record sets out the working definition used across the pair, so that every later record, triggers, expression, measurement, can be read against the same sense of the word. Without a fixed definition the facets drift: a trigger that belongs to awe gets filed under admiration, and a confusion that belongs to envy gets filed under admiration instead.

History

The word admiration comes from the Latin admirari, to wonder at, built from ad, toward, and mirari, to wonder. It entered English through Old French in the late fourteenth century, first carrying the sense of wonder or astonishment, and only later settling into its modern sense of delighted approval of excellence. Philosophers kept the older wonder-sense alive: Kant used admiration for the response to the starry heavens and the moral law, and Hume treated admiration as one of the passions that follows the perception of excellence.

This facet in detail

A young violinist hears a master play a passage she has struggled with for months, and the playing is so clean and so musical that she stops practicing and simply listens, smiling, leaning forward. She does not want the master's skill taken away, and she does not feel smaller for hearing it; she wants to practice the passage again, in the way the master played it. That is the shape of admiration as the literature describes it: delight at excellence, paired with a wish to draw nearer to it by emulation. The same shape appears in a reader meeting a fine sentence, a crowd applauding a rescue, and a student watching a teacher solve a hard problem with ease.

How it is described

What the record shows

The literature reports that admiration is reliably described as a pleasant, low-arousal positive emotion, distinct from the high-arousal wonder of awe and from the possessive edge of envy. Studies of admiration report that it predicts emulation: people who report admiration for a skilled model are more likely to practice the skill themselves, and to seek the model's company. The Geneva Emotion Wheel lists admiration as one of its twenty emotion families, and respondents place it among the pleasant, moderately aroused emotions.

Cautions and limits

The record's cautions are plain. Admiration has no single universal facial prototype in the Ekman tradition, so it cannot be read off a face the way a smile can be read as joy. Self-report is the main evidence, and self-report can flatter: people describe themselves as admiring when they are merely approving, or as approving when they are envious. The line between admiration and hero worship is drawn differently by different writers, and the literature does not settle it. Every claim in this pair is therefore stated as reported, with its source, and none is presented as a measurement of any person.

How did the word admiration get its modern sense, and what did it mean before it meant what it means now? The history matters because the older sense, wonder, still colours the modern one: admiration keeps a trace of astonishment in it, and that trace explains why admiration and awe are so often confused. The record follows the term from its Latin root through its first English uses to its modern philosophical and psychological senses, naming the writers who fixed each turn. A term's history does not settle what the emotion is, but it shows which questions the word was built to answer, and it warns the reader where the modern sense is narrower than the old one.

History

Admiration comes from the Latin admirari, to wonder at, from ad, toward, and mirari, to wonder. The word entered English through Old French in the late fourteenth century, when it meant wonder or astonishment at something strange or marvellous. Through the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries the sense narrowed toward favourable wonder: admiration of a person's virtue, of a work of art, of a deed. By the eighteenth century the philosophical literature used it steadily for the response to excellence: Hume's Treatise of Human Nature (1739-1740) treats admiration as a passion raised by the perception of excellence, and Kant's Critique of Practical Reason (1788) gives admiration its most famous sentence, on the starry heavens and the moral law. Darwin took the term into the science of expression in 1872, and the twentieth-century emotion literature kept it as the name for delight at another's excellence.

This facet in detail

In 1788 Kant wrote the sentence that fixed admiration in the philosophical record: two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration, the starry heavens above and the moral law within. The sentence shows both senses of the word at once: wonder at something vast, and approval of something excellent. A reader meeting the sentence today can see the older wonder-sense and the modern approval-sense standing side by side, which is why the history of the term matters for the definition: admiration never quite lost its wonder, and the wonder is what separates it from cool approval.
