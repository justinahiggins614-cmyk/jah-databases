Near-Earth Objects JAH-FPD-000001 · simple

Near-Earth objects (NEOs) are asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them within 1.3 astronomical units of the Sun.

Near-Earth objects (NEOs) are asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them within 1.3 astronomical units of the Sun. More than 36,000 are catalogued, including over 2,400 potentially hazardous asteroids larger than 140 metres. NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office tracks them, and surveys add hundreds each year. No known object poses a significant impact threat in the next century.

The Torino Scale JAH-FPD-000002 · simple

The Torino Scale rates impact hazards from 0 (no hazard) to 10 (certain global catastrophe), combining impact probability and energy.

The Torino Scale rates impact hazards from 0 (no hazard) to 10 (certain global catastrophe), combining impact probability and energy. It exists to communicate risk honestly: most objects start at 1 and fall to 0 as observations improve, and no object has ever stayed above 1 for long. Apophis briefly reached level 4 in 2004 before better tracking removed the risk entirely.

The Palermo Scale JAH-FPD-000003 · advanced

The Palermo Scale compares an impact's risk with the background risk from undiscovered objects of the same size, using a logarithmic scale: 0 means the risk equals background, positive values mean it exceeds it.

The Palermo Scale compares an impact's risk with the background risk from undiscovered objects of the same size, using a logarithmic scale: 0 means the risk equals background, positive values mean it exceeds it. It is the technical scale astronomers use to prioritize follow-up observations, while the Torino Scale is the public one. Both scales exist to keep rare events in proportion.

The DART Mission JAH-FPD-000004 · simple

NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) crashed a spacecraft into the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos in September 2022, shortening its orbit around Didymos by 33 minutes — far more than predicted, because the impact ejected debris that added momentum.

NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) crashed a spacecraft into the asteroid moonlet Dimorphos in September 2022, shortening its orbit around Didymos by 33 minutes — far more than predicted, because the impact ejected debris that added momentum. It was the first demonstration that a kinetic impactor can deflect an asteroid, and it proved the technique works on a real asteroid, not only in simulation.

On 15 February 2013 a 20-metre asteroid exploded over Chelyabinsk, Russia, at about 30 kilometres altitude, releasing about 500 kilotons of energy — thirty times the Hiroshima bomb — and injuring about 1,500 people, mostly from broken glass. The Kinetic Impactor JAH-FPD-000010 · intermediate

A kinetic impactor deflects an asteroid by hitting it with a spacecraft at high speed, changing its velocity by a tiny amount that grows into a large miss distance over years.

A kinetic impactor deflects an asteroid by hitting it with a spacecraft at high speed, changing its velocity by a tiny amount that grows into a large miss distance over years. DART proved it works. The technique needs years of warning, a well-measured orbit, and a spacecraft heavy and fast enough for the target's size — which is why surveys matter more than interceptors.

The Gravity Tractor JAH-FPD-000011 · advanced

A gravity tractor deflects an asteroid by hovering a massive spacecraft nearby for months or years, letting its tiny gravitational pull slowly tug the asteroid off course.

A gravity tractor deflects an asteroid by hovering a massive spacecraft nearby for months or years, letting its tiny gravitational pull slowly tug the asteroid off course. It is slow and needs a heavy spacecraft, but it works on any asteroid regardless of composition or spin, and it cannot accidentally break the target apart. It is the gentle alternative to impact, suited to long warning times.

The Yarkovsky Effect JAH-FPD-000013 · advanced

The Yarkovsky effect is the tiny push sunlight gives an asteroid: the afternoon side radiates heat and recoils, slowly changing the orbit over years.

The Yarkovsky effect is the tiny push sunlight gives an asteroid: the afternoon side radiates heat and recoils, slowly changing the orbit over years. It is small — metres per year — but over decades it can move an asteroid by thousands of kilometres, which matters for precise impact predictions. Measuring it needs years of tracking, and it is now included in orbit models for hazardous asteroids such as Bennu.

Asteroid Composition JAH-FPD-000016 · advanced

Asteroids fall into types by composition: C-type (carbonaceous, dark), S-type (silicaceous, rocky), and M-type (metallic, iron-nickel).

Asteroids fall into types by composition: C-type (carbonaceous, dark), S-type (silicaceous, rocky), and M-type (metallic, iron-nickel). Composition decides how an asteroid behaves — whether it breaks up in the atmosphere, how a kinetic impactor affects it, and whether it could be mined. Missions such as Hayabusa2, OSIRIS-REx and DART measured composition directly, and the DART result depended on Dimorphos being a rubble pile.

Impact Effects Modeling JAH-FPD-000022 · advanced

Impact effects modelling predicts what an impact does: crater size, blast wave, thermal radiation, tsunamis for ocean impacts, and climate effects for the largest events.

Impact effects modelling predicts what an impact does: crater size, blast wave, thermal radiation, tsunamis for ocean impacts, and climate effects for the largest events. The models are calibrated against craters on Earth and the Moon, nuclear test data, and events such as Chelyabinsk and Tunguska. The largest uncertainty is the impactor itself — its size, speed, angle and composition — which is why surveys and characterization come first.
