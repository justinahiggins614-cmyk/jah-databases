Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Established: Plutchik's wheel is a documented model in the published emotion literature; this record describes the model as reported, with no claim about any person's health.

Plutchik's wheel of emotions, the model that arranges eight basic emotions around a circle and builds the rest from their blends.

Plutchik's wheel answers a plain question: if there are only a few basic emotions, where do the rest come from? The model arranges eight basic emotions — joy, trust, fear, surprise, sadness, disgust, anger, anticipation — around a wheel, with opposites facing each other, and builds every other emotion as a blend of two neighbours: joy plus trust makes love, trust plus fear makes submission, and so on through eight primary dyads. The record sets out the model in brief, its key claims, the evidence and reception, the cultural variation, and the related models.

History

Robert Plutchik published the wheel in 1980, in his book Emotion: A Psychoevolutionary Synthesis, as part of a theory that emotions are evolved responses with a purpose: fear protects, anger destroys an obstacle, joy reproduces. The wheel itself came later, in the 1980s, as a teaching diagram: eight petals, each with three intensities — serenity, joy, ecstasy for the joy petal — and the blends between them. It has since become one of the most reproduced diagrams in the emotion literature, taught in classrooms and used in design.

An example

A teacher draws the wheel on the board and asks the class to find love on it. A student points between joy and trust, and the teacher asks what love would be without the trust: joy alone, glad but watchful. Then she asks for the opposite of love — the wheel shows it on the far side, between disgust and anger, where contempt sits. In a minute the class has used the wheel to do what it was built for: to find a feeling by its neighbours and its opposite.

The model in brief

Key claims

The wheel claims that emotions are evolved responses with a purpose; that eight basics cover the ground; that opposites face each other across the wheel; that neighbours blend into primary dyads; and that intensity grows toward the centre of the wheel.

Evidence and reception

The wheel has been widely received as a teaching model, reproduced in classrooms and in design, and used as a framework for naming blends. Critics have questioned the choice of the eight basics and the claim that blends are exact, and the modern literature treats the wheel as a map rather than as a measurement.

Cultural variation

The wheel is taught in many languages, and its eight basics translate more easily than its blends: love as joy plus trust is recognised widely, while the later dyads are less familiar outside the English literature. The record notes where the translation holds and where it does not.

import json
import math
import random

BASICS = {'joy': (0.8, 0.6), 'trust': (0.6, 0.1), 'fear': (-0.6, 0.7), 'surprise': (0.1, 0.8), 'sadness': (-0.7, -0.3), 'disgust': (-0.6, 0.2), 'anger': (-0.7, 0.6), 'anticipation': (0.4, 0.5)}
title = "Plutchik's Wheel of Emotions"
valence = 0.8
arousal = 0.6
seed = 301
trials = 20
choices = 5
claimed_note = "As reported in the literature: Plutchik's wheel arranges eight basic emotions in a circle and builds the rest from blends."

def compute():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pts = [(valence + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05),
            arousal + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05)) for _ in range(trials)]
    mv = sum(p[0] for p in pts) / trials
    ma = sum(p[1] for p in pts) / trials
    mag = math.hypot(mv, ma)
    nearest, dist = None, None
    for name, (bv, ba) in BASICS.items():
        d = math.hypot(mv - bv, ma - ba)
        if dist is None or d < dist:
            nearest, dist = name, d
    quadrant = ("pleasant" if mv >= 0 else "unpleasant") + ", " + ("activated" if ma >= 0 else "calm")

            "distance": round(dist, 3), "quadrant": quadrant, "levels": levels}

result_b = compute()

Ekman's Basic Emotions Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: established Established: Ekman's basic emotions are a documented model in the published emotion literature; this record describes the model as reported, with no claim about any person's health.

Ekman's basic emotions, the model that names a small set of universal emotions, each with a distinct facial expression recognised across cultures.

Ekman's model answers a plain question: are some emotions the same everywhere, in every culture? The model names a small set — happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust, and later contempt — and claims that each has a distinct facial expression, recognised by people who have never met, in cultures with no shared language. The record sets out the model in brief, its key claims, the evidence and reception, the cultural variation, and the related models.

History

Paul Ekman began the work in the 1960s, studying facial expressions in the United States and then in Papua New Guinea, among the Fore people, who had little contact with the outside world. In 1971 he and Wallace Friesen published the finding that the Fore recognised the same expressions as Americans, and the model grew from there: the Facial Action Coding System in 1978, the list of basics, and the claim of universality. The model has been debated ever since, and the debate is part of the record.

An example

A researcher shows a photograph of a face with raised brows, wide eyes, and an open mouth to a person in a remote village who has never seen a photograph before. The person says the face shows surprise — the same answer given by a student in California. The researcher shows a face with a curled lip and narrowed eyes, and the answer is contempt. The model is built from such moments: the same face, the same name, in places with no shared language.

Key claims

Evidence and reception

The model has been widely received as the standard account of universal emotion, taught in textbooks and used in research. Critics, notably Lisa Feldman Barrett, have argued that context and language shape expression more than the model allows, and the debate continues in the literature.

Cultural variation

The model's claim is universality, and the test of it is cross-cultural: the Fore studies are the famous case, and later studies in other cultures have found both agreement and difference. Display rules — when a feeling may be shown — differ widely, and the record notes where the display differs from the feeling.

Russell's Circumplex Model Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established Established: Russell's circumplex model is a documented model in the published emotion literature; this record describes the model as reported, with no claim about any person's health.

Russell's circumplex model, the map that places every feeling on two dimensions — valence, how pleasant it is, and arousal, how active it is.

Russell's model answers a plain question: can every feeling be placed on a single map? The model says yes, with two dimensions: valence, from unpleasant to pleasant, and arousal, from calm to activated. Joy sits high and to the right, sadness low and to the left, and every other feeling finds its place between them. The record sets out the model in brief, its key claims, the evidence and reception, the cultural variation, and the related models.

History

James Russell published the circumplex in 1980, in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, drawing on earlier work by Wundt and by Schlosberg. The model grew from the observation that people describe feelings in much the same way everywhere: pleasant or unpleasant, active or calm. It has since become the most used map in the emotion literature, and the demo in every record of this program runs on it.

An example

A researcher asks a hundred people to rate twenty feeling words on two scales — how pleasant, and how active — and plots the answers. Joy lands high and to the right, sadness low and to the left, and the rest fall into a circle around them: excitement near joy but higher, contentment near joy but lower, anger near fear but to the left. The circle that appears is the circumplex, and the researcher has drawn the map the model describes.
