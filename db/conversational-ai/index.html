ELIZA and the DOCTOR Script JAH-CONV-000002 · simple

Evidence: established The facts are documented in the cited public sources; the demo ledger values are this record's own model figures, labeled as such.

ELIZA, written by Joseph Weizenbaum at MIT in 1966, was the first chatbot. Its best-known script, DOCTOR, played a Rogerian psychotherapist: it reflected the user's words back as questions, so 'I am sad' became 'How long have you been sad?' It worked with pattern matching: keywords triggered transformations, and when no pattern matched it fell back to generic replies such as 'Please go on.' ELIZA had no understanding at all, yet users treated it as if it understood them, sometimes telling it things they would not tell a person. Weizenbaum was disturbed by this, and the tendency of people to attribute understanding to a machine that merely reflects their words is now called the ELIZA effect. ELIZA's method, pattern matching plus canned replies, is still visible in the simplest chatbots today.

Evaluation II: Automatic Metrics JAH-CONV-000015 · simple

Evidence: established The facts are documented in the cited public sources; the demo ledger values are this record's own model figures, labeled as such.

Automatic metrics score a conversational agent without a human in the loop. BLEU, borrowed from translation, counts word overlap with a reference reply, but a good reply can use different words than the reference, so BLEU correlates weakly with human judgement in open conversation. Perplexity measures how surprised the model is by real conversations, which tracks fluency but not sense. SSA, sensibleness and specificity average, is a human metric that automatic approximations try to imitate. The worked test in this record compares predicted and actual SSA scores across five conversations, and the mean squared error measures how well the automatic prediction tracks the human judgement.
