JAH-R20-PCOL-000001 — Collider taxonomy — how colliders are classified

Particle colliders are classified along several independent axes, and every machine in this database is tagged on all of them. First, by colliding species: hadron colliders (proton-proton like the LHC, proton-antiproton like the Tevatron), lepton colliders (electron-positron like LEP, electron-proton like HERA), and heavy-ion colliders (gold-gold at RHIC, lead-lead at the LHC). Second, by geometry: circular rings, which reuse the same magnets lap after lap but lose energy to synchrotron radiation, versus linear accelerators, which avoid that loss but get only one pass. Third, by purpose: discovery machines that push energy higher, and factory machines that collide at lower energy but enormous rates to study one particle precisely.

The central figure of merit is centre-of-mass energy — the energy actually available to make new particles — combined with luminosity, the collision rate per unit area, which determines how much data a machine collects. A discovery machine needs both: energy to reach new physics, luminosity to see it. The synchrotron-radiation problem explains the field's shape: electrons radiate energy as the fourth power of their energy divided by their mass to the fourth power, so circular electron colliders hit a wall — which is why the highest-energy electron machines proposed are linear (ILC, CLIC), while circular proton machines keep growing (FCC).

Every record that follows gives the same fields: location, operating years, circumference or length, colliding species, peak centre-of-mass energy, purpose, headline results, and status. The taxonomy here lets any two colliders be compared apples-to-apples across a century of accelerator history.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000002 — Tevatron — Fermilab's proton-antiproton collider

The Tevatron, at Fermilab near Chicago, was the world's highest-energy collider from 1987 until the LHC surpassed it in 2010. It collided protons with antiprotons at 1.96 TeV centre-of-mass energy in a 6.28-kilometre ring — the first large-scale machine built with superconducting magnets, proving the technology the LHC would later scale up. Its two detectors, CDF and D0, were the workhorses of 1990s particle physics.

The Tevatron's headline achievement was the 1995 discovery of the top quark, the heaviest known elementary particle at about 173 GeV — as heavy as a gold atom — completing the three-generation structure of matter. CDF and D0 announced it jointly after both saw the signal. The machine also made the first precise measurements of the W boson mass that constrained the Higgs boson's mass before it was found, discovered the single top quark, and observed matter-antimatter asymmetries in B mesons.

The Tevatron shut down on 30 September 2011 after 28 years of operation. Its legacy is twofold: the physics (top quark, precision electroweak) and the proof that superconducting-magnet colliders could be built and run reliably — the engineering confidence the LHC needed. Fermilab's program moved on to the intensity frontier: neutrino beams and muon experiments rather than the energy frontier.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000003 — RHIC — the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider

RHIC, at Brookhaven National Laboratory on Long Island, New York, is the world's first and most versatile heavy-ion collider. Its 3.8-kilometre ring collides gold nuclei at up to 200 GeV per nucleon pair — and it can collide many species combinations (gold-gold, copper-copper, uranium-uranium, proton-proton, proton-gold) and even polarized protons, whose spins are aligned, to study the proton's inner structure.

RHIC's headline result, announced in 2005, was the discovery that the quark-gluon plasma is not the thin gas theorists expected but a nearly perfect liquid — the most perfect liquid ever observed, flowing with almost no viscosity. This came from measuring how particles spray out of the collision preferentially in the plane of the impact (elliptic flow), a signature of liquid-like collective behavior in the first microseconds after the Big Bang. RHIC's STAR and PHENIX detectors (now joined by sPHENIX) mapped the plasma's temperature and density.

RHIC also runs the world's only polarized proton-proton collider program, which showed that quarks' spins account for only about 30 percent of the proton's spin — the "proton spin puzzle" that drove a generation of experiments. RHIC continues operating today and will hand its tunnel to the Electron-Ion Collider, the next U.S. flagship, reusing the infrastructure just as the LHC reused LEP's tunnel.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000004 — LEP — the Large Electron-Positron Collider

LEP, which ran at CERN from 1989 to 2000 in the 27-kilometre tunnel the LHC now occupies, was the ultimate precision machine of its era. It collided electrons and positrons at energies up to 209 GeV — first at the Z boson resonance (91 GeV) as a "Z factory" producing 17 million Z bosons, then at higher energies hunting the Higgs and W pairs. Its four detectors (ALEPH, DELPHI, L3, OPAL) measured the Z boson's mass and width with exquisite precision.

LEP's precision program tested the Standard Model at the per-mille level: it pinned the number of light neutrino species to three (from the Z width), measured the W mass, and its electroweak fits predicted the top quark's mass before the Tevatron found it — and later constrained the Higgs mass to be light, pointing the LHC where to look. When LEP closed in 2000, there was a famous last-minute flurry: ALEPH saw hints of a 115 GeV Higgs, not quite significant enough to extend the run, and the tunnel was handed to the LHC.

LEP also set the practical limit for circular electron colliders: at 209 GeV it was losing about 3.5 percent of its beam energy to synchrotron radiation every turn, requiring enormous radio-frequency power just to top up. That wall is why every future high-energy electron-positron collider on the drawing board is either linear or enormously larger in circumference.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000005 — SLC — the Stanford Linear Collider

The SLC at SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory in California was the world's first linear collider, operating from 1988 to 1998. Instead of a ring, it fired electrons and positrons down the 3-kilometre SLAC linac, bent them around two arcs, and collided them once at 92 GeV — the Z boson resonance. Being linear, it suffered no synchrotron-radiation energy loss, proving the linear-collider concept that the ILC and CLIC would later adopt.

The SLC's great innovation was polarization: its electron beam was up to 80 percent polarized, which made its measurements of the Z boson's couplings uniquely sensitive — in some measurements a single SLC result matched the combined precision of all four LEP experiments. Its detector, SLD, used a pioneering CCD vertex detector to tag the short-lived particles containing bottom and charm quarks with unprecedented precision.

Though it produced far fewer Z bosons than LEP (about 600,000 versus 17 million), the SLC's polarized measurements were essential to the world-average electroweak fits. Its legacy is the linear-collider proof of principle: single-pass collisions work, polarization is powerful, and nanometer-scale beams can be made to collide — the techniques every future linear collider inherits.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000006 — HERA — electron-proton collisions at DESY

HERA, at DESY in Hamburg, Germany, was the world's first and only electron-proton collider, running from 1992 to 2007 in a 6.3-kilometre ring. It collided 27.5 GeV electrons with 920 GeV protons for a centre-of-mass energy of 318 GeV — a highly asymmetric collision that acted as a microscope with resolution a thousand times finer than the proton's size, probing the proton's inner structure at the smallest distances ever measured.

HERA's two detectors, H1 and ZEUS, mapped the proton's parton distribution functions — the momentum share carried by quarks and gluons — across an enormous range, including the discovery that the gluon density rises steeply at small momentum fractions. Those maps are essential infrastructure: every LHC prediction, from Higgs production rates to new-physics backgrounds, is computed using parton distributions measured in large part at HERA. Without HERA's maps, the LHC would be flying half-blind.

HERA also polarized its electron beam (a first for a collider) and ran with both electron and positron beams and both proton beam charges, disentangling the proton's quark flavors. Its legacy lives on in every LHC analysis and in the design of the Electron-Ion Collider, which extends HERA's microscope to nuclei.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000007 — B-factories — KEKB, PEP-II, and the matter-antimatter asymmetry

B-factories are colliders optimized not for energy but for rate: they collide electrons and positrons at the Upsilon(4S) resonance (10.58 GeV) to mass-produce B mesons — particles containing bottom quarks — by the hundreds of millions, so that vanishingly rare matter-antimatter asymmetries can be measured. Two ran in the 2000s: KEKB at KEK in Japan with the Belle detector, and PEP-II at SLAC with the BaBar detector.

Their triumph was the 2001 measurement of CP violation in B-meson decays — the large asymmetry predicted by Kobayashi and Maskawa's 1973 theory of quark mixing. Belle and BaBar each observed it independently, confirming the mechanism and earning Kobayashi and Maskawa (with Nambu) the 2008 Nobel Prize. The measurement showed the Standard Model's matter-antimatter asymmetry is real but far too small to explain why the universe contains matter at all — sharpening the mystery rather than solving it.

The program continues: SuperKEKB, KEKB's successor, began colliding in 2018 with the Belle II detector, aiming for 50 times the data of Belle — the world's highest-luminosity collider program. BEPC-II in Beijing runs at lower energies as a charm factory, and VEPP-2000 in Novosibirsk and DAΦNE in Frascati cover the low-energy precision frontier. Factories prove the collider field's second strategy: when energy can't go higher, go brighter.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000008 — Future colliders — FCC, ILC, CLIC, CEPC, muon collider, EIC

The collider field's future is a portfolio of proposed machines, each attacking a different frontier. The Future Circular Collider (FCC), CERN's proposal, would build a 100-kilometre ring near Geneva: first an electron-positron Higgs factory (FCC-ee), then a 100 TeV proton-proton collider (FCC-hh) in the same tunnel — seven times the LHC's energy. China's CEPC proposes a similar 100-kilometre Higgs factory, also with a proton-proton successor (SppC) in mind.

The linear options: the International Linear Collider (ILC), proposed for Japan, would collide electrons and positrons at 250 GeV and upgradable to higher energies over ~20-30 km, using superconducting radio-frequency cavities; CLIC, CERN's compact linear concept, would use a novel two-beam acceleration scheme to reach up to 3 TeV in 50 km. The muon collider is the dark horse: muons are 207 times heavier than electrons, so they radiate far less in rings — a 10 TeV muon collider could fit in a ~10 km ring — but muons decay in microseconds, so the machine must make, cool, and collide them in a flash.

Under construction now is the Electron-Ion Collider (EIC) at Brookhaven, colliding polarized electrons with ions to map the gluon structure of protons and nuclei — HERA's successor with spin. Each proposal is public, costed, and debated openly; the field's next decades depend on which get built. This record tracks proposals as proposals: designs and goals are public facts, construction is not claimed until it happens.

JAH-R20-PCOL-000009 — Collider records table — energies, luminosities, eras

This record is the database's master comparison table: every collider on one scale. Centre-of-mass energies span six orders of magnitude — from DAΦNE's 1.02 GeV phi factory to the LHC's 13.6 TeV, with the proposed FCC-hh at 100 TeV beyond. Luminosities span a similar range: LEP's precision program ran at modest rates, while SuperKEKB aims for 8 x 10^35 cm^-2 s^-1, the brightest beams ever.

The eras read clearly. The 1970s-80s: discovery rings (SPS collider, Tevatron) found the W, Z, and top. The 1990s-2000s: precision machines (LEP, SLC, HERA, B-factories) tested the Standard Model to the per-mille and mapped the proton. The 2010s-2020s: the LHC energy frontier found the Higgs and now hunts beyond. The 2030s-2040s: whichever of the proposed machines gets built defines the next era.

Two derived quantities let any machines be compared: energy per unit cost (colliders have historically delivered roughly ten times the energy per decade per constant budget — the Livingston plot) and integrated luminosity per year (the dataset size that actually determines discovery reach). The Livingston plot — energy versus time on a log scale — shows particle accelerators climbing steadily for nearly a century, and every future proposal is an attempt to keep that line rising.
