Addressable Architectural Lighting Controllers Rank 1 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

An addressable controller gives every luminaire or group its own address on a control bus, so brightness, colour, and scene membership can be set per fixture instead of per circuit.

Public practice uses addressable drivers and controllers from established lighting-control makers, wired on DALI-2 or DMX512-A buses or carried over Art-Net and sACN networks. Each fixture carries an address, a group assignment, and a scene memory, and a central controller sends short addressed commands rather than switching whole circuits on and off.

Commissioning software discovers the fixtures, assigns addresses, and records the as-built map, so a failed driver can be replaced and re-addressed without rewiring the wall. Dimming curves, fade times, and minimum levels are set per fixture, which is what lets one facade hold many different scenes at once.

Light-Control Network Rank 2 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

A light-control network is the wiring, switches, gateways, and addressing plan that lets many controllers act as one system instead of many isolated panels.

Public networks layer a field bus such as DALI-2 or KNX beneath an IP backbone carrying Art-Net or sACN, with gateways translating between the two. Segments are kept short, terminated, and documented, and each segment has a spare capacity allowance so a later fixture can join without re-addressing the whole floor.

Network supervision watches for offline nodes, and a controller that loses its link falls back to its last scene or a stored safe level rather than going dark. This layered shape is ordinary building practice, scaled up with more segments and more gateways.

Scene Controllers Rank 3 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

A scene controller stores named lighting scenes — fixed combinations of levels across many fixtures — and recalls them with one command.

In public practice a scene is a table: fixture, level, colour temperature, and fade time. Scenes are named for their use, such as evening facade, festival, or maintenance, and they are stored in the controller and in the backup file, not only in an operator's memory.

A recall command fades every fixture to its scene value over the scene's fade time, so a plaza can move from daylight mode to evening mode without visible stepping. Scenes are tested after every re-addressing, because a changed address silently breaks any scene that still names the old one.

print("Fade time per recall in seconds: %s" % (fade_seconds,))

Scheduling Controllers Rank 4 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

A scheduling controller changes scenes automatically by clock, calendar, or astronomical event, so routine changes need no operator at the panel.

Public scheduling combines a real-time clock with an astronomical clock: sunrise and sunset are computed for the site's latitude and longitude, and offsets shift scenes a fixed number of minutes before or after those events. Weekly calendars handle market days and holidays, and a holiday override table lets one date replace the normal week without editing the weekly pattern.

Every scheduled change is logged with its time and its scene, so a complaint about a wrong level can be checked against the log rather than argued about. Battery-backed clocks and a network time source keep the schedule honest through power cuts.

Daylight and Night Reference Modes Rank 5 · feasibility: achievable

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

Reference modes are named operating states — daylight reference, dusk reference, night reference — that the control system can be measured against, so 'too bright' and 'too dim' stop being matters of opinion.

A daylight reference mode records the scene the system holds while daylight dominates: facade levels low, interior task levels at their working value, and street lighting off or at its daytime minimum. A night reference mode records the full evening scene, and a dusk reference records the transition between them.

Each mode names its target illuminance at named measurement points, and the controller can be asked at any time which mode it believes it is in and what its targets are. Modes are engineering conveniences, not claims about the city's described radiance; they give testers something fixed to measure.

Adaptive Brightness Controller Rank 6 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

An adaptive brightness controller adjusts output continuously in response to measured conditions, instead of holding one fixed level all night.

Public adaptive control reads daylight sensors, occupancy sensors, and the clock, then trims each zone toward its target: dimming when daylight contributes, raising levels when a plaza fills, and holding a floor level for safety when a sensor fails. The adjustment is bounded — a minimum level, a maximum level, and a maximum rate of change per minute — so the adaptation never flickers or plunges a walkway into darkness. Tuning is done zone by zone, because a facade, a street, and an interior need different targets and different rates even under the same sky.

Working demo program

# Adaptive Brightness Controller
import math
target_lux = 200
measured_lux = 260
dim_step_pct = 5
correction_pct = (measured_lux - target_lux) / target_lux * 100
print("Target in lux: %s" % (target_lux,))
print("Measured in lux: %s" % (measured_lux,))
print("Excess above target in percent: %s" % (round(correction_pct, 1),))
print("Dim steps of 5 percent to correct: %s" % (math.ceil(correction_pct / dim_step_pct),))

Sensor-Loop Light Feedback Rank 7 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

Sensor-loop feedback closes the loop: a light sensor measures what the controlled lights actually produce, and the controller corrects the difference between measured and target.

A public feedback loop places its sensor where it sees the lit surface, not the luminaire itself, and samples on a fixed interval — typically seconds, not minutes — so corrections track real change rather than chasing noise. The loop applies a proportional correction with a dead band: differences smaller than the dead band are ignored, so the system does not hunt around its target. Sensor placement, sampling interval, dead band, and gain are all recorded, because a loop that cannot be described cannot be tuned, and a loop that hunts is worse than a fixed level.

Working demo program

# Sensor-Loop Light Feedback

print("Error in lux: %s" % (error_lux,))
print("Dead band in lux: %s" % (dead_band_lux,))
print("Correction required (error exceeds dead band): %s" % (error_lux > dead_band_lux,))

Brightness Uniformity Controller Rank 8 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

A uniformity controller keeps brightness even across a surface or a street, so no patch glares while its neighbour sits dim.

Public uniformity practice measures illuminance at a grid of points, then computes the ratio of minimum to average, and the ratio of minimum to maximum. A controller trims the brightest fixtures down and lifts the dimmest zones up until the minimum-to-average ratio meets the design target for that surface — a street, a facade, or a hall.

The grid spacing, the measurement height, and the target ratio are part of the record, because a uniformity claim without a grid is untestable. Uniformity is a property of the measured grid, never of a single bright spot.

print("Uniformity minimum-to-average: %s" % (round(min_lux / avg_lux, 3),))

Glare-Aware Dimming Rank 9 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

Glare-aware dimming lowers or redirects light that would dazzle a viewer, trading a little brightness for comfort and safety.

Public glare practice starts from viewer positions: where people stand, walk, and look, and at what height their eyes are. Luminaires whose light reaches those eyes above the design limit are dimmed, shielded, or re-aimed, and the change is verified by re-measuring from the viewer position, not from the luminaire.

This record's estimator is a simplified screening calculation for planning; it is not a certified unified glare rating, and any compliance claim still needs the proper standard calculation and measurement. Dimming for glare is bounded below by the safety minimum for the walkway, so comfort never extinguishes the path.

Control Protocols Rank 10 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Control protocols are the published languages controllers and fixtures use to speak to each other, and choosing among them is a real engineering decision with real trade-offs.

The established public options are DALI-2 for addressed wired control of individual drivers, DMX512-A for fast channel-based control of many fixtures, KNX for building-wide wired control, Art-Net and sACN for carrying DMX universes over IP networks, Zigbee-based wireless mesh for retrofit zones, and 0-10 volt analogue dimming for simple single-zone dimming. DALI-2 suits per-fixture addressing, DMX512-A suits fast scene changes across many channels, and IP transport suits city-scale distribution; no one protocol is best at everything, and large systems normally mix them behind gateways. This record states each option's addressing, speed, and wiring honestly, and it does not present any proprietary or Signature-original protocol as a published standard.

Photometric Verification as a Control Loop Rank 11 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

Photometric verification as a control loop means the controller itself checks its light against the design intent, on a schedule, and reports the deviation instead of waiting for a complaint.

In public practice a verification pass measures illuminance at the design grid with a calibrated meter, compares each point with its design target, and flags any point outside its tolerance band. The controller then trims the offending zones and re-measures, and the pass is logged with its date, meter identity, and calibration date.

A meter past its calibration date does not verify anything, and a verification pass that measures only one point does not verify a grid. This loop is the control-side half of photometric testing; the energy pair carries the full test procedures, and the two cross-link rather than duplicate.

Failover Rank 12 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Failover is the defined behaviour when a controller, a link, or a power feed fails, so a failure degrades the light gracefully instead of extinguishing it.

Public failover practice names, per zone, a fallback scene and a fallback level, stored in the fixture or its local controller so it survives the loss of the central controller. A watchdog timer detects a silent controller, a redundant controller takes over where one is fitted, and a failed power feed hands the zone to its backup feed or its stored level.

Every fallback is tested by actually breaking the link in a test window, because a fallback that has never been triggered is a hope, not a design. Failover records name the trigger, the fallback, and the date of the last live test.

Commissioning Rank 13 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Commissioning is the structured process that proves a lighting control system does what its design says, before it is handed over.

Public commissioning works through a checklist per zone: addresses verified against the as-built map, scenes recalled and measured, schedules triggered and logged, sensors tested by shading and uncovering them, failover triggered by breaking a link, and every result recorded with its date and tester. Defects go on a punch list with an owner and a retest date, and handover happens only when the punch list is empty or its remaining items are formally accepted with reasons. Commissioning is not a ribbon-cutting; it is the evidence file that the system works.

City-Scale Coordination Rank 14 · feasibility: design

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

City-scale coordination is the design for running many district control systems as one city system, with district autonomy preserved and city-wide scenes possible.

The design layers district controllers beneath a city coordinator: districts keep their own scenes, schedules, and fallbacks, and the coordinator issues only city-wide scenes and city-wide overrides, such as a festival mode or an emergency dimming. The coordinator never rewrites a district's local fallback, and a district that loses its city link keeps running on its own schedule.

This is labeled design, not demonstrated, at whole-city scale: district systems are demonstrated, and city-wide coordination of a luminous city at the scale pictured in the design brief is a design target, not an operating city. Bandwidth, latency, and failure-mode budgets for the coordinator link are part of the design record.

Control Record Template Rank 15 · feasibility: achievable

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

A control record template is the fixed set of fields every light-control record carries, so records from different districts can be compared and audited.

The template fields are: component identity, district and zone, control protocol, address range, scene table, schedule table, sensor list with placement, fallback scene and level, target illuminance grid, tolerance band, commissioning date and tester, and last verification date with meter identity. A record missing its fallback or its tolerance band is incomplete, and a record missing its commissioning date is uncommissioned, whatever its other fields say. The template is deliberately boring: its value is that the same questions get asked of every zone, every time.

Control Acceptance Tests Rank 16 · feasibility: achievable

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Control acceptance tests are the pass-or-fail checks a lighting control system must clear before handover, written before the system is tested.

The acceptance set covers address verification, scene recall accuracy, schedule execution, sensor-loop response, uniformity against the design grid, glare-aware dimming from viewer positions, failover by broken link, and verification-loop logging. Each test names its method, its measurement points, its tolerance, and its pass condition in advance, so a tester cannot move the goalposts after seeing the result. A system passes acceptance when every test passes or every failure is formally accepted with a reason and a retest date; a system with an unrecorded failure has not been accepted.
