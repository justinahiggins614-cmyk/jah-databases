Skillets and Frying Pans

JAH-COOKWARE-000001 · ladder rank 1 · public-harvest

The skillet is the most-used pan in the kitchen: shallow sloped sides for easy turning. Cast iron skillets sear beautifully and last generations; keep them seasoned with thin oil layers baked on. Stainless skillets with an aluminum core heat evenly and go from stovetop to oven. Nonstick skillets need only medium heat and no metal utensils. A 10- or 12-inch skillet handles most daily cooking.

Saucepans

JAH-COOKWARE-000002 · ladder rank 2 · public-harvest

A saucepan is a deep, straight-sided pan with a long handle and lid, made for sauces, grains, and reheating. Heavy bottoms prevent scorching; look for encapsulated aluminum or copper cores. Sizes run 1 to 4 quarts — a 2-quart saucepan is the everyday workhorse. Stir frequently when cooking sauces to prevent sticking and burning. Lids trap steam and speed cooking.

Stockpots

JAH-COOKWARE-000003 · ladder rank 3 · public-harvest

A stockpot is a tall, large pot — 8 to 20 quarts — for stocks, soups, pasta, and canning. Tall sides slow evaporation, which is what you want for long-simmered stock. A pasta insert or steamer basket turns a stockpot into a multi-cooker. Bring to a boil, then reduce to the barest simmer for clear stock. Never fill past two-thirds; boiling liquids surge.

Dutch Ovens

JAH-COOKWARE-000004 · ladder rank 4 · public-harvest

A Dutch oven is a heavy lidded pot — usually enameled cast iron — for braising, baking bread, and deep frying. The heavy lid traps moisture, basting food as it cooks. It goes from stovetop to a 450-degree oven without complaint. Enameled interiors need no seasoning and clean easily; avoid metal utensils on the enamel. A 5.5-quart Dutch oven suits most families.

Woks

JAH-COOKWARE-000005 · ladder rank 5 · public-harvest

A wok's round bottom and sloped sides create heat zones: fierce heat at the bottom, gentler heat up the sides. Carbon steel woks season like cast iron and are the traditional choice. Stir-frying needs the wok smoking hot before oil goes in. A flat-bottomed wok works on electric stoves; round bottoms need a wok ring or gas burner. Keep food moving — the technique is in the toss.

Roasting Pans

JAH-COOKWARE-000007 · ladder rank 7 · public-harvest

A roasting pan is a large, shallow, heavy pan with a rack for roasts and turkeys. The rack lifts meat out of its drippings so it roasts instead of steaming. Heavy-gauge pans won't warp at high heat. Deglaze the pan after roasting — those browned bits are the base of great gravy. Choose a pan 2 to 3 inches larger than the roast on each side.

Cookware Materials Guide

JAH-COOKWARE-000008 · ladder rank 8 · public-harvest

Cookware materials trade heat behavior, weight, and care. Stainless steel is durable and nonreactive but a poor heat conductor alone — look for clad layers. Cast iron holds heat superbly and lasts forever but is heavy and reactive with acids until well seasoned. Copper heats fastest and most evenly but needs lining and polishing. Aluminum is light and conductive; hard-anodized aluminum resists scratching. Match the material to the job: sear in cast iron, simmer sauces in clad stainless, fry eggs in nonstick.
