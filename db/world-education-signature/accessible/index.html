What Education Is

Education is the organised passing on of knowledge, skills, and values: from teacher to student, from one generation to the next. It happens in schools, in universities, in workplaces, and at home, and it happens throughout life. Education is both the process — teaching and learning — and the result: the knowledge and the ability that a person gains. It is the way a society preserves what it knows and prepares its young for what is to come.

A child learns to read in about 2 years of schooling: the first year is the letters and sounds, and the second is the words and sentences. A medical student trains for about 6 years: the first years are the sciences, and the later years are the clinical practice. A university degree takes 3 or 4 years: about 120 credits, at 10 hours of study per credit. An apprenticeship takes 2 to 4 years: the apprentice learns by working alongside an expert, and by studying. A language learner needs about 600 hours to reach a basic level in a language like Spanish, and about 2,200 hours for a language like Chinese.

A History of Education

The history of education is the history of how societies pass on what they know: from the parent teaching the child, to the scribe school, to the university, to the mass school, to the online course. It is a history of expansion: from the few to the many, from the elite to the universal, and from the classroom to the world. Education has always been the way a society preserves itself, and it has always been the way it changes.

The timeline: scribe schools, about 2000 BCE; Plato's academy, 387 BCE; the University of Bologna, 1088; Oxford, 1096; Prussian compulsory schooling, 1763; British compulsory schooling, 1870; universal education, 20th century; online learning, 21st century. The span from the scribe schools to universal education is about 4,000 years. The history is a history of expansion: from the few scribes to the many children, from the elite to the universal, and from the classroom to the world.

How People Learn

Learning is the change in a person from experience and study: new knowledge, new skills, and new understanding. It happens by practice, by feedback, by explanation, and by discovery. The science of learning — cognitive science — studies how memory works, how attention works, and how understanding grows. Learning is not the filling of an empty vessel: it is the building of a structure, and the structure is built by the learner.

Ebbinghaus showed that forgetting is exponential: after 20 minutes, about 58 percent is remembered, and after a day, about 33 percent, unless the learning is repeated. Spaced repetition — repeating at increasing intervals — is the most effective method: it moves the knowledge to long-term memory. The working memory holds about 7 items, plus or minus 2, so a lesson should not overload it. Feedback is the most powerful tool: a learner who gets immediate feedback learns faster than one who does not. The zone of proximal development is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with help: the teacher works in the zone, giving just enough help.

Schools and Schooling

A school is an institution for education: a place where teachers and students meet, and where learning is organised. Schooling is the system: the years, the grades, the subjects, and the examinations. The school is the main institution of education, and it is the place where most children spend most of their childhood. The school teaches the curriculum, and it also teaches the social: how to live with others, and how to be a citizen.

A school year is about 180 days in most countries: the students attend for about 6 hours a day, so the year is about 1,080 hours. A primary school has about 6 years, from age 5 to 11, and a secondary school has about 6 years, from age 11 to 17. A class has about 25 students in most countries, and the teacher teaches about 25 hours a week. A school has about 500 students in a primary school, and about 1,000 in a secondary school. The cost of schooling is about 10,000 dollars per student per year in most rich countries.

Teachers and Teaching

Teaching is the work of helping others learn: explaining, demonstrating, questioning, and guiding. A teacher is a person who teaches: they know the subject, they know how to teach it, and they know the students. Teaching is both a science and an art: the science is the knowledge of how people learn, and the art is the judgement of what to do in the moment. Teaching is the most important work in education, and it is the hardest.

A teacher teaches about 25 hours a week, in lessons of about an hour: the lesson has a goal, a plan, and an assessment. A teacher's career is about 30 years, and they teach about 1,000 students in that time. The best teachers explain clearly, question well, and give feedback: the research shows that the teacher is the most important factor in a student's learning, more than the class size or the funding. A teacher's salary is about 50,000 dollars a year in most rich countries, and the training takes 3 or 4 years.

The Curriculum

The curriculum is what is taught: the subjects, the topics, and the order. It is the plan of an education, and it is the most contested thing in education: what should be taught, to whom, and in what order? The curriculum is decided by the state, by the school, and by the teacher, and it is the battleground of education. The curriculum is both the content and the plan.

The English national curriculum has 10 subjects: English, mathematics, science, art, design and technology, geography, history, music, physical education, and computing. A school week has about 25 hours of teaching: mathematics takes about 5 hours, English about 5, and science about 3. The trivium was grammar, logic, and rhetoric; the quadrivium was arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy: together they were the seven liberal arts. A university degree has about 120 credits: the curriculum is the set of courses, and the credits are the measure.

Literacy

Literacy is the ability to read and write: to decode the symbols, to understand the meaning, and to write one's own thoughts. It is the foundation of education: without literacy, no other learning is possible. Literacy is not just the skill: it is the practice, the habit, and the love of reading. A literate person can read a book, write a letter, and learn from the written word.

A child learns to read in about 2 years: the first year is the letters and sounds, and the second is the words and sentences. About 86 percent of the world's adults are literate, and the rate is rising: in the rich countries, it is over 99 percent. The science of reading says that phonics is the most effective method: the child learns the sounds, then the words, then the sentences. A fluent reader reads about 200 words per minute, and a good reader reads about 300. The vocabulary of an adult is about 20,000 words, and it is built by reading.

Numeracy

Numeracy is the ability to use numbers: to count, to calculate, to measure, and to reason with data. It is the mathematical literacy, and it is the foundation of the modern world: without numeracy, no science, no engineering, and no finance is possible. Numeracy is not just the skill: it is the confidence, the habit, and the sense of number. A numerate person can check a bill, understand a graph, and reason about risk.

A child learns to count to 10 in the first year of school, and to 100 in the second: the place value is the key, and it is learned by grouping in tens. The multiplication tables are learned by about age 9: 7 times 8 is 56, and 9 times 6 is 54. A numerate adult can check a bill: a 10 percent tip on 45 dollars is 4.50 dollars, and a 20 percent discount on 80 dollars is 16 dollars off. A graph shows the data: the line goes up, and the trend is clear. The risk of a disease is a number: 1 in 100 is 1 percent, and 1 in 1,000 is 0.1 percent.

Assessment and Examinations

Assessment is the checking of learning: the test, the examination, and the coursework that show what a student knows and can do. It is both the measurement and the feedback: it tells the student how they are doing, and it tells the teacher what to teach next. Examinations are the formal assessments: the timed tests, with the questions, the marks, and the grades. Assessment is the most powerful tool in education, and the most contested.

A school examination has about 100 marks, and it takes about 2 hours: the student answers the questions, and the marks are the measure. The grade boundaries are set after the marking: the A grade is the top 10 percent, and the pass is the top 60 percent. A reliable test has a reliability of about 0.9: the same student, tested twice, gets a similar result. Formative assessment — the feedback during the course — is the most effective: the research shows that it improves learning more than any other single method. The Chinese civil service examination lasted 2,000 years, from the Han dynasty to 1905.

Special Educational Needs

Special educational needs are the needs of learners who need extra help: the child with dyslexia, the child with autism, the child with a hearing loss, and the child who is gifted. Special education is the provision of that help: the extra teaching, the adapted materials, and the support. It is the commitment of education to every child: that no child is left behind, and that every child can learn.

Dyslexia affects about 10 percent of children: they read slowly and with errors, despite normal intelligence, and they need extra help with phonics and with reading. Autism affects about 1 percent of children: they find social interaction hard, and they need support with communication and with routine. A hearing loss affects about 1 in 1,000 children at birth, and more later: they need hearing aids, and they need the teacher to face them. A gifted child needs extension: the work must be harder, or they will be bored. The Warnock Report of 1978 said that about 20 percent of children have special educational needs at some point in their schooling.

Early Childhood Education

Early childhood education is the education of children from birth to age 5: the nursery, the pre-school, and the kindergarten. It is the most important education: the brain develops fastest in the first years, and the learning in those years shapes the rest of life. Early childhood education is play, language, and socialisation: the child learns by playing, by talking, and by being with others.

A child hears about 30 million words by age 3 in a rich family, and about 10 million in a poor family: the gap is 20 million words, and it is the word gap. The brain develops fastest in the first 3 years: 90 percent of the brain's growth happens by age 5. A nursery has about 1 adult for every 8 children, and a pre-school has about 1 for every 13. The Head Start programme in America, started in 1965, showed that early education improves the school results and the adult lives of poor children. A child who attends pre-school is more likely to finish school, and less likely to need special education.

Secondary Education

Secondary education is the education of children from about age 11 to 17: the secondary school, the high school, and the college. It is the education of the adolescent: the subjects deepen, the choices begin, and the examinations decide the future. Secondary education is both the general education — the subjects every student takes — and the specialisation — the subjects the student chooses.

A secondary school has about 1,000 students, from age 11 to 17, and about 100 teachers: the student-teacher ratio is about 10 to 1. The GCSE is taken at age 16, in about 8 subjects: the student needs 5 good passes, including English and mathematics, to go on. The A level is taken at age 18, in about 3 subjects: the student needs 3 good grades to go to university. About 50 percent of young people in Britain go to university, and about 70 percent stay in education or training to age 18. A secondary school week has about 25 hours of teaching, and the student does about 10 hours of homework.

Higher Education

Higher education is the education after school: the university, the college, and the polytechnic. It is the advanced knowledge, the research, and the profession: the student studies a subject in depth, and they learn to think, to research, and to work. Higher education is both the teaching and the research: the university teaches, and it creates new knowledge, and it serves the society around it.

A bachelor's degree takes 3 or 4 years, and it has about 120 credits a year: the student studies about 1,200 hours a year. The tuition fee is about 9,000 pounds a year in England, and the student loan is repaid from earnings above a threshold. About 40 percent of young people in the world go to higher education, and in Britain it is about 50 percent. A university has about 20,000 students, and about 2,000 staff: the student-staff ratio is about 10 to 1. A doctorate takes 3 or 4 years after the master's, and it is a piece of original research.

Vocational Education

Vocational education is the education for work: the training for a trade, a craft, or a profession. It is the apprenticeship, the college course, and the on-the-job training: the student learns by doing, and they learn the skills of the job. Vocational education is the other half of education: the academic education teaches the knowledge, and the vocational education teaches the skill.

An apprenticeship takes 2 to 4 years: the apprentice works 4 days a week, and studies 1 day a week, and they are paid. A technical college course takes 1 or 2 years, full time, and it leads to a certificate or a diploma. About 50 percent of young people in Germany go into vocational education, and the dual system — the apprenticeship plus the college — is the model. The electrician's training takes about 4 years, and the plumber's about 4 years too. A vocational qualification is the gate to the trade: without it, the person cannot practise.

Adult and Lifelong Learning

Adult and lifelong learning is the learning that continues after school: the evening class, the online course, the professional development, and the self-study. It is the recognition that learning does not stop at 18 or 21: it continues throughout life, as the world changes and the person grows. Lifelong learning is the education of the adult, and it is the fastest-growing part of education.

The Open University was founded in 1969, and it has about 200,000 students: it teaches adults at a distance, by the book, the broadcast, and the online course. A MOOC teaches millions: the first MOOCs, in 2011, had 100,000 students each. An adult learns about 10 hours a week, in the evening and at the weekend: the learning is part-time, and it is fitted around work and family. Professional development is about 5 days a year for most professionals: the training, the courses, and the conferences. A person changes career about 5 times in a working life, and each change needs new learning.

Distance and Online Learning

Distance and online learning is education at a distance: the student learns away from the teacher, by the book, the broadcast, and the internet. It is the oldest new form of education: the correspondence course is 150 years old, and the online course is 30. Online learning is the most flexible form: the student learns when and where they want, and the teacher is a screen. It is the form that is growing fastest, and it is the form that reaches the most people.

A MOOC has about 100,000 students, and the completion rate is about 10 percent: about 10,000 students finish. An online course has about 10 hours of video, and about 50 hours of study: the student learns at their own pace. The Open University has about 200,000 students, and it teaches by the book, the broadcast, and the online course. In 2020, the pandemic moved all education online: the schools closed, and the students learned from home, for a year. Blended learning is the mix: the student learns online, and they meet the teacher in person, for the discussion and the practice.

Educational Technology

Educational technology is the use of technology in education: the computer, the internet, the video, and the software that teach, that assess, and that manage learning. It is the newest tool in education, and it is the most powerful: it can teach millions, it can adapt to the student, and it can give immediate feedback. Educational technology is both the tool and the method: the technology changes what can be taught, and how.

A school has about 1 computer for every 5 students, and the students use it for about 5 hours a week. An adaptive tutor gives the next problem at the right difficulty: the student who gets it right gets a harder problem, and the student who gets it wrong gets an easier one, and the learning is faster. A video lesson is watched about 10,000 times, and the student pauses and replays it: the learning is at the student's pace. A learning management system manages the course: the materials, the assignments, and the grades are all online. An AI tutor answers the student's questions in seconds, and it gives feedback immediately.

Education Policy

Education policy is the decisions of the state about education: what should be taught, who should pay, and who should go to school. It is the law, the funding, and the accountability: the state decides the curriculum, it funds the schools, and it holds them to account. Education policy is the most important influence on education, after the teacher, and it is the most contested: the decisions are political, and the stakes are high.

The state spends about 10,000 dollars per student per year in rich countries, and about 5 percent of the national income on education. The league table ranks the schools by their test results: the top school has the best results, and the parents use the table to choose. Inspection visits a school every 3 or 4 years, and it judges the school on a scale from outstanding to inadequate. The policy of expansion in the 1960s doubled the number of university students in a decade. The policy of accountability in the 1980s introduced the tests and the league tables, and the schools changed their teaching to match.

Education Around the World

Education around the world is different in every country: the years, the subjects, the examinations, and the funding are all different. But the pattern is the same: the primary school, the secondary school, and the university, and the goal is the same: the knowledge, the skills, and the values. Education around the world is the comparison: what works, where, and why? It is the learning from others, and it is the global picture.

PISA tests 15-year-olds every 3 years, in reading, mathematics, and science: Singapore tops the ranking, and Finland is near the top. The literacy rate is about 86 percent in the world, and over 99 percent in the rich countries: the rate is rising, and the goal is universal literacy. Compulsory schooling is 12 years in most countries, starting at 5 or 6: the child spends about 12 years in school, and about 1,080 hours a year. Finland starts school at 7, and it has no examinations until 18: the teachers are highly trained, with a master's degree, and the system is trusted. Singapore's students score highest in mathematics: the system is highly selective, and the teaching is intensive.

Inclusion and Equity

Inclusion and equity are the commitments of education to every child: that no child is left behind, and that every child has a fair chance. Inclusion is the teaching of all children together, in the ordinary school. Equity is the giving of more to those who need more: the child from a poor family, the child with a disability, and the child who speaks another language. Inclusion and equity are the values of education, and they are the measure of its justice.

The gap between the rich and the poor is about 2 years of learning by age 16: the child from a rich family is 2 years ahead of the child from a poor family, in the test results. The pupil premium in England gives extra funding for each disadvantaged child: about 1,000 pounds a year, and the school must spend it on the child. The word gap is 20 million words by age 3: the child from a rich family hears 30 million words, and the child from a poor family hears 10 million. Inclusion means that the child with special needs learns with the others: the school adapts, and the child is included.

The Science of Reading

The science of reading is the study of how reading works: the decoding, the fluency, and the comprehension, and how they are taught. It is the science of the brain: the letters, the sounds, and the meaning, and how they connect. The science says that phonics is the most effective method, that fluency is the bridge, and that comprehension is the goal. The science of reading is the foundation of teaching reading, and it is the most studied part of education.

The five pillars of reading are phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension: they are taught in that order, and each builds on the last. A child learns the sounds in the first year, the letters in the first year, and the words in the second: the reading is learned in about 2 years. A fluent reader reads about 200 words per minute, and a good reader reads about 300. The vocabulary of an adult is about 20,000 words, and it is built by reading: the child who reads widely has the largest vocabulary. Comprehension is the goal: the reader understands, infers, and evaluates, and the understanding is the reading.

Classroom Management

Classroom management is the organisation of the class: the routines, the rules, and the relationships that make learning possible. It is the teacher's work of creating the order in which learning happens: the students know what to do, they do it, and the teacher teaches. Classroom management is not the discipline: it is the prevention of the disorder, by the routines and the relationships. It is the foundation of teaching, and it is the first skill a teacher learns.

A lesson has about 60 minutes, and the teacher spends about 5 minutes on the entry routine: the students enter, sit, and start the work. The routines are the procedures: the handing out takes 1 minute with a routine, and 5 minutes without one. Withitness is the teacher's awareness: the teacher who sees everything prevents the disorder before it happens. The rules are few: 3 to 5 rules, stated positively, and enforced consistently. The relationship is the foundation: the student who trusts the teacher learns, and the student who does not, does not.

Education and Society

Education and society are bound together: education preserves society, and it changes it. The school teaches the knowledge, the skills, and the values of the society, and it prepares the young for the society they will live in. But education also changes society: the educated person questions, innovates, and reforms, and the society grows. Education is both the preservation and the change, and it is the institution that does both.

Social mobility in Britain: the child from a poor family is about 2 years behind by age 16, and only about 1 in 4 goes to university, against 1 in 2 from a rich family. The school is the engine of mobility: the child who does well at school rises, and the education is the way up. Citizenship is taught in school: the student learns the rights, the duties, and the participation, and they vote at 18. Cultural capital is the knowledge that a society values: the child who reads widely has it, and the school can give it by teaching the books, the music, and the art. Meritocracy is the ideal: the position decided by merit, not by birth, and the examination is the test.

The Future of Education

The future of education is the question of what education will become: the online course, the artificial intelligence tutor, the personalised learning, and the lifelong learning. It is the future of the school, the teacher, and the student: will the school remain, will the teacher remain, and will the student learn differently? The future is uncertain, but the direction is clear: the technology will change the form, and the human will remain the heart.

A personalised course adapts to the student: the pace is the student's, the content is the student's, and the feedback is immediate. An AI tutor answers the student's questions in seconds, and it gives feedback immediately: the student learns faster, and the teacher guides. A micro-credential is a badge for a skill: the student earns it in weeks, not years, and the employer recognises it. Lifelong learning is the future: the adult learns throughout life, and the career changes 5 times. The school of the future has the technology, the personalisation, and the lifelong learning, and the human is its heart: the teacher, the relationship, and the care.
