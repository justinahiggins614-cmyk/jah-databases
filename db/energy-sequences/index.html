The burning fuel chain runs chemical energy through thermal energy to kinetic energy: fuel reacts and releases heat, the heat boils water to steam, and the steam drives a piston or a turbine. Each step has its own verdict. The chemical-to-thermal step is conditionally stable, because a burn runs away when heat release outruns heat loss. A kitchen kettle on a gas stove runs the same chain at small scale, and a steam locomotive once ran it on rails.

History

Newcomen's engine of 1712 pumped water from mines with this chain. Watt's separate condenser of 1776 made it efficient enough to power factories, and the steam turbine of the 1880s made it the way most electricity is still generated. Carnot in 1824 gave the limit no engine in the chain can beat.

Water falls, spins a turbine, and makes electricity: the steady chain behind every hydroelectric dam.

The waterfall power chain runs gravitational potential energy through kinetic energy to electrical energy: water held high falls and gains speed, the moving water spins a turbine, and the turbine drives a generator. Both steps are stable — the fall is steady while the flow is controlled, and the generator is steady while its load stays within rating — so the whole sequence is stable. The chain follows the water: drought lowers the output and flood raises it, but neither is a runaway, because the gates set the flow.

Behind a dam, a reservoir holds a lake's worth of water high above the river. Gates open, water rushes down the penstock and spins the turbine, and the generator feeds the grid. In a dry summer the operators open the gates less and the output falls; in a wet spring they open them more. The chain has run this way at the same dam for generations.

History
