A Database That Answers Questions It Was Never Asked Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: research-stage Public research on proactive retrieval, question answering, and user modeling exists and is published, but no complete database that reliably answers questions it was never asked exists at the needed scale yet.

A design for a database that answers questions it was never asked: it watches what its users need, prepares answers before they ask, and offers them at the moment of need. This is a design, not a real product.

A database answers only the questions it is asked, and its users often do not know what to ask: they do not know what the database holds, they do not know how to phrase the question, and they do not know the answer exists until someone else asks first. A database that answers questions it was never asked would watch what its users need, prepare answers before they ask, and offer them at the moment of need, so the user learns what they did not know to ask. The problem is the guessing: the database must tell a real need apart from a passing curiosity, because a database that offers answers nobody wants is a database that interrupts, and a database that misses a real need has failed at its one job.

Worked example, Signature design: the same unasked-answer database maps the questions users never think to ask, offers each answer only when the work calls for it, and learns from every dismissal.

History

Answering unasked questions descends from librarians, who offered a reader a book they had not asked for, and from recommendation systems, which learned what people wanted from what they did; the database version needs that offering to happen at the moment of need, without interrupting.

Proposed solution

Build a map of the questions users ask most, and the questions they never think to ask; Prepare the unasked answers as drafts, and show them only when the user's work calls for them; Let the user dismiss an offered answer with one action, and learn from the dismissal; Keep the user's questions private, on the database, and share them nowhere; Report which answers were accepted, so the database learns what its users need.

Dependencies

Published research on proactive retrieval and question answering described in general terms; a way to watch what users need without spying; a standard for measuring whether an offered answer was wanted.

Feasibility

research-stage — Public research on proactive retrieval, question answering, and user modeling exists and is published, but no complete database that reliably answers questions it was never asked exists at the needed scale yet.

Verification path

A simulation of the need budget shows the database offering answers its users accept; a prototype offers one unasked answer and measures acceptance; a full-scale test offers answers across real work and logs acceptances and dismissals.

A Database That Repairs Its Own Corrupted Records Rank 2 · simple

Feasibility: research-stage Public research on error correction, replication, and self-healing storage exists and is published, but no complete database that repairs its own corrupted records with proof exists at the needed scale yet.

A design for a database that repairs its own corrupted records: it finds the damage, rebuilds the record from its copies, and proves the repair is correct. This is a design, not a real product.

A record corrupts quietly: a disk writes a wrong byte, a program saves a half-finished change, or a copy drifts from its original, and nobody notices until the record is needed and found broken. By then the damage may have spread, and the repair may be a guess. A database that repairs its own corrupted records would find the damage early, rebuild the record from its copies, and prove the repair is correct before trusting it. The problem is the proof: a repair the database makes on its own must be proven right, must never destroy a good record, and must be logged so a reader can see what was changed and why.

Worked example, Signature design: the same self-repairing database keeps three copies in three places, checks them on a schedule, and makes every repair reversible, so a wrong repair can be undone.

History

Repairing corrupted records descends from error-correcting codes and from replicated storage, where copies rebuilt what was lost; the database version brings that rebuilding home to every record, with a proof beside each repair.

Proposed solution

Keep three copies of every record, in three places, so any one can rebuild the others; Check each copy against the others on a schedule, and report any difference; Repair in the open: show the damage, the repair, and the proof, in plain words; Make every repair reversible, so a wrong repair can be undone; Count the repairs and the records saved, and report both.

Dependencies

Multiple copies of every record; a way to check copies against each other; a way to prove a repair correct; a logging standard a reader can follow.

Feasibility

Feasibility: breakthrough-dependent The design needs a specific breakthrough that has not happened: writing and reading at molecular density, at a cost and speed a real archive can afford.

A design for a database that stores a billion files in a grain of sand: data written at molecular density, kept in a speck of material, and read back without error. This is a design, not a real product.

A billion files need a warehouse of disks, and the warehouse needs power, cooling, and space, all of which cost more every year the archive grows. A database that stores a billion files in a grain of sand would hold the whole archive in a speck of material, needing no power to keep it and no warehouse to hold it. The problem is the density itself: writing a billion files into a speck needs a medium that holds data at molecular density, a writer that can write it, and a reader that can find any one file without reading the whole speck. Without those, the grain of sand is a metaphor, not a database.

Worked example, Signature design: the same grain-of-sand database writes only its archive to the speck, indexes it by file name, copies each file twice inside it, and reads a sample back each month to prove the archive is whole.

History

Storing a billion files in a grain of sand descends from the history of storage density, where each generation held more in less, from rooms of punch cards to chips that hold libraries; the grain of sand is the next step, and it waits on a medium, not on desire.

Proposed solution

Write only the archive to the speck, and keep daily files on ordinary storage; Index the speck by file name, so any file can be found without reading the whole thing; Copy each file twice inside the speck, so a damaged copy can be rebuilt from the other; Read back a sample of files each month, and report the result; Publish the cost per file written and read, so the owner can judge the trade.

Dependencies

Feasibility

A Database That Forgets on Purpose, Perfectly Rank 4 · simple

Feasibility: research-stage Public research on certified deletion, cryptographic erasure, and data removal exists and is published, but no complete database that forgets perfectly, with proof, exists at the needed scale yet.

A design for a database that forgets on purpose, perfectly: when its owner says forget, the record is gone from every copy, every backup, and every index, and the forgetting can be proven. This is a design, not a real product.

A database remembers everything, and forgetting is hard: a record deleted from one place survives in a backup, a copy, or an index, and its owner cannot prove it is gone. A database that forgets on purpose, perfectly, would remove the record from every copy, every backup, and every index, and prove the forgetting, so its owner can trust that forgotten means forgotten. The problem is the proof: the database must find every copy, including the ones it made for safety, and remove them all, without breaking the records that must stay, and it must prove the removal without keeping a list of what it removed, because that list would itself remember the record.

Worked example, Signature design: the same perfect-forgetting database tags every copy when it is made, removes them in one agreed step, and keeps a record of the forgetting itself, but never of what was forgotten.

History

Forgetting on purpose descends from the right to be forgotten and from the old habit of burning a letter, where the destruction was total and visible; the database version needs that totality across copies, backups, and indexes, with a proof beside it.

Proposed solution

Tag every copy at the moment it is made, so the forgetting can find them all; Remove the copies in a single agreed step, so no copy is left behind; Prove the forgetting with a check that reads the whole database and finds nothing; Keep a record of the forgetting itself: when it happened, and who asked, but not what was forgotten; Let the owner test the forgetting by searching for the record and finding nothing.

Dependencies

Published research on certified deletion and data erasure described in general terms; a way to tag every copy at creation; a way to prove removal without keeping a list; a standard for reporting the proof.

Feasibility

A Database That Proves Every Answer It Gives Rank 5 · simple

Feasibility: theoretical The idea is consistent with known logic and with published work on provenance and verified querying, but no known path exists to proving every answer for arbitrary questions at the needed scale.

A design for a database that proves every answer it gives: each answer carries a proof, built from the records it used, that a reader can check. This is a design, not a real product.

A database answers, and its user must trust the answer: trust that the records were right, trust that the question was understood, and trust that the answer follows from the records. Trust is not proof, and a wrong answer believed is worse than no answer at all. A database that proves every answer it gives would attach to each answer a proof, built from the records it used, that a reader can check step by step, so the answer is not believed but verified. The problem is the proof itself: proving an answer for arbitrary questions, over arbitrary records, is a task no known method can complete at the needed scale, and without that method, the database can only prove the easy answers and ask trust for the rest.

Worked example, Signature design: the same proving database proves in small pieces, keeps each proof short enough to read, and lets an independent checker verify every proof before the answer is trusted.

History

Proving every answer descends from the scientific habit of showing method and result together, so others can repeat the work, and from provenance in databases, where an answer could be traced to its sources; the database version needs that tracing to become a proof for every answer.

Proposed solution

Prove in small pieces: prove each step of the answer, then prove the steps fit; Keep the proof short enough to read, even when the answer is long; Let an independent checker, separate from the database, verify the proof; Mark the answers the database cannot prove, and answer those with a plain statement of uncertainty; Log every proof and every refusal, so the owner can see what was proven.

Dependencies

A proof method for answers over arbitrary records; published research on provenance and verified querying described in general terms; an independent checker; a standard for carrying a proof beside an answer.

Feasibility

theoretical — The idea is consistent with known logic and with published work on provenance and verified querying, but no known path exists to proving every answer for arbitrary questions at the needed scale.

Verification path

A simulation of the proof loop shows a small answer proven and checked; a prototype proves one real answer and refuses one it cannot prove; a full-scale test proves answers across real use and publishes the proof log.

A Database That Serves a Whole Planet at Once Rank 23 · complex

Feasibility: breakthrough-dependent The design needs a specific breakthrough that has not happened: planetary-scale infrastructure that keeps copies in agreement and answers billions at once, at a cost a real archive can afford.

A design for a database that serves a whole planet at once: every person on Earth asking at the same time, and every one of them answered. This is a design, not a real product.

A database serves its users one at a time, and when too many ask at once, it slows, queues, or fails. A database that serves a whole planet at once would answer every person on Earth asking at the same time, with no one waiting and no one turned away. The problem is the scale itself: billions of simultaneous questions need a planetary infrastructure of machines, networks, and power, all working together, and no single database can hold that infrastructure alone. The design must name the infrastructure it needs, the way its copies stay in agreement, and what happens when one part of the planet fails, because a planetary database that fails in one place must not fail everywhere.

Worked example, Signature design: the same planetary database is built as agreeing copies, tests itself by failing one region on purpose, and publishes the answer time for every region.

History

Serving a whole planet at once descends from the history of global networks, where each generation served more people at once, from telegraphs to the internet; the database version needs that serving to cover every person on Earth at the same time, which no single database has done.

Proposed solution

Build the database as many copies that agree, not as one machine that must not fail; Let each copy serve its own region, and share updates with the others; Agree on the records first, and serve the questions second, so no copy answers wrongly; Test the planet by failing one region on purpose, and watching the others carry it; Publish the answer time for every region, so the planet's service is visible.

Dependencies

A planetary infrastructure of machines, networks, and power; published research on planetary-scale systems described in general terms; a way to keep copies in agreement; a standard for measuring answer time across regions.

Feasibility

Verification path

A simulation of the planetary budget shows the database answering a stated number of simultaneous questions; a prototype serves two regions in agreement; a full-scale test serves real users across continents and logs the answer time.
