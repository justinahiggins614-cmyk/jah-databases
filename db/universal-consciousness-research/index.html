The Hard Problem of Consciousness JAH-FUC-000001 · advanced

The hard problem, named by David Chalmers in 1995, asks why physical brain processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all — why seeing red feels like something, rather than nothing.

The hard problem, named by David Chalmers in 1995, asks why physical brain processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all — why seeing red feels like something, rather than nothing. Easy problems explain functions such as discrimination and report; the hard problem asks about experience itself. Whether the hard problem is a real problem, a confusion, or a problem for future science is itself contested among philosophers and scientists.

Global Workspace Theory JAH-FUC-000002 · intermediate

Global workspace theory, proposed by Bernard Baars in 1988, holds that consciousness is information made globally available: specialist brain systems compete for access to a shared workspace, and the winner is broadcast widely.

Global workspace theory, proposed by Bernard Baars in 1988, holds that consciousness is information made globally available: specialist brain systems compete for access to a shared workspace, and the winner is broadcast widely. Stanislas Dehaene's global neuronal workspace version ties this to fronto-parietal activity seen in experiments. The theory predicts that reported stimuli engage broader activity than unreported ones, which experiments confirm — though rivals dispute the interpretation.

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) JAH-FUC-000003 · advanced

Integrated information theory, developed by Giulio Tononi, proposes that consciousness is integrated information, measured by a quantity called Phi: a system is conscious to the extent its parts constrain each other irreducibly.

Integrated information theory, developed by Giulio Tononi, proposes that consciousness is integrated information, measured by a quantity called Phi: a system is conscious to the extent its parts constrain each other irreducibly. IIT predicts that some simple systems are minimally conscious and that the cerebellum contributes little to consciousness. Phi cannot be computed for a real brain, the theory is contested, and in 2023 a large adversarial collaboration found its predictions wanting on key tests — the debate continues.

Higher-Order Thought Theory JAH-FUC-000004 · advanced

Higher-order thought theory holds that a mental state is conscious when it is the target of a higher-order thought — when the mind represents itself as being in that state.

Higher-order thought theory holds that a mental state is conscious when it is the target of a higher-order thought — when the mind represents itself as being in that state. On this view, consciousness is not a property of first-order perception but of meta-representation. The theory explains why metacognition and consciousness track together, and it predicts that creatures without higher-order representation are not conscious, a claim its critics dispute.

Neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) are the minimal brain activity sufficient for a conscious experience.

Neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) are the minimal brain activity sufficient for a conscious experience. Experiments contrast reported and unreported stimuli to find them, and the leading candidates are sustained activity in fronto-parietal networks and recurrent processing in sensory cortex. After decades of work there is no consensus on the full NCC, and rival theories predict different ones — which is why adversarial collaborations now test them head to head.

Philosophers distinguish access consciousness — information available for report and reasoning — from phenomenal consciousness, the experience itself. Ned Block argued they can come apart: a state might be experienced without being reportable. Experiments cannot settle the distinction by report alone, since report is the usual measure, which is why no-report paradigms were developed to study experience without asking for it.

Anesthesia and Consciousness JAH-FUC-000009 · advanced

General anesthesia reversibly abolishes consciousness, and studying how it does so teaches us what consciousness needs.

General anesthesia reversibly abolishes consciousness, and studying how it does so teaches us what consciousness needs. Anesthetics disrupt connectivity between brain regions, especially fronto-parietal communication, and reduce the complexity of brain responses to stimulation. The perturbational complexity index falls during anesthesia and rises on waking, which is why it is studied as a bedside measure of consciousness.

Disorders of consciousness range from coma to the vegetative state to the minimally conscious state, in which patients show inconsistent signs of awareness. Which animals are conscious is an open scientific question, approached through behaviour, brain structure and analogy. The 2012 Cambridge Declaration stated that the neural substrates of consciousness are present in many non-human animals, including mammals, birds and octopuses. In 2024 the New York Declaration extended the case to insects and other invertebrates as a reasonable possibility, while stressing that certainty is not available. The question matters for welfare law and for how animals are treated.

Machine Consciousness: the Open Debate JAH-FUC-000014 · advanced

Whether a machine could be conscious is an open debate, not a settled question.

Whether a machine could be conscious is an open debate, not a settled question. Some argue that the right computation, in the right architecture, would be conscious; others argue that biology, embodiment or causal structure is essential, and that current AI systems — which predict text without a body or a life — show no evidence of experience. No test for machine consciousness exists, and claims either way are philosophical positions, not measurements. This record states the debate; it does not settle it.

Free will and consciousness are linked but distinct: Benjamin Libet's 1980s experiments found brain activity preceding the conscious decision to move by hundreds of milliseconds, which some took as evidence against free will. The philosophical debate — compatibilism, libertarianism, determinism — continues, and neuroscience constrains it without settling it.

Consciousness Measurement: the PCI JAH-FUC-000023 · intermediate

The perturbational complexity index (PCI) measures consciousness by perturbing the brain with a magnetic pulse (TMS) and measuring the complexity of the EEG response.

The perturbational complexity index (PCI) measures consciousness by perturbing the brain with a magnetic pulse (TMS) and measuring the complexity of the EEG response. Wakeful adults score above about 0.31; sleep, anesthesia and unresponsive patients score below it. PCI is the best-validated bedside measure of consciousness, and it is a measure — a number that tracks a state — not a theory of what consciousness is.

Dualism holds that mind and matter are different kinds of stuff; physicalism holds that mind arises from physical processes.
