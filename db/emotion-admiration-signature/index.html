Signature design study: how the Signature edition presents definition of admiration. The design opens with a plain definition, stated before any detail, so a reader who knows nothing about admiration can follow every later record. Design question: How can the definition of admiration be stated plainly enough for a first-time reader, and precisely enough for the later records to build on? The material this design presents: What exactly is admiration, and where does it sit among the positive social emotions? The question matters because admiration is easily confused with its neighbours: it resembles awe, but it does not need vastness; it resembles love, but it does not need attachment; it resembles envy, but it carries no wish to take anything away. The literature treats admiration as a positive emotion directed at the excellence of another person or thing, an emotion that moves the admiring person toward emulation rather than possession. This record sets out the working definition used across the pair, so that every later record, triggers, expression, measurement, can be read against the same sense of the word. Without a fixed definition the facets drift: a trigger that belongs to awe gets filed under admiration, and a confusion that belongs to envy gets filed under admiration instead.

History

The design draws on the history below. The word admiration comes from the Latin admirari, to wonder at, built from ad, toward, and mirari, to wonder. It entered English through Old French in the late fourteenth century, first carrying the sense of wonder or astonishment, and only later settling into its modern sense of delighted approval of excellence. Philosophers kept the older wonder-sense alive: Kant used admiration for the response to the starry heavens and the moral law, and Hume treated admiration as one of the passions that follows the perception of excellence.

This facet in detail

The design's worked example: A young violinist hears a master play a passage she has struggled with for months, and the playing is so clean and so musical that she stops practicing and simply listens, smiling, leaning forward. She does not want the master's skill taken away, and she does not feel smaller for hearing it; she wants to practice the passage again, in the way the master played it. That is the shape of admiration as the literature describes it: delight at excellence, paired with a wish to draw nearer to it by emulation. The same shape appears in a reader meeting a fine sentence, a crowd applauding a rescue, and a student watching a teacher solve a hard problem with ease.

What the record shows

What the design presents as reported: The literature reports that admiration is reliably described as a pleasant, low-arousal positive emotion, distinct from the high-arousal wonder of awe and from the possessive edge of envy. Studies of admiration report that it predicts emulation: people who report admiration for a skilled model are more likely to practice the skill themselves, and to seek the model's company. The Geneva Emotion Wheel lists admiration as one of its twenty emotion families, and respondents place it among the pleasant, moderately aroused emotions.

Cautions and limits

Design limits, stated with the material: The record's cautions are plain. Admiration has no single universal facial prototype in the Ekman tradition, so it cannot be read off a face the way a smile can be read as joy. Self-report is the main evidence, and self-report can flatter: people describe themselves as admiring when they are merely approving, or as approving when they are envious. The line between admiration and hero worship is drawn differently by different writers, and the literature does not settle it. Every claim in this pair is therefore stated as reported, with its source, and none is presented as a measurement of any person.

import json
import math
import random

title = 'Admiration: Definition'
valence = 0.62
arousal = 0.28

def run_once():

    ratings = []
