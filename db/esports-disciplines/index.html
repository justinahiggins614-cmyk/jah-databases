MOBA Games Rank 1 · simple · strategy genre · intermediate

MOBA games, short for multiplayer online battle arena, are team strategy games in which two teams of usually five players control individual heroes or champions, defend their own base, and try to destroy the enemy base. Matches play out on a map with three lanes, a jungle area between them, and defensive towers, and players earn gold and experience by defeating enemy units and opponents, spending gold on items that strengthen their hero. Each hero has a distinct role, such as damage dealer, tank, or support, and team composition and coordination decide matches as much as individual skill. Games typically last twenty to forty minutes, and the genre is one of the most widely played and watched in competitive gaming.

History

MOBA games grew from custom maps for strategy games in the early twenty-first century into standalone titles with global leagues.

Rules

Two teams of five defend bases on a three-lane map. Destroying the enemy base wins; towers, gold, and experience structure play.

Techniques

Last-hitting, map awareness, team fighting, objective control, and role play are the core skills.

Training

Teams drill lane matchups, team fights, draft strategy, and replay review.

TEAM_SIZE = 5
LANES = 3
print("MOBA match example")
print("Team size: %d, lanes: %d" % (TEAM_SIZE, LANES))
print("Players per match: %d" % (TEAM_SIZE * 2))
print("Match length example: 30 minutes")

First-Person Shooters Rank 2 · simple · action genre · intermediate

First-person shooter games place the player behind the eyes of a character, with aiming, movement, and shooting as the core skills, and competitive play usually runs in team formats such as five against five. Modes include bomb defusal, where attackers plant a device and defenders stop them, and control-point or capture modes, and matches are played as series of short rounds in which economy matters: teams earn in-game currency for eliminations and round wins and spend it on weapons and equipment. Aiming precision, crosshair placement, movement, and communication decide rounds, and professional play adds detailed map strategy, utility use, and role specialisation. The genre demands fast reactions, steady aim under pressure, and constant information sharing with teammates.

History

First-person shooters grew from early 1990s action games into team-based competitive titles with global leagues and tournaments.

Rules

Teams play rounds in modes such as bomb defusal; round wins and eliminations earn currency for weapons and equipment.

Techniques

Aiming, crosshair placement, movement, utility use, and team communication are the core skills.

Training

Teams drill aim routines, map strategy, round economy, and communication protocols.

Working demo program

ROUNDS_TO_WIN = 13
ROUNDS_PLAYED = 20
print("Shooter match example")
print("Rounds to win: %d, rounds played: %d" % (ROUNDS_TO_WIN, ROUNDS_PLAYED))
print("Team A rounds: 12, team B rounds: 8")
print("Rounds remaining to win for team A: %d" % (ROUNDS_TO_WIN - 12))

Real-Time Strategy Rank 3 · simple · strategy genre · advanced

Real-time strategy games ask players to gather resources, build bases and armies, and outmanoeuvre opponents in real time, with no turns and no pauses for thought. A match runs from a small starting base through an economy phase, a technology phase, and army clashes, and the player who manages resources, scouting, and production queues best usually wins. The genre's defining skill is multitasking: a top player issues hundreds of actions per minute, switching between base management, army control, and scouting while planning several moves ahead. Matches are usually one against one, which makes the genre the most individual of team-free competitive games, and its depth rewards study of build orders, counters, and map control.

History

Real-time strategy grew from early 1990s computer strategy games into a major competitive genre with global one-against-one tournaments.

Rules

Players start with a base, gather resources, build armies, and win by destroying the opponent or meeting the match condition.

Techniques

Resource management, build orders, scouting, multitasking, and army control are the core skills.

Training

Players drill build orders, practise multitasking routines, and study replays of their own and opponents' matches.

Working demo program

ACTIONS_PER_MIN = 250
MATCH_MIN = 20
print("Real-time strategy actions example")
print("Actions per minute: %d, match length: %d minutes" % (ACTIONS_PER_MIN, MATCH_MIN))
print("Total actions: %d" % (ACTIONS_PER_MIN * MATCH_MIN))
print("Actions per second: %.1f" % (ACTIONS_PER_MIN / 60.0))

Fighting Games Rank 4 · simple · action genre · intermediate

Fighting games are one-against-one contests in which players control martial artists or fantasy fighters in short rounds, using attacks, blocks, throws, and special moves, with the first to win a set number of rounds taking the match. Matches are played as best-of series, and the genre's depth comes from spacing, timing, and reading the opponent: players must know when to attack, when to block, when to throw, and how to punish mistakes. Characters have distinct move sets, strengths, and weaknesses, so matchup knowledge matters, and top players study frame data, the precise timing of every move, to find advantages of a few hundredths of a second. Fighting game tournaments are famous for open brackets, where anyone can enter and face the best players in the world.

History

Fighting games grew from arcade contests in the late twentieth century into global tournaments with open brackets and professional players.

Rules

One against one, rounds to a set number, best-of series. Health bars decide rounds; timeouts go to the player with more health.

Techniques

Spacing, timing, blocking, throwing, combos, and matchup knowledge are the core skills.

Training

Players drill combos, study frame data, practise matchups, and play long sets against varied opponents.

Working demo program

ROUNDS_TO_WIN = 2

print("Rounds to win a set: %d, sets to win a match: %d" % (ROUNDS_TO_WIN, SETS_TO_WIN))
print("Maximum rounds in a match: %d" % (ROUNDS_TO_WIN * 2 * SETS_TO_WIN - 1))

Sports Simulations Rank 5 · simple · simulation genre · beginner

Sports simulation games recreate real sports in video game form, from football and basketball to racing and golf, and competitive play mirrors the structure of the real sport with seasons, tournaments, and team management. Players control athletes or whole teams, learning the rules, tactics, and timing of the sport, and competitive modes range from one-against-one matches to team leagues and management contests. The genre attracts players who love a real sport and want to play it year-round, and its tournaments often run alongside the real sport's calendar. Skill comes from game knowledge, tactical choice, and execution under pressure, and the best players study both the game and the real sport it models.

History

Sports simulation games grew from early computer sports titles into annual franchises with competitive leagues and tournaments.

Rules

Matches follow the rules of the sport modelled, adapted to game controls, with seasons and tournaments structuring competition.

Techniques

Game knowledge, tactical choice, timing, and execution under pressure are the core skills.

Training

Players drill game mechanics, study tactics, and play ranked matches and tournaments.

Battle Royale Rank 6 · compounded · action genre · beginner

Battle royale games drop dozens or a hundred players onto a large map, where they scavenge weapons and equipment, fight, and survive while a shrinking safe zone forces encounters, until one player or team remains. Matches combine looting, positioning, and combat: players must choose where to land, what to carry, when to fight, and when to move, and the shrinking zone means hiding forever is not a winning plan. Team formats add revival and coordination, with squads of two to four players sharing loot and covering each other. The genre's appeal is its unpredictability: every match starts equal, and any player can win with smart decisions and sharp aim.

History

Battle royale grew from mods and survival games in the 2010s into one of the most widely played competitive genres.

Rules

Dozens of players drop onto a map; a shrinking zone forces encounters; the last player or team standing wins.

Techniques

Landing choice, looting efficiency, positioning, rotation timing, and combat aim are the core skills.

Training

Players drill landing routes, practise rotations, and review endgame positioning.

Working demo program

print("Players: %d, squads: %d" % (PLAYERS, TEAMS))
print("Players per squad: %d" % (PLAYERS // TEAMS))
print("Zones example: 8 shrinking circles per match")

Card Battlers Rank 7 · compounded · strategy genre · intermediate

Card battlers are strategy games in which players build decks of cards representing creatures, spells, and effects, then duel by playing cards with limited resources, usually mana that grows each turn. Matches turn on deck building, resource management, and reading the opponent: a player must know when to spend resources, when to save them, and how to answer the opponent's threats. Digital card games add ranked ladders, tournaments, and regular new card releases that reshape the game, so the best decks change over time. The genre rewards planning, probability judgement, and adaptation, because every hand is different and no plan survives contact with the opponent's deck unchanged.

History

Card battlers grew from physical trading card games into digital titles with ranked ladders and tournaments.

Rules

Players build decks, play cards with growing mana, and win by reducing the opponent's health or meeting the win condition.

Techniques

Deck building, resource management, probability judgement, and opponent reading are the core skills.

Training

Players test decks, study the current card pool, and review match decisions.

print("Cards drawn per turn: 1")
print("Hand after 5 turns (before plays): %d" % (HAND_SIZE + 5))

Racing Simulations Rank 8 · compounded · simulation genre · intermediate

Racing simulations recreate motorsport with realistic physics, tracks, and car behaviour, and competitive play runs as online races, leagues, and championships mirroring real series. Players use steering wheels, pedals, and sometimes full rigs, learning braking points, racing lines, and car setup as in real motorsport, and races follow real formats with qualifying, pit stops, and safety rules. The genre attracts both gamers and real drivers, some of whom use simulation for training, and its top competitions draw professional-level preparation. Skill comes from consistency, racecraft, and setup knowledge, and the best sim racers are fast over a full race distance, not just one lap.

History

Racing simulations grew from early driving games into realistic titles with online leagues and professional-level competition.

Rules

Races follow real formats: qualifying, grid starts, pit stops, and driving standards enforced by stewards or game systems.

Techniques

Braking points, racing lines, car setup, and racecraft are the core skills.

Training

Drivers practise tracks, study telemetry, and race in leagues to build consistency.

Rhythm Games Rank 9 · compounded · music genre · beginner

Rhythm games ask players to press buttons, tap screens, or move in time with music, hitting notes as they arrive on a track, with accuracy, combos, and timing deciding scores. Competitive play runs as score attack on set songs, with difficulty levels from beginner charts to expert charts that demand hundreds of inputs per minute, and players are graded on timing windows from perfect to miss. The genre trains hand-eye coordination, rhythm sense, and stamina, and its communities share custom songs and charts that extend every game far beyond its built-in track list. Rhythm games are among the most accessible competitive genres, because the core test, hitting notes in time, is instantly understood by any new player.

History

Rhythm games grew from arcade dance and music machines in the late twentieth century into console, handheld, and mobile competitive titles.

Rules

Players hit notes in time with music; timing windows grade hits; combos build scores and misses break them.

Techniques

Timing accuracy, reading speed, hand coordination, and stamina are the core skills.

Training

Players practise charts at rising speeds, drill difficult patterns, and build stamina through longer songs.

Working demo program

print("Perfect rate: %.0f%%" % (PERFECT / NOTES * 100.0))
print("Accuracy (perfect plus good): %.0f%%" % ((PERFECT + GOOD) / NOTES * 100.0))

Speedrunning Rank 10 · compounded · skill genre · advanced

Speedrunning is the practice of completing a video game as fast as possible, with categories for finishing the main story, finishing with all items, or finishing with special conditions, and times verified by video and community rules. Runners study games frame by frame to find shortcuts, glitches, and optimal routes, then practise segments thousands of times until a full run comes together, and a single mistake can end a record attempt. Leaderboards track the fastest verified times per game and category, and events gather runners to perform live for charity audiences. Speedrunning rewards patience, precision, and deep game knowledge, because the fastest route is rarely the route the game designers intended.

History

Speedrunning grew from demo competitions and online video sharing in the late twentieth century into a global community with charity events.

Rules

Categories define what counts as finishing; times are verified by video under community rules for each game.

Techniques

Route planning, glitch use within category rules, segment practice, and execution under pressure are the core skills.

Training

Runners practise segments, study routes, and attempt full runs repeatedly.

Working demo program

print("Total run: %d seconds (%.1f minutes)" % (sum(SEGMENTS), sum(SEGMENTS) / 60.0))
print("Longest segment: %d seconds" % max(SEGMENTS))

Mobile Esports Rank 11 · compounded · platform genre · beginner

Mobile esports covers competitive gaming on phones and tablets, with titles adapted from MOBA, shooter, battle royale, and card genres, redesigned for touch controls and shorter matches. Mobile competition has grown fastest where phones are the main gaming device, with large tournaments, regional leagues, and team organisations built around mobile titles. Controls use virtual joysticks, touch aiming, and custom button layouts, and matches are often shorter than their PC equivalents to fit mobile play sessions. The genre's scale is enormous, because billions of people own phones, and its players include many who have never owned a gaming PC or console.

History

Mobile esports grew with smartphones in the 2010s into major tournaments and leagues, especially in regions where phones dominate gaming.

Rules

Matches follow the genre rules of each title, adapted to touch controls and often shorter formats.

Techniques

Touch aiming, virtual joystick movement, custom layouts, and quick decision-making are the core skills.

Training

Players drill touch controls, practise ranked matches, and review team play.

Working demo program

print("Daily play time: %d minutes (%.1f hours)" % (MATCH_MIN * MATCHES_PER_DAY, MATCH_MIN * MATCHES_PER_DAY / 60.0))

VR Esports Rank 12 · complex · platform genre · intermediate

VR esports covers competitive gaming in virtual reality, where players wear headsets and use motion controllers, and their physical movement, aiming, and positioning become part of the game. Titles span shooters, sports, rhythm, and strategy, adapted to room-scale movement, and competitions run in arenas with tracked play spaces or at home with standard headset setups. Physical fitness matters more than in other genres, because players move, duck, and aim with their whole bodies, and matches are often shorter to manage fatigue. The genre is younger than PC and mobile esports, with smaller but growing tournaments, and it points toward a future in which competitive gaming is as physical as traditional sport.

History

VR esports grew with consumer virtual reality headsets in the late 2010s into arena and online competitions.

Rules

Matches follow each title's rules, played in tracked spaces with headset and controller tracking.

Techniques

Physical movement, spatial awareness, motion aiming, and stamina are the core skills.

Training

Players drill movement, practise in tracked spaces, and build fitness for match lengths.

Coaching and Analytics Rank 13 · complex · team structure · advanced

Coaching and analytics in esports cover the staff and systems behind competitive teams: head coaches who set strategy and drafts, assistant coaches who work on individual skills, analysts who study opponents and statistics, and performance staff who manage training, health, and schedules. Analysts break down match data into patterns: which strategies work on which maps, where a team loses rounds, and how opponents behave under pressure, and coaches turn those patterns into practice plans. Teams review replays together, drill weak areas, and prepare specific plans for each opponent, much like traditional sports teams. The discipline has professionalised rapidly, and the best teams now treat coaching and analytics as equal to player talent.

History

Esports coaching grew from team captains into professional staff structures, with analysts and performance roles added as competition matured.

Rules

Coaches and analysts work within tournament rules on communication during matches, which vary by title and event.

Techniques

Replay review, statistical analysis, draft preparation, and practice planning are the core skills.

Training

Staff study matches, build opponent profiles, and design drills for team weaknesses.

Working demo program

MATCHES = 20
WINS = 13
print("Team analytics example")
print("Matches: %d, wins: %d" % (MATCHES, WINS))
print("Win rate: %.0f%%" % (WINS / MATCHES * 100.0))
print("Losses: %d" % (MATCHES - WINS))

Tournament Formats Rank 14 · complex · competition structure · intermediate

Tournament formats are the structures that organise esports competition: single elimination, where one loss ends a run; double elimination, where a loss sends a team to a lower bracket with a second chance; round robin, where every team plays every other team; and Swiss format, where teams play opponents with similar records each round. Leagues run over weeks with regular matches and standings, while cups and majors compress competition into days. Format choice shapes everything: single elimination rewards peak performance under pressure, double elimination rewards consistency, round robin rewards depth across many opponents, and Swiss balances fairness with manageable length. Organisers choose formats to fit the number of teams, the time available, and the story they want the event to tell.

History

Esports tournament formats were borrowed from traditional sports and chess, then adapted to online play and large team counts.

Rules

Each format defines progression, elimination, and seeding; brackets and standings are published before play.

Techniques

Seeding, bracket management, scheduling, and tiebreaker rules are the organiser's core skills.

Training

Organisers study past events, simulate brackets, and plan schedules around match lengths.

Broadcast and Production Rank 15 · complex · media structure · intermediate

Broadcast and production in esports covers how matches are shown to audiences: camera direction, commentary, analysis desks, replays, graphics, and streaming, produced live for viewers at home and in arenas. Observers control the in-game camera, choosing which player's view or which fight to show, while commentators explain action and analysts break down strategy between maps. Production teams manage overlays showing scores, statistics, and player cameras, and directors switch between game, studio, and crowd shots to build drama. Major events fill arenas with live crowds and broadcast to millions online, and the quality of production now rivals traditional sports television.

History

Esports broadcasting grew from community streams into professional productions with studios, arenas, and dedicated observer teams.

Rules

Broadcasts follow event rules on stream delay, which prevents teams gaining information from the stream.

Techniques

Camera observation, commentary, analysis, directing, and graphics operation are the core production skills.

Training

Production teams rehearse shows, practise observer camera work, and review broadcasts.

Fair Play and Anti-Cheat Rank 16 · complex · integrity · advanced

Fair play and anti-cheat in esports cover the rules and systems that keep competition honest: anti-cheat software that detects unauthorised programs, match integrity rules against match-fixing and information leaks, and conduct rules for players and teams. Anti-cheat systems monitor game files and behaviour for signs of aimbots, wallhacks, and other unauthorised assistance, and tournaments add referee oversight, player-area controls, and equipment checks. Players caught cheating face bans, prize forfeits, and team disqualification, and the most serious integrity cases are handled by tournament organisers and, where law applies, by legal authorities. Fair play also covers sportsmanship: respect for opponents, officials, and audiences, and honest play even when no one is watching.

History

Anti-cheat developed alongside online gaming, growing from simple checks into dedicated software and tournament integrity programmes.

Rules

Unauthorised software, match-fixing, and information leaks are banned; penalties include bans, forfeits, and disqualification.

Techniques

Detection software, referee oversight, equipment checks, and player education are the integrity tools.

Training

Players learn the rules, tournament staff train in integrity procedures, and teams brief players before events.
