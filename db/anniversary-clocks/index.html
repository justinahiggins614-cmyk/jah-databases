The 400-Day Principle: One Winding Per Year JAH-ANC-000001

Specifications. An anniversary clock is a small domestic clock designed to run for about 400 days on one winding, so it is traditionally wound once a year, often on an anniversary, which gives the clock its name. The movement is driven by a mainspring, and the long running time is achieved by a very slow, low-energy timekeeper: a torsion pendulum that rotates back and forth rather than swinging. Typical anniversary clocks stand 20 to 30 centimetres tall under a glass dome.

Mechanism. The mainspring drives a gear train that steps down to an escape wheel, and the torsion pendulum regulates the release. Because the torsion pendulum turns slowly and needs only a tiny impulse each cycle, the train consumes very little power, and the spring's energy stretches over more than a year. The going barrel and train are geared for a long, slow delivery rather than a powerful one.

Maintenance. Wind once a year on a fixed date and record it, and do not overwind: stop as soon as firm resistance is felt. Keep the clock level, out of draughts, and under its dome, since dust on the suspension spring or the rotating balls upsets the delicate balance. Have the movement cleaned every five to ten years.

Build and restoration guidance. When servicing a 400-day clock, never force the winding arbor and never handle the suspension spring with bare fingers, since skin oil corrodes it. Always lock the pendulum before moving the clock, and release it only after the clock is level.

Tools. Winding key, spirit level, dome cloth, service logbook.

The Torsion Pendulum: A Rotating Ball Balance JAH-ANC-000002

Specifications. The torsion pendulum of an anniversary clock is usually a horizontal bar or disc carrying two or four decorative balls, suspended from a thin spring wire. The pendulum does not swing side to side: it rotates clockwise and anticlockwise, twisting the suspension spring first one way and then the other. A full rotation cycle takes about 10 to 20 seconds on most anniversary clocks, far slower than an ordinary pendulum's beat.

Mechanism. The suspension spring acts as the restoring force. When the pendulum rotates, it twists the spring, which stores energy and then unwinds, rotating the pendulum back. The escape wheel gives the pendulum a tiny impulse at each cycle to replace the energy lost to air resistance and friction. The period depends on the pendulum's moment of inertia and the spring's torsional stiffness, not on gravity, which is why the clock is less sensitive to levelling than a swinging pendulum, though it still must sit level to beat evenly.

Maintenance. Never spin the torsion pendulum by hand to start it, and never let the balls strike the dome or the pillars. If the pendulum wobbles instead of rotating cleanly, the suspension spring is bent or the bottom block is loose. Keep the balls clean and matched, since an unbalanced ball set makes the rotation uneven.

Build and restoration guidance. When fitting a new suspension spring, use the exact thickness specified for the movement, because a thicker spring runs fast and a thinner one runs slow. Hang the pendulum, level the clock, and observe several full rotations before judging the beat.

Tools. Suspension spring gauge, level, ball-polishing cloth, tweezers.

The Suspension Spring: The Heart of the 400-Day Clock JAH-ANC-000003

Specifications. The suspension spring is a thin flat strip or wire of spring steel, typically 0.05 to 0.10 millimetres thick, that both hangs the torsion pendulum and provides its restoring force. Different movements use different spring thicknesses, and makers issued numbered suspension units so a repairer could match the spring to the movement. The spring hangs from a top block fixed to the movement and carries a bottom block that holds the pendulum.

Mechanism. Twisting the spring stores elastic energy, and the spring's stiffness sets the rotation period together with the pendulum's inertia. The escapement's anchor or verge engages with the pendulum's rotation through a pin or fork, releasing one tooth of the escape wheel per cycle. Because the spring is so thin, it is also the most fragile part of the clock and the most common cause of a stopped anniversary clock.

Maintenance. Replace a kinked, rusted or broken suspension spring rather than trying to straighten it, and always fit the correct thickness for the movement. Handle the new spring by its blocks only, keep it dry, and check that it hangs vertically without touching the pillars or the back plate.

Build and restoration guidance. When replacing a suspension unit, fit the top block, hang the pendulum, and set the bottom block so the pendulum rotates freely and evenly. Test the rate over several days before returning the clock, since a new spring settles slightly at first.

Tools. Suspension unit set, spring thickness gauge, tweezers, magnifier.

Setting the Beat on a Torsion Clock JAH-ANC-000004

Specifications. Beat setting on an anniversary clock means making the torsion pendulum rotate evenly in both directions, so the escapement releases the same amount of energy on each side of the rotation. An out-of-beat torsion clock may run for a while and then stop, or may show a visibly uneven rotation with a longer pause on one side. The adjustment is made at the bottom block or the anchor, depending on the movement, and only in very small steps.

Mechanism. The escapement engages the pendulum through a fork or pin. If the engagement is centred, each rotation gives the escape wheel an equal step; if it is offset, one side of the rotation releases too early and the other too late. The clock's beat is judged by watching the rotation and listening to the faint tick, which should be evenly spaced in time even though the rotation itself is slow.

Maintenance. Level the clock first, because a tilted clock cannot be put into beat. Adjust in tiny steps and observe at least ten full rotations after each change. Never bend the suspension spring to correct the beat, since that changes the period and damages the spring.

Build and restoration guidance. Build a beat-setting routine into every service: level, observe, adjust, observe again. Record the final beat observation in the service notes so the next repairer knows the clock was left in beat.

Tools. Spirit level, beat observation chart, fine screwdriver, magnifier.

Regulating the Rate: Faster and Slower JAH-ANC-000005

Specifications. The rate of an anniversary clock is adjusted by changing the torsion pendulum's moment of inertia, usually by moving the decorative balls in or out along their arms, or by raising or lowering a regulating disc. Moving the balls outward slows the clock, because the same spring must turn a larger inertia; moving them inward speeds it up. The adjustment is sensitive, and a millimetre of ball movement can change the rate by minutes per week.

Mechanism. The rotation period follows the torsion formula, in which the period grows with the square root of the moment of inertia. The escapement and train then convert the pendulum's rotation into the hands' motion through fixed gear ratios, so a change in the pendulum period changes the displayed rate directly. The regulating adjustment never changes the gearing, only the pendulum.

Maintenance. Adjust the balls equally on both sides, move them in small steps, and wait at least a week between adjustments before judging the result. Record the ball positions and the weekly rate, so the adjustment can be repeated or reversed. Do not regulate against a clock that is out of beat or dirty, since those faults mask the true rate.

Build and restoration guidance. When restoring a clock with missing balls, match the replacements in weight and size to the surviving balls, because unequal balls unbalance the rotation. Set the balls to a mid-position first, then regulate from there.

Tools. Ball position gauge, rate logbook, fine ruler, tweezers.

German 400-Day Clocks: Schatz, Kundo and Kern JAH-ANC-000006

Specifications. The classic 400-day anniversary clock was made in large numbers in Germany in the twentieth century, most famously by makers including Schatz, Kundo and Kern, whose movements powered clocks sold under many brand names and in many case styles. Typical examples stand about 25 centimetres tall, with a brass movement, a glass dome, and a four-ball torsion pendulum. Production was at its height from the mid-twentieth century, and surviving examples are now common on the second-hand market.

Mechanism. Despite many case styles, the movements share the same principle: a mainspring barrel, a going train, an anchor or verge escapement acting on the torsion pendulum, and a suspension spring matched to the movement. The differences between makers lie in plate layout, suspension unit numbering, and the style of the pendulum and dial, not in the basic timekeeping idea.

Maintenance. Identify the movement by its plate markings and suspension number before ordering parts, since suspension springs are not interchangeable between makers. Clean and oil on a five-to-ten-year cycle, wind annually, and keep the dome on, because dust is the main enemy of these delicate movements.

Build and restoration guidance. When restoring a German 400-day clock, keep the original dial, hands and pendulum style, and replace only the worn parts with matching stock. Record the maker's marks and suspension number in the restoration notes for the next repairer.

Tools. Maker's mark reference, suspension number chart, dome cloth, service logbook.

Glass Domes: Protection, Display and Care JAH-ANC-000007

Specifications. The glass dome of an anniversary clock is both its protection and its display case: it keeps dust and draughts off the delicate torsion pendulum while letting the rotating balls be seen. Domes are made of blown or moulded glass, typically 15 to 20 centimetres tall, and sit on a wooden or metal base with a felt or rubber seal. A chipped or cracked dome should be replaced, since a damaged dome no longer seals and can shed glass near the movement.

Mechanism. The dome works by stilling the air around the pendulum. A draught across an uncovered torsion pendulum steals energy and disturbs the rotation, so the clock may stop even though the movement is healthy. The dome also keeps dust off the suspension spring, where a single fibre can change the period or stop the clock.

Maintenance. Lift the dome straight up, never tilt it against the pendulum, and clean it with a soft cloth and glass cleaner, never with abrasive pads. Check the base seal annually and replace hardened felt. Keep the clock out of direct sunlight under its dome, since heat expands the air inside and can mist the glass.

Build and restoration guidance. When fitting a replacement dome, measure the base diameter and the height over the pendulum with the pendulum at its highest point. Test that the dome clears the balls at every point of the rotation before leaving the clock.

Tools. Soft cloth, glass cleaner, base seal felt, dome measuring gauge.

Disc Pendulums and Other Torsion Designs JAH-ANC-000008

Specifications. Not every anniversary clock uses a ball pendulum. Some use a flat disc, a decorative figure, or a set of ornamental shapes as the rotating mass, and a few use a two-ball or three-ball arrangement instead of the usual four. The principle is identical in every case: a rotating mass suspended from a torsion spring. Disc pendulums are often found on clocks designed as display pieces, where the rotating disc itself is the decoration.

Mechanism. The disc's moment of inertia depends on its mass and on how that mass is spread from the centre, so a large light disc can have the same period as a small heavy ball set if the inertia matches. The suspension spring must still be matched to the inertia, and the escapement must still receive one impulse per rotation cycle. Changing the pendulum style without changing the spring changes the rate.

Maintenance. Keep the disc clean and balanced, and check that it does not wobble, since a wobbling disc rubs energy from the rotation. Handle decorative pendulums by their centre block, never by the rim or the figures, which bend easily.

Build and restoration guidance. When replacing a lost disc pendulum, match the original's weight and diameter as closely as possible, then regulate the rate by the standard ball or disc adjustment. Record the replacement's dimensions in the service notes.

Tools. Disc balance gauge, soft cloth, centre-block tweezers, rate log.

Winding, Overwinding and the Mainspring JAH-ANC-000009

Specifications. The mainspring of a 400-day clock is a long, thin spring coiled in a barrel, designed to deliver a small, steady torque for over a year. It is wound through the base or the back of the movement with a key, and the winding arbor usually turns many times to reach full wind. The spring's torque falls as it unwinds, but the torsion pendulum needs so little power that the clock keeps good time across most of the wind, with the rate changing only near the very end.

Mechanism. The barrel turns slowly as the spring unwinds, and the gear train steps that motion up to the escape wheel. A slipping bridle or a stopwork arrangement prevents overwinding damage on some movements, but many anniversary clocks rely on the winder stopping at firm resistance. Forcing the key past resistance can break the spring or the winding arbor.

Maintenance. Wind slowly and steadily, stop at firm resistance, and never wind a clock that has just been moved until it is level and the pendulum is released. If the clock stops shortly after winding, suspect a broken mainspring or a jammed train, not a winding fault, and do not keep forcing the key.

Build and restoration guidance. When replacing a mainspring, match its length, width and thickness to the original, fit it with a proper winder, and test the full 400-day torque delivery on the bench where possible. A spring that is too strong overdrives the escapement and a spring that is too weak stops the clock early.

Tools. Winding key, mainspring winder, barrel clamp, torque log.

Cleaning and Oiling a 400-Day Movement JAH-ANC-000010

Specifications. A 400-day movement should be cleaned and oiled every five to ten years, or sooner if it stops, runs unevenly, or shows visible dust and dried oil. The movement is small and delicate, with fine pivots and a thin suspension spring, so cleaning is done by dismantling, washing the parts in a clock-cleaning solution, rinsing, drying, and reassembling with a tiny amount of clock oil at each pivot. The suspension spring is replaced, not cleaned, if it is kinked or rusted.

Mechanism. Dried oil and dust increase friction in the train, and the torsion pendulum's tiny impulse cannot overcome much extra friction, so a dirty anniversary clock stops long before a dirty ordinary clock would. Cleaning restores the free running of the train, and fresh oil protects the pivots. Over-oiling is as harmful as under-oiling, since excess oil migrates onto the suspension spring and the escape wheel.

Maintenance. Oil one tiny drop per pivot, keep oil off the suspension spring, the pendulum and the dial, and run the clock for a fortnight after service before judging the rate. Keep a service label inside the base with the date and the work done.

Build and restoration guidance. Photograph the movement before dismantling, lay the parts out in train order, and reassemble in reverse order, checking each wheel for free running as it goes in. Fit a new suspension spring at every full service unless the old one is provably perfect.

Tools. Clock cleaning solution, pivot oilers, parts tray, service labels.

Transporting an Anniversary Clock Safely JAH-ANC-000011

Specifications. Transport is the most dangerous time in an anniversary clock's life, because the torsion pendulum swings freely and the suspension spring is easily broken by a jolt. A clock prepared for transport has its pendulum locked or removed, its dome packed separately, and its movement secured so nothing can shift. The clock travels upright, never on its side, and is never carried by its dome.

Mechanism. Locking the pendulum, usually with a transit screw or a clip, stops the rotating mass from twisting the suspension spring beyond its elastic limit. Removing the pendulum entirely is safer for a long journey, provided the suspension spring is also protected and the pendulum is packed so its balls cannot dent each other. The mainspring should be left wound or unwound according to the maker's instruction for that movement, never half-wound by guesswork.

Maintenance. After transport, level the clock, release or refit the pendulum, check the suspension spring for kinks, and observe the rotation for a full day before judging the rate. A clock that ran well before transport and runs badly after it has almost always suffered a suspension or beat disturbance in transit.

Build and restoration guidance. Build a transit kit for the clock: a pendulum lock or a padded pendulum box, a dome box, and a written release checklist fixed inside the base. A clock with a transit kit survives moves that destroy clocks without one.

Tools. Transit screw or clip, padded pendulum box, dome box, release checklist.

Identifying Anniversary Clocks: Marks, Plates and Styles JAH-ANC-000012

Specifications. Anniversary clocks are identified by their movement plate markings, their suspension unit number, their case and dial style, and their pendulum design. German movements usually carry a maker's name or trademark stamped on the back plate, and the suspension number is often stamped on the movement or listed in repair references. Case styles range from plain brass and glass to ornate gilt, marble-base and novelty designs, and the style helps date the clock within broad periods.

Mechanism. Identification matters mechanically, not just for collectors, because the suspension spring, the mainspring and the pendulum are matched to the movement. Ordering parts by appearance alone risks a spring of the wrong thickness, which changes the rate and may stop the clock. The plate markings are therefore the first thing a repairer reads, before any part is ordered.

Maintenance. Record the plate markings, suspension number and pendulum style in the service notes at the first service, and keep that record with the clock. Photograph the back plate, since stamps wear and later owners may not be able to read them.

Build and restoration guidance. When restoring an unidentified clock, compare the plate layout and suspension mounting with repair references before ordering parts, and test any candidate suspension spring for rate before fitting it permanently.

Tools. Maker's mark reference, camera, suspension chart, service notes.

Temperature, Draughts and Placement JAH-ANC-000013

Specifications. An anniversary clock should stand on a firm, level surface away from direct sunlight, radiators, draughty windows and doors, and vibrating appliances. The torsion pendulum's period depends on the spring's stiffness, which changes slightly with temperature, and on air resistance, which changes with draughts, so placement affects the rate more than owners expect. A clock that gains in winter and loses in summer is usually responding to its room, not to a fault.

Mechanism. The suspension spring stiffens slightly as temperature falls, shortening the rotation period and making the clock gain, and softens as temperature rises, making it lose. Draughts steal energy from the slow rotation and can stop the clock, while vibration from a nearby appliance disturbs the beat. The glass dome reduces draught effects but cannot remove temperature effects.

Maintenance. Choose one stable spot and leave the clock there, regulate it in that spot, and do not move it seasonally. If the rate changes with the seasons, record the room temperature with the rate before adjusting anything, so the adjustment is made for the right reason.

Build and restoration guidance. When installing a clock for a customer, pick the spot together, level the clock there, and leave a written note of the spot and the regulated rate. A clock moved to a new room needs re-regulation, not repair.

Modern Anniversary Clocks and Quartz Replicas JAH-ANC-000014

Specifications. Modern anniversary-style clocks include both true mechanical 400-day movements, still made in small numbers, and quartz replicas that imitate the rotating-ball appearance with a battery movement. A quartz replica usually rotates its balls by a small motor rather than by a torsion spring, runs on a battery, and needs no annual winding. The two types look similar under a dome but are different machines, and they are serviced differently.

Mechanism. In a true mechanical anniversary clock, the torsion spring is the timekeeper and the mainspring is the power source. In a quartz replica, a quartz crystal and an electronic circuit divide time, and the rotating balls are decoration driven by the same circuit. A replica that stops usually needs a battery, while a mechanical clock that stops needs diagnosis of its spring, train or suspension.

Maintenance. For a mechanical clock, follow the annual winding and five-to-ten-year service cycle. For a quartz replica, replace the battery promptly, keep the battery contacts clean, and never oil a quartz movement as if it were mechanical. Label the base with the type, so a future owner does not wind a quartz clock or fit a battery to a mechanical one.

Build and restoration guidance. When restoring a clock of unknown type, check the back plate and the base for a battery compartment before winding anything. Converting a mechanical clock to quartz destroys its character and value, and should only be done at an owner's explicit instruction, with the original movement kept.

Tools. Battery tester, type label, soft cloth, service notes.

Collecting Anniversary Clocks: Condition and Value JAH-ANC-000015

Specifications. Anniversary clocks are collected for their case styles, their makers, their pendulum designs and their condition, and the most valued examples are complete, original and running: original dome, original dial and hands, matching pendulum, and a movement that runs its full 400-day cycle. Common faults that reduce value are replaced domes, repainted dials, mismatched pendulums and broken suspension springs, all of which are repairable but all of which must be disclosed.

Mechanism. A collector's first test is mechanical: does the clock run, does the torsion pendulum rotate evenly, and does it keep time over a week? A clock that looks perfect but stops after a day has a train or suspension fault, and a clock that runs well but carries a wrong pendulum will never regulate correctly. Condition and mechanism are judged together, never separately.

Maintenance. Buy running clocks where possible, service a new purchase before displaying it, and keep the service record with the clock. Store spare domes and pendulums safely, since a broken dome or a lost ball set is harder to replace than a mainspring.

Build and restoration guidance. When preparing a clock for sale or display, service it fully, fit the correct suspension spring, regulate it over a fortnight, and write an honest condition note covering every replacement part. An honest note protects both seller and buyer.

Tools. Condition checklist, service record forms, dome storage boxes, rate log.

Displaying Anniversary Clocks: Light, Dust and Safety JAH-ANC-000016

Specifications. A displayed anniversary clock needs stable light, low dust and protection from knocks, pets and curious hands. Display it under its dome on a firm shelf or cabinet, out of direct sunlight, with enough clearance to lift the dome straight up for winding and service. A clock displayed in a busy hallway or near a door will be knocked, draughted and stopped more often than one displayed in a quiet room.

Mechanism. The display affects the mechanism directly: sunlight heats the dome and the spring, dust settles on the suspension and the train, and vibration from foot traffic disturbs the beat. A good display is therefore a mechanical decision, not only a visual one, and the best-looking spot is not always the best-running spot.

Maintenance. Dust the dome weekly, clean the shelf monthly, wind on the same date each year, and check the rotation monthly. Keep the clock away from the edge of the shelf, and never display it where the dome can be knocked by a door or a passing bag.

Build and restoration guidance. When building a display cabinet for anniversary clocks, fit a level shelf, a dust seal, soft lighting that does not heat the dome, and a locked door if children or pets are present. Leave service access behind or above the clock, so it can be lifted out without disturbing its neighbours.

Tools. Display shelf level, soft lighting, dust cloth, winding-date card.
