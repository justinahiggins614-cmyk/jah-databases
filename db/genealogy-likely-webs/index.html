What a Likely Web Is Rank 1 · simple

A likely web is a network of proposed relationships between records, built from evidence and labeled by confidence, in which no link is claimed as proven unless a record proves it. A web connects a baptism to a marriage, a marriage to a census household, and a household to a burial, with each connection scored on name, age, place, and associates. The web is a research tool: it proposes the most likely reading of scattered records, and it shows its workings so that another researcher can test, strengthen, or break any link. A web that hides its weak links is a trap; a web that labels them is a map.

Evidence, Not Proof: The LIKELY Label Rank 2 · simple

Evidence, not proof, is the rule of the LIKELY label: a link labeled LIKELY is the best current reading of the evidence, with its basis stated and its confidence given, and it must never be quoted as a proven fact. Proof in family history means a record that directly states the relationship, such as a birth certificate naming parents, or two records that agree on every identifying field. Everything short of that is inference, and inference is labeled by strength: strong, moderate, or weak, with the evidence for each level stated. A researcher who copies a LIKELY link without its label has turned a tool into a false claim, and this pair forbids that copying by keeping the label attached to every link.

Name Matching Scores Rank 3 · simple

Name matching scores compare names across records by sound, spelling, and forename use, and they are the first filter in any likely web. A full name match on an uncommon name in the right place scores high, while a match on a common name scores low, and a forename change, such as a middle name used as a first name, lowers the score unless a second record explains it. Worked examples in this pair use synthetic placeholders, Person A and Person B, labeled synthetic, never real people, so that the scoring method can be taught without any personal data. A name score alone never proves a link: it proposes a candidate, and age, place, and associates must confirm it.

Date Windows and Age Logic Rank 4 · simple

Date windows and age logic test whether a proposed link is possible in time: a parent must be old enough at a child's birth, a marriage must follow birth by a plausible interval, and a person cannot appear in two places at once without travel time. Age windows allow for stated-age error, usually a few years in census and death records, and a link that requires an impossible age is broken, not stretched. A worked synthetic example shows the logic: Person A, stated age twenty in one record and stated age forty-five twenty years later, fails the age window, while Person B, stated age twenty and then forty, passes it. The window is a filter, not a proof, and passing it only keeps a candidate alive.

Place Proximity Scoring Rank 5 · simple

Place proximity scoring asks how far apart the places in two records are, and whether a person could plausibly have moved between them in the time available. A baptism in one parish and a marriage in the next parish scores high for proximity, while a marriage hundreds of miles away with no migration evidence scores low, unless a passenger list, a census move, or a settlement cluster explains it. Place names must be matched carefully: the same place name may exist in several counties, and a county given as a birthplace is a region, not a village. Proximity is supporting evidence, and it is strongest when the places form a sequence, not a single jump.

Household Reconstruction Rank 6 · simple

Household reconstruction builds a household across records: a marriage pair, their children by baptism order, and the household's census appearances, with each member tracked by age and place. The reconstruction is LIKELY where census and vital records agree on the members, and it is uncertain where a child appears in one record but not the next, which may mean death, departure, or undercount. A worked synthetic example reconstructs a household of Person A, spouse Person B, and three children, labeled synthetic, showing how a missing child in a later census is marked as unknown rather than assumed dead. Reconstruction turns scattered entries into a family, but every member added without a record is marked as proposed, not proven.

Combining Independent Evidence Rank 7 · compounded

Combining independent evidence is the core of a likely web: name, age, place, and associates are independent checks, and a link supported by three of them is stronger than a link supported by one, even if that one is strong. Independence matters: two records copied from the same source are one piece of evidence, not two, and a census entry and a vital record that agree are stronger than two census entries that agree, because they come from different systems. The combination rule is stated plainly: a link needs at least two independent agreements to reach moderate confidence, and three to reach strong confidence, with the agreements named. A link built on a single agreement stays weak, however suggestive it looks.

Confidence Levels and How They Are Set Rank 8 · compounded

Confidence levels are set by rule, not by feeling: weak means one agreement or a suggestive pattern, moderate means two independent agreements, and strong means three independent agreements with no contradiction. Strong is still LIKELY, not proven, because only a direct record proves a relationship, and a strong web link may still fall to a new record. Levels are recorded with their basis, so that a reader can see why a link is strong and which agreement would break it. A web whose levels are set by gut feeling is not a web but a guess, and this pair's rule exists to prevent that.

Conflicting Evidence: Two Candidates, One Name Rank 9 · compounded

Conflicting evidence, such as two candidates with the same name in the same place and period, is the hardest problem in likely webs, and it is solved by comparison, not by choice. Each candidate is scored on age, place, associates, and occupation, and the candidate with more independent agreements wins, while the loser is kept as a rejected candidate with its reason, not deleted. Deleting a rejected candidate hides the problem, and the next researcher will repeat it; keeping it shows that the choice was considered. Where the candidates tie, the link stays unresolved, labeled as a tie, and no link is made until a new record breaks it.

Negative Evidence: Absence as Signal Rank 10 · compounded

Negative evidence is absence used carefully: a person absent from a register that should contain them, if they lived there, is evidence against a link, but only when the register's coverage is known. A missing baptism in a parish with complete registers counts against a claimed birth there, while a missing baptism in a parish with gaps counts for little. Negative evidence is never proof of absence from life: a person may have been born elsewhere, registered under a variant, or missed by undercount. The rule is proportionate: negative evidence lowers confidence by a stated amount, and it breaks a link only when the register's completeness makes absence decisive.

Migration Links in a Likely Web Rank 11 · compounded

Migration links in a likely web connect records across places: a baptism in one county, a marriage in another, and a census household in a third, scored as a sequence with plausible travel and timing. A migration link is LIKELY when the sequence is ordered, the ages fit, and at least one associate or occupation travels with the person, because a name alone traveling is weak evidence. Settlement clusters support migration links: a migrant who settles among people from the same origin is more likely to be the same person than a migrant who appears alone. A migration link that jumps a long distance without explanation stays weak, however well the names match.

Surname Clusters as Supporting Evidence Rank 12 · complex

Surname clusters as supporting evidence are groups of same-surname households in one place, used to support a link between two records carrying that surname. A cluster is strong supporting evidence when the surname is rare in the region, and weak when the surname is common, because common names cluster by chance. Clusters are never proof: they generate candidates and support links, and the link itself still needs age, place, and associate agreement. A cluster used as proof would turn every common surname into a false family, and this pair's rule forbids that reading by keeping clusters in the supporting-evidence column, never in the proof column.

DNA Matches as Supporting Evidence: Aggregate Only Rank 13 · complex

DNA matches as supporting evidence, in aggregate only, are reported here as a method: groups of test results sharing a lineage can support a surname or regional hypothesis at the aggregate level, as covered in the surname-geography and haplogroup-webs pairs. This pair holds no individual match lists, no individual genomes, and no finding about any living person, and it treats aggregate DNA as one supporting line among several, never as proof of a pedigree. A DNA aggregate that supports a link raises its confidence by one level at most, and a DNA aggregate that contradicts a link lowers it, but neither alone makes or breaks a link. The method is included so that researchers understand what DNA can add to a web, and what it cannot.

When to Break a Link Rank 14 · complex

When to break a link is decided by rule: a link breaks when a new record contradicts it on age, place, or identity, when its agreements turn out to share a single source, or when a better candidate scores higher on independent evidence. Breaking is recorded, not hidden: the old link is kept as a broken link with its reason, so that the next researcher understands why it was abandoned. A link that is kept after contradiction, because it is convenient or familiar, corrupts the whole web, because later links built on it inherit its error. The discipline of breaking is what separates a web from a story.

Documenting Uncertainty for the Next Researcher Rank 15 · complex

Documenting uncertainty for the next researcher means writing down every link's basis, confidence, and alternatives, in a form that survives the researcher. A web document lists each link, its agreements, its contradictions, its rejected candidates, and its gaps, so that a stranger can rebuild the reasoning without asking the author. Uncertainty is documented in plain words: LIKELY, with the evidence named, and gaps marked as gaps, not smoothed over. A web that documents its uncertainty is a gift to the next researcher; a web that hides it is a debt.
