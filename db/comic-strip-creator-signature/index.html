A Three-Panel Joke That Lands Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: buildable-now Three-panel joke structure is established practice in comics today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a comic strip creator that builds a joke in three panels: a setup, a turn, and a punchline, read left to right. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

A joke told in the wrong order does not land: the punchline comes first and the setup comes last, the reader is confused instead of amused, and the comic fails at the one thing it was made to do. A creator that builds every joke in three panels, with the setup on the left, the turn in the middle, and the punchline on the right, gives the joke its order back: the reader follows the story panel by panel, the turn surprises them, and the punchline lands. The three-panel shape is the oldest joke shape in comics, and the creator keeps it, because it works: setup, turn, punchline, and nothing else.

Worked example, Signature design: the same pencil joke is built punchline-first, the setup is one line, and the punchline sits in the last panel alone.

History

Three-panel jokes are as old as newspaper comics, where a joke had to fit in a strip three pictures wide and land before the reader turned the page. The creator keeps that old shape, because it still works.

Proposed solution

Start from the punchline, then build the setup toward it; Give each part one panel, and no more; Keep the setup to one line, so the turn has room to surprise; Put the punchline last, and test it on a fresh reader; Cut any panel that does not serve the punchline.

Dependencies

A three-panel shape; a setup, a turn, and a punchline; a left-to-right order; a fresh reader to test the joke.

Feasibility

Verification path

Show a three-panel joke to five people who have not seen it and check that at least four laugh or smile at the last panel; check that no one needed the order explained.

A comic with bubbles in the wrong order cannot be read: the answer comes before the question, the joke is spoiled, and the reader has to guess which bubble to read first. A creator that places bubbles in reading order, top to bottom and left to right, with a tail that points clearly to the speaker, and checks the order before export, removes the guessing: the reader follows the bubbles in order, the conversation makes sense, and the joke lands. A field guide index to the twenty-three records in this database: a map of the whole set, ordered simple to compounded to complex, so a reader can find any record by number and by name. This is a design index for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

This database holds twenty-three full records about the comic strip creator, ordered from simple to compounded to complex, and a reader arriving without a map cannot tell which record to read first, which records belong together, or where the harder designs begin. The field guide index solves that by listing every record by number and by name, in order, with a one-line account of what each record covers and which rung of the ladder it sits on. Without the index, the simple records and the complex records blur together, and a reader who needs the basics may open a complex design first, while a reader who wants the hardest designs may never find them. The index is the map that keeps the ladder readable, and it is the record that turns twenty-three separate designs into one set a reader can walk through from start to finish.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order, with its Signature design noted; Give each record a one-line account of what its Signature version changes; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.

Dependencies

The twenty-three records themselves, listed in order; the ladder ranks simple, compounded, and complex; agreement that an index lists, and never replaces, the records it points to.
