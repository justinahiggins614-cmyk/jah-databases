A book that is only read alone is only half a book, and this chapter gives the book a ceremony of reading: a crystal-light ceremony at the wall. A ceremony is a shared way of doing a simple thing with attention, and the ceremony of reading in this book has four movements: gathering, silence, reading aloud, and silence again. The gathering brings readers together without hurry. The first silence lets each reader arrive. The reading aloud lets the quoted words be heard in a human voice, slowly, with pauses at the punctuation. The final silence lets the words settle before commentary begins, because commentary that rushes in too quickly drowns the text it claims to serve. The ceremony is a practice proposed by this book, not a rite claimed from any authority, and it is offered for any group that wishes to try it.

The ceremony works because it slows down the fastest readers and gives the slowest readers room. In ordinary reading, the quick finish first and the slow feel left behind, but in a ceremony of reading, everyone hears the same words at the same pace, and the pauses belong to everyone equally. The reader who leads the ceremony is asked to read as plainly as possible, without performance, because performance draws attention to the reader while plain reading draws attention to the words. After the reading, the ceremony allows one round of short responses, each no longer than a sentence, so that every voice is heard before the longer discussion begins, and no single voice can take the whole hour.

The ceremony of reading also protects the boundary of this book. Because the quoted words are read aloud first, and the commentary is labeled as commentary afterwards, no listener can mistake a comment for a quotation, and no leader can smuggle an opinion into the text by reading it in the same tone. The ceremony therefore ends with a spoken reminder: the words just heard are cited, the words that follow are commentary, and the difference matters. A group that keeps that reminder will read honestly even when it disagrees, and a group that drops it will soon argue about words that were never in the passage at all, which is the most common failure of shared reading.
