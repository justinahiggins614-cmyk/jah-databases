Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis

The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is the idea that language shapes thought: that the language a person speaks affects how they see the world, what they notice, and what they can think. In its strong form, language determines thought; in its weak form, language influences it. The hypothesis is the most famous idea in the study of language and thought, and the most debated. Linguistic Relativity

Linguistic relativity is the claim that thought differs with language: that speakers of different languages think differently about the same world, because their languages give them different categories. The claim is weaker than determinism: it says language influences thought, not that it fixes it, and it is tested by comparing speakers of languages that differ in one category, such as colour, space, or time. Linguistic Determinism

Linguistic determinism is the claim that language fixes what can be thought: that a person cannot think what their language has no words for. It is the strong form of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, and it is now rejected by most linguists, because speakers can think new thoughts, borrow words, and describe what their language lacks. Determinism survives as a warning: language makes some thoughts easy and others hard, even if it does not forbid them. Spatial Language

Spatial language is the talking about space: up and down, left and right, near and far, and the frames that say where things are. Some languages use left and right, some use north and south, and some use uphill and downhill, and the frame a language uses affects how its speakers remember where things are and how they find their way. Spatial language is the most tested case of linguistic relativity. Number Words

Mentalese

Mentalese is the proposed language of thought: the inner code in which thinking happens, distinct from any spoken language. The idea is that the mind thinks in a language of its own, with its own symbols and rules, and that spoken languages are translations of it. Mentalese is a hypothesis, not a fact, and it is debated whether the mind needs any language at all to think. Evidentiality

Evidentiality is the marking of how you know: whether you saw it, heard it, inferred it, or were told it. Some languages must mark the source of every claim, so that a speaker cannot say it rained without saying whether they saw the rain or heard about it. Evidentiality shows that grammar can carry the speaker's evidence, and it affects how speakers think about knowledge itself.
