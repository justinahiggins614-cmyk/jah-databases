Sprints Rank 1 · simple · running · beginner

Sprints are short, flat-out races over 60, 100, 200, and 400 metres, and the 100 metres is the most famous race in athletics. Sprinters start from blocks on the sound of the gun, drive hard through the first metres, rise into full stride, and hold top speed to the line, with races decided by hundredths of a second and photo finishes when needed. The 400 metres adds a different challenge: it is a sprint held for a full lap, so runners must pace the first half well enough to survive the second. Training mixes starts, acceleration work, top-speed running, and strength work in the gym, and races reward explosive power, relaxed technique at speed, and the ability to stay composed under pressure.

History

Sprinting is as old as organised athletics, with the 100 metres as the flagship event of the Olympic programme since the modern Games began in 1896.

Rules

Runners stay in lanes for 100, 200, and 400 metres. False starts are penalised, and photo finishes decide close results.

Techniques

Block starts, drive phase, upright sprinting posture, and relaxed arm action are the core technical elements.

Training

Sessions mix block starts, acceleration runs, flying sprints at top speed, and gym strength work.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 100
TIME_S = 10.0
print("Sprint speed example")
print("Distance: %d m, time: %.1f seconds" % (DISTANCE_M, TIME_S))
print("Average speed: %.1f m/s" % (DISTANCE_M / TIME_S))
print("Average speed: %.1f km/h" % (DISTANCE_M / TIME_S * 3.6))

Middle-Distance Running Rank 2 · simple · running · intermediate

Middle-distance running covers the 800 and 1500 metres, with the mile as the classic imperial cousin, and it sits exactly between sprinting and endurance: runners need speed and the ability to hold it while fatigue builds. The 800 metres is two laps of tactical racing, often with a fast first lap and a fight for position into the final straight, while the 1500 metres adds laps of positioning, surges, and a sprint finish. Tactics matter as much as fitness: a runner who leads from the gun risks being out-kicked, while a runner who sits too far back risks being boxed in. Training combines speed sessions, longer tempo runs, and race-pace repetitions that teach the body to run fast while tired.

History

Middle-distance racing has been central to athletics since the nineteenth century, with the mile as its most storied distance and the 1500 metres as the Olympic metric standard.

Rules

The 800 metres starts in lanes and breaks for the inside after the first bend; the 1500 metres uses a waterfall start with free positioning.

Techniques

Even pacing, positioning in the pack, surging without wasting energy, and a timed final kick are the key skills.

Training

Sessions mix race-pace repetitions, tempo runs, speed work, and longer endurance runs.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 800
TIME_S = 110.0
print("800 metres pace example")
print("Distance: %d m, time: %.1f seconds" % (DISTANCE_M, TIME_S))
print("Pace per 400 m lap: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 2.0))
print("Pace per 100 m: %.2f seconds" % (TIME_S / 8.0))
print("Average speed: %.2f m/s" % (DISTANCE_M / TIME_S))

Long-Distance Running Rank 3 · simple · running · intermediate

Long-distance running covers the 5000 and 10000 metres on the track and the road and cross-country races beyond them, and it rewards aerobic endurance, efficient running form, and patience. Track races over 5000 and 10000 metres combine long periods of even pacing with surges and a fast finish, while road and cross-country races add hills, surfaces, and weather to the challenge. Training is built on mileage: regular easy runs, longer weekend runs, tempo runs at a strong steady effort, and interval sessions that sharpen speed. The best long-distance runners look relaxed at speeds that would exhaust most people, because years of training have made their stride economical and their aerobic engine enormous.

History

Long-distance track racing has been part of the Olympic programme since the early modern Games, with road and cross-country racing growing alongside it.

Rules

Track races use a waterfall start and free positioning; lapped runners keep to the outside when being passed.

Techniques

Efficient stride, even pacing, relaxed upper body, and surge timing are the key technical elements.

Training

Weekly mileage, long runs, tempo runs, and intervals build aerobic capacity and race sharpness.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 5000
TIME_S = 840.0
print("5000 metres pace example")
print("Distance: %d m, time: %.1f seconds (14:00)" % (DISTANCE_M, TIME_S))
print("Pace per 1000 m: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 5.0))
print("Pace per 400 m lap: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 12.5))

Hurdles Rank 4 · simple · running · intermediate

Hurdle races combine sprinting with clearing barriers: the 110 metres hurdles for men and 100 metres hurdles for women, and the 400 metres hurdles for both, with ten hurdles in every race. Hurdles are designed to fall forward when struck, so clipping one costs time but does not stop a race, while deliberately knocking hurdles down or running around them is penalised. Technique is precise: sprinters take a set number of strides between hurdles, lead with one leg, snap the trail leg through quickly, and keep the upper body low so speed is barely interrupted. The 400 metres hurdles adds endurance and stride-pattern management, because fatigue changes stride length and runners must adjust their pattern mid-race.

History

Hurdling developed from nineteenth-century obstacle races into the Olympic sprint and long hurdle events.

Rules

Ten hurdles per race. Hurdles fall forward when struck; deliberately knocking them down or bypassing them is penalised.

Techniques

Stride patterns between hurdles, lead-leg action, trail-leg snap, and low clearance keep speed through each barrier.

Training

Sessions mix hurdle drills, sprint work, rhythm runs over hurdles, and flexibility and strength work.

Working demo program

HURDLES = 10
DISTANCE_M = 110
print("Sprint hurdles example")
print("Distance: %d m, hurdles: %d" % (DISTANCE_M, HURDLES))
print("Hurdles per 10 m: %.2f" % (HURDLES / (DISTANCE_M / 10.0)))
print("Stride pattern example: 3 strides between hurdles")
print("Total strides between hurdles: %d" % (3 * (HURDLES - 1)))

Steeplechase Rank 5 · simple · running · advanced

The steeplechase is a distance race with barriers and a water jump: 3000 metres over 28 barriers and seven water jumps, run on the track with the water pit on one bend. Runners hurdle solid barriers that do not fall, unlike sprint hurdles, and land in or beyond a water pit after one barrier on each lap, so the race demands hurdling skill, water-jump technique, and distance fitness together. The water jump is the signature obstacle: runners step on the barrier and drive forward to land as far past the water as possible, because landing in deep water costs time and energy. Steeplechase rewards runners who stay relaxed over barriers, keep their rhythm, and save a kick for the final lap after the last water jump.

History

Steeplechase grew from cross-country races between church steeples, giving the event its name, into a standardised 3000 metres track event.

Rules

Runners clear 28 barriers and seven water jumps over 3000 metres. Barriers are solid; runners may step on them.

Techniques

Barrier clearance, water-jump drive and landing, and rhythm maintenance between obstacles are the core skills.

Training

Sessions mix distance running, hurdle drills, water-jump practice, and race-pace repetitions.

Working demo program

BARRIERS = 28
WATER_JUMPS = 7
LAPS = 7.5
print("Steeplechase obstacles")
print("Distance: 3000 m, laps: %.1f" % LAPS)
print("Barriers: %d, water jumps: %d" % (BARRIERS, WATER_JUMPS))
print("Total obstacles: %d" % (BARRIERS + WATER_JUMPS))
print("Barriers per lap: %.1f" % (BARRIERS / LAPS))

Relays Rank 6 · compounded · running · intermediate

Relays are team races in which four runners each run a leg and pass a baton within a marked exchange zone, and the most famous are the 4x100 metres and 4x400 metres. The 4x100 metres is a sprint relay in which exchanges happen at full speed, with the outgoing runner starting before the incoming runner arrives and taking the baton without looking back, while the 4x400 metres allows more time but demands that tired runners still run clean exchanges. A dropped baton, an exchange outside the zone, or running out of lane can disqualify a team, so relay practice matters as much as individual speed. The fastest teams are not always the four fastest individuals; they are the four who exchange best.

History

Relay racing has been part of the Olympic programme since the early twentieth century, with the 4x100 and 4x400 metres as the classic events.

Rules

Four runners per team; the baton must be exchanged inside the zone, and lane and impeding rules apply.

Techniques

Blind exchanges at speed, exchange-zone timing, and running order strategy are the defining skills.

Training

Teams drill exchanges repeatedly, practise running order, and rehearse under race pressure.

Working demo program

LEGS = [10.2, 9.8, 10.0, 9.9]
print("4x100 metres relay example")
for i, t in enumerate(LEGS, 1):
    print("Leg %d: %.1f seconds" % (i, t))
print("Total: %.1f seconds" % sum(LEGS))
print("Average leg: %.2f seconds" % (sum(LEGS) / len(LEGS)))

Marathon Rank 7 · compounded · running · advanced

The marathon is a road race of 42.195 kilometres, inspired by the legend of the Greek messenger who ran from Marathon to Athens, and it is the longest running event on the Olympic programme. Marathon racing demands extraordinary endurance, careful pacing, and fuel management: runners take on fluids and carbohydrates during the race, because the body's stored fuel runs low after about 30 kilometres, and the final kilometres test whoever paced best. Elite men run under two hours and ten minutes and elite women under two hours and twenty minutes on fast courses, while mass races welcome tens of thousands of runners of all abilities. Training builds over months, with long runs, tempo work, and recovery, and the race rewards patience above all: start too fast and the last ten kilometres become a struggle.

History

The marathon recalls the legend of the run from Marathon to Athens and joined the first modern Olympic Games in 1896; the distance was standardised at 42.195 kilometres.

Rules

Road courses are measured and certified; mass starts are used, with timing mats recording each runner.

Techniques

Even pacing, fuelling on the run, and relaxed form under fatigue are the core skills.

Training

Months of mileage, long runs up to and beyond 30 kilometres, tempo runs, and recovery weeks build marathon fitness.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_KM = 42.195
TIME_MIN = 150
print("Marathon pace example")
print("Distance: %.3f km, time: %d minutes (2:30)" % (DISTANCE_KM, TIME_MIN))
print("Pace per kilometre: %.2f minutes" % (TIME_MIN / DISTANCE_KM))
print("Pace per kilometre: %.0f seconds" % (TIME_MIN * 60 / DISTANCE_KM))
print("Halfway split: %.1f minutes" % (TIME_MIN / 2.0))

Race Walking Rank 8 · compounded · running · intermediate

Race walking is a judged athletics discipline in which athletes must keep one foot on the ground at all times and keep the supporting leg straight from contact until it passes under the body, with judges watching for violations and issuing warnings and disqualifications. Races are held over 20 kilometres and, in newer formats, marathon-distance relay events, and walkers move at speeds that surprise first-time watchers, with elite walkers covering 20 kilometres in well under 90 minutes. Technique is everything: hip rotation, a straight leg, and rapid foot turnover produce speed without breaking into a run. Training mixes long walks, technique drills, and strength work, and races reward discipline, because a walker who loses form under fatigue collects warnings fast.

History

Race walking grew from nineteenth-century pedestrian contests into an Olympic discipline with 20 kilometre races and relay formats.

Rules

One foot must contact the ground at all times; the supporting leg must be straight from contact until vertical. Judges issue warnings and disqualifications.

Techniques

Hip rotation, straight-leg action, and fast foot turnover produce speed within the rules.

Training

Long walks, technique drills, and strength work build the specific fitness and form the event demands.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_KM = 20
TIME_MIN = 85
print("20 km race walk pace example")
print("Distance: %d km, time: %d minutes" % (DISTANCE_KM, TIME_MIN))
print("Pace per kilometre: %.2f minutes" % (TIME_MIN / DISTANCE_KM))
print("Average speed: %.2f km/h" % (DISTANCE_KM / (TIME_MIN / 60.0)))

Long Jump Rank 9 · compounded · jumping · intermediate

The long jump is a field event in which athletes sprint down a runway, take off from a board, and land in a sand pit, with the distance measured from the take-off board to the nearest mark in the sand. Jumpers build speed on the runway, hit the board with a precise final stride, and use a hang or hitch-kick technique in the air to keep balance and prepare for landing, then land feet-first and fall forward to protect the distance. Fouls happen when a jumper crosses the take-off board or runs past it, and competitions give each athlete several attempts, with the best legal jump counting. The event rewards speed more than any other jump: the fastest sprinter-jumpers usually jump furthest, provided their take-off is accurate.

History

The long jump has been part of athletics since ancient competitions and the first modern Olympic Games in 1896.

Rules

Take-off must be from behind the board; distance is measured to the nearest sand mark; fouls do not count.

Techniques

Runway speed, board accuracy, in-air technique, and forward landing are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix sprinting, take-off drills, bounding, plyometrics, and gym strength work.

Working demo program

RUNWAY_M = 40
SPEED_MS = 10.0
print("Long jump approach example")
print("Runway: %d m, approach speed: %.1f m/s" % (RUNWAY_M, SPEED_MS))
print("Approach time: %.1f seconds" % (RUNWAY_M / SPEED_MS))
print("Jumps example: 6 attempts, best counts")

Triple Jump Rank 10 · compounded · jumping · advanced

The triple jump, once called the hop, step, and jump, is a field event in which athletes take off from a board and complete three phases before landing in the sand: a hop on the take-off leg, a step onto the other leg, and a jump into the pit. The phases must flow together without losing speed, and the best jumpers balance the three phases so no single phase dominates or collapses. Like the long jump, distance is measured from the board to the nearest sand mark, and fouls come from crossing the board. Triple jump demands speed, strength, and rhythm, and it punishes athletes who overreach on the hop, because a hop that is too long destroys the step and the jump that follow.

History

The triple jump has been an Olympic event since the first modern Games in 1896, evolving from the hop, step, and jump.

Rules

Take-off from behind the board; hop, step, then jump in sequence; distance measured to the nearest sand mark.

Techniques

Phase balance, rhythm, speed maintenance, and a forward landing are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix sprinting, bounding, phase drills, plyometrics, and strength work.

Working demo program

PHASES = [6.0, 4.8, 5.2]
print("Triple jump phases example (metres)")
print("Hop: %.1f, step: %.1f, jump: %.1f" % tuple(PHASES))
print("Total: %.1f m" % sum(PHASES))
print("Phase shares: %.0f%%, %.0f%%, %.0f%%" % tuple(p / sum(PHASES) * 100.0 for p in PHASES))

High Jump Rank 11 · complex · jumping · intermediate

The high jump is a field event in which athletes run a curved approach, take off from one foot, and clear a horizontal bar without knocking it down, landing on a large mat. The modern technique is the Fosbury flop, named for Dick Fosbury, who won Olympic gold with it in 1968: jumpers arch their backs over the bar head-first and shoulders-first, which lets the body's centre of mass pass under the bar while the body passes over it. Competitions raise the bar in stages, athletes may pass heights, and each athlete gets three attempts per height, with elimination after three consecutive failures. The event rewards speed on the curve, a powerful take-off, and a flexible, well-timed arch over the bar.

History

High jumping became a modern Olympic event in 1896; the Fosbury flop, introduced at the 1968 Games, became the dominant technique.

Rules

The bar rises in stages; three attempts per height; three consecutive failures eliminate the athlete; knocking the bar down fails the attempt.

Techniques

Curved approach, single-foot take-off, back arch over the bar, and a safe landing on the mat are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix sprinting on a curve, take-off drills, back-arch flexibility, and gym strength work.

Working demo program

HEIGHTS = [1.90, 1.95, 2.00, 2.05]
print("High jump bar progression example (metres)")
for h in HEIGHTS:
    print("Bar: %.2f m" % h)
print("Total rise: %.2f m" % (HEIGHTS[-1] - HEIGHTS[0]))
print("Attempts allowed per height: 3")

Pole Vault Rank 12 · complex · jumping · advanced

The pole vault is a field event in which athletes sprint down a runway carrying a long flexible pole, plant it in a box, and use its bend and recoil to rise over a bar set several metres high, landing on a large mat. Modern poles are made of fibreglass and carbon fibre, and vaulters choose pole length and stiffness to match their speed and weight, because the pole must bend enough to store energy without breaking rhythm. Technique runs through the plant, the swing, the rock-back, the turn, and the push-off over the bar, and competitions raise the bar in stages with three attempts per height. Pole vault is the most technical and equipment-dependent jump, and it rewards speed, strength, courage, and precise timing in equal measure.

History

Pole vaulting grew from practical pole use for crossing obstacles into an Olympic event, with fibreglass poles transforming it in the twentieth century.

Rules

The bar rises in stages; three attempts per height; knocking the bar down or breaking the pole rules fails the attempt.

Techniques

Plant, swing, rock-back, turn, and push-off form the vault sequence; pole selection matches speed and weight.

Training

Sessions mix sprinting, pole drills, gymnastics strength, and short-approach vaulting.

Working demo program

POLE_M = 4.90
GRIP_M = 4.60
print("Pole vault example")
print("Pole length: %.2f m, grip height: %.2f m" % (POLE_M, GRIP_M))
print("Grip below pole tip: %.2f m" % (POLE_M - GRIP_M))
print("Bar heights example: 5.00, 5.20, 5.40 m")

Shot Put Rank 13 · complex · throwing · intermediate

The shot put is a throwing event in which athletes push a heavy metal ball, the shot, from the shoulder with one hand, from inside a circle, and the distance is measured from the circle to the nearest landing mark. Men throw a 7.26 kilogram shot and women a 4 kilogram shot, and athletes use either the glide technique, sliding back across the circle, or the rotational technique, spinning like a discus thrower before release. The shot must stay in contact with the neck and shoulder until release, and stepping outside the circle or over the stop board fouls the throw. Competitions give several attempts, with the best legal throw counting, and the event rewards explosive leg and hip power transferred through a braced upper body.

History

Shot putting grew from stone-putting contests into a standardised Olympic event with fixed weights and circle rules.

Rules

The shot stays at the neck until release; the athlete must not leave the circle or cross the stop board; throws are measured to the nearest mark.

Techniques

Glide and rotational techniques both build speed across the circle into a braced, explosive release.

Training

Sessions mix throws, Olympic lifting, plyometrics, and core strength work.

Working demo program

print("Men: %.2f kg, women: 4.00 kg" % WEIGHT_KG)
print("Difference: %.2f kg" % (WEIGHT_KG - 4.0))
print("Throws example: 6 attempts, best counts")

Discus Throw Rank 14 · complex · throwing · intermediate

The discus throw is a field event in which athletes spin inside a circle and release a disc, with men throwing a 2 kilogram discus and women a 1 kilogram discus, and the distance measured to the nearest landing mark inside a marked sector. The throw builds speed through one and a half rotations across the circle, with the discus held flat so it flies with aerodynamic lift, and the release angle and spin decide how far it carries. Throwing takes place inside a safety cage, because a misthrown discus travels fast and far, and stepping outside the circle or landing outside the sector fouls the throw. Competitions give several attempts, and the event rewards rhythm, balance through the spin, and a clean release.

History

Discus throwing dates to ancient Greek competitions and the first modern Olympic Games in 1896.

Rules

Throws must land inside the sector; the athlete must stay inside the circle; the discus is 2 kilograms for men and 1 kilogram for women.

Techniques

One and a half rotations, a flat discus for lift, and a clean release angle are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix throws, spins, balance drills, and strength work.

Working demo program

for k, v in WEIGHTS.items():
    print("%s: %.1f kg" % (k, v))
print("Sector angle example: about 35 degrees")

Hammer Throw Rank 15 · complex · throwing · advanced

The hammer throw is a field event in which athletes spin inside a circle and release a hammer: a metal ball on a wire with a handle, weighing 7.26 kilograms for men and 4 kilograms for women, with the distance measured to the nearest landing mark inside a sector. Throwers build speed through three or four rotations, with the hammer orbiting around them and pulling hard on their arms and core, then release it at the right moment and angle. The event takes place inside a safety cage, and stepping outside the circle or landing outside the sector fouls the throw. Hammer throwing rewards balance, core strength, and the courage to stay relaxed while a heavy ball pulls the thrower around at high speed.

History

Hammer throwing grew from Irish and Scottish heavy events into a standardised Olympic event with a wire-handled hammer.

Rules

Throws must land inside the sector; the athlete must stay inside the circle; weights are 7.26 kilograms for men and 4 kilograms for women.

Techniques

Three or four rotations, balance against the hammer's pull, and a timed release are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix throws, turns, core strength, and balance work.

print("Rotations: %d" % ROTATIONS)
print("Weights: men 7.26 kg, women 4.00 kg")

Javelin Throw Rank 16 · complex · throwing · advanced

The javelin throw is a field event in which athletes run down a runway and throw a spear-like implement, with men throwing an 800 gram javelin and women a 600 gram javelin, and the distance measured to the nearest landing mark inside a sector. Throwers build speed on the runway, move into a cross-step and withdrawal, and release the javelin over the shoulder at an angle that lets it fly point-first and land tip-first, because a flat or tail-first landing does not count. The runway ends in a foul line that throwers must not cross, and competitions give several attempts with the best legal throw counting. Javelin rewards the blend of sprint speed, throwing power, and a clean release, and it is the only throwing event run from a runway rather than a circle.

History

Javelin throwing dates to ancient spear competitions and the first modern Olympic Games, with the modern implement standardised in the twentieth century.

Rules

The javelin must land tip-first inside the sector; throwers must not cross the foul line; weights are 800 grams for men and 600 grams for women.

Techniques

Run-up speed, cross-step, withdrawal, and a clean over-shoulder release are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix throws, sprinting, throwing drills, and shoulder and core strength work.

Working demo program
