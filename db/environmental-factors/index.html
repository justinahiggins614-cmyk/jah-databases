JAH-R20-ENVF-000001 — Temperature — full range

FACTOR: Temperature UNIT: kelvin (K) RANGE: 0 K (absolute zero, unattainable) to 5.5 x 10^12 K (LHC heavy-ion collisions); cosmic range 2.725 K (CMB) to 1.57 x 10^7 K (Sun's core) to 10^10 K (supernova cores)

Temperature sets which physics is possible. The factor's full range across the lane: the coldest sustained macroscopic temperature in the collider world is the LHC magnets' 1.9 K superfluid helium — colder than deep space (2.725 K cosmic microwave background). Laboratory ultracold atoms reach picokelvin, noted for completeness. Room temperature, 293 K, is where humans and most instruments live. The Sun's surface is 5,772 K, its core 15.7 million K (fusion regime). Heavy-ion collisions at the LHC and RHIC reach trillions of kelvin — 5.5 x 10^12 K — recreating the quark-gluon plasma of the first microseconds. Supernova cores exceed 10^10 K.

Why it matters for colliders: superconducting magnets only work below their critical temperature (niobium-titanium at 1.9-4.2 K); detectors' thermal noise falls with cooling; and the QGP program is entirely a temperature program — heating nuclear matter until it melts. The database records each machine's operating temperature against this range.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000002 — Pressure and vacuum — full range

FACTOR: Pressure / vacuum UNIT: pascal (Pa); beam vacuum often in mbar RANGE: 10^-12 mbar (best laboratory ultrahigh vacuum) to 10^11 Pa (Earth's core) to 10^16 Pa (neutron-star interior); LHC beam pipe ~10^-10 mbar

Pressure — or its absence — is a collider's working fluid condition. The LHC beam pipe holds ~10^-10 mbar (10^-8 Pa): so empty that a proton can circle 11,245 times per second for hours with little chance of hitting a stray molecule. The best laboratory vacuums reach ~10^-12 mbar. Interplanetary space is ~10^-6 Pa; the sparsest intergalactic voids approach 10^-16 Pa — a better vacuum than any laboratory, which is why some propose space-based instruments.

Going up: sea-level air is 101,325 Pa; the Mariana Trench is ~1.1 x 10^8 Pa; Earth's core reaches ~3.6 x 10^11 Pa; Jupiter's core ~10^12 Pa; a neutron star's interior exceeds 10^16 Pa, where matter becomes neutron-degenerate. Diamond-anvil cells in laboratories reach ~10^11 Pa, recreating planetary-core conditions on a tabletop.

Why it matters: beam lifetime depends on residual gas scattering; outgassing limits how fast a machine can be pumped down; and at the high end, pressure creates the phases colliders study — metallic hydrogen in gas giants, quark matter in neutron stars. Each machine's vacuum spec is recorded against this range.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000003 — Magnetic field — full range

FACTOR: Magnetic field strength UNIT: tesla (T) RANGE: 10^-12 T (intergalactic fields) to 10^11 T (magnetars); human exposure: Earth's 25-65 uT; LHC dipoles 8.33 T; strongest lab pulsed fields ~100 T

Magnetic fields steer every circular collider. The range: intergalactic space carries ~10^-12 T; the interstellar medium ~10^-10 T; Earth's surface field is 25-65 µT (the compass regime); a refrigerator magnet is ~5 mT; an MRI scanner runs 1.5-7 T; the LHC's 1,232 dipoles each sustain 8.33 T across a 15-metre bore — about 100,000 times Earth's field — to bend 6.8 TeV protons around the ring. The strongest continuous lab fields reach ~45 T; pulsed magnets briefly exceed 100 T. Jupiter's field is ~4.2 gauss (0.42 mT) at the cloud tops but enormous in extent; the Sun's sunspots reach ~0.3 T. Magnetars — the collapsed remnants — carry 10^9 to 10^11 T, the strongest fields known, strong enough to distort atoms.

Why it matters: bending power (field x radius) sets a ring collider's maximum energy — the LHC's energy is really a statement about 8.33 T in a 27 km tunnel. Detector solenoids (CMS's 3.8 T, ATLAS's 2 T) set momentum resolution. The database records each magnet system's field against this range, and notes the public R&D push toward 16 T niobium-tin magnets for the FCC-hh.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000004 — Radiation — dose, flux, and shielding

FACTOR: Ionizing radiation UNIT: sievert (Sv) for dose; particles/cm^2/s for flux RANGE: 0.001 mSv/yr (deep underground labs) to 2.4 mSv/yr (global background average) to 10^4 Sv/hr (inside an operating reactor core); LHC detector lifetime doses ~10^16 neutrons/cm^2

Radiation is both the signal and the enemy. Natural background: the global average is ~2.4 mSv/year (UNSCEAR), ranging from ~1 to >10 mSv/year by geology; a chest CT is ~7 mSv; airline crews accumulate extra cosmic dose with altitude. Deep underground laboratories (Gran Sasso, SNOLAB) cut cosmic backgrounds by factors of millions — the environment particle hunters need. Space: the Van Allen belts trap protons and electrons at fluxes that kill unshielded electronics; a Mars transit delivers ~0.7 Sv to an astronaut (public NASA figures).

Collider radiation: the LHC's detectors absorb lifetime fluences of ~10^16 neutrons/cm^2 near the beam — enough to destroy ordinary silicon, which is why the experiments use radiation-hard sensors and plan replacements for the High-Luminosity era. Beam-loss monitors and collimators manage the machine's own spray. Activation makes accelerator components radioactive over time, handled under public radioprotection rules.

Why it matters: every detector in the lane is designed against this factor — shielding, sensor choice, and replacement schedules all flow from the expected dose. The database records each experiment's radiation environment and hardness measures against the range.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000005 — Gravity and acceleration — full range

FACTOR: Gravitational acceleration UNIT: m/s^2; also expressed in g (9.81 m/s^2) RANGE: 0 (deep-space free fall) to 274 m/s^2 (Sun's surface) to 10^12 m/s^2 (neutron-star surface); human centrifuges ~10 g; LHC protons: centripetal ~10^14 m/s^2 equivalent

Gravity is the collider at cosmic scale and a correction at laboratory scale. The range: deep-space free fall is effectively 0 g — the environment of LISA's test masses and of the WLCG's irrelevance to gravity. The Moon is 1.62 m/s^2, Mars 3.71, Earth 9.81, Jupiter 24.79, the Sun's surface 274. White dwarfs reach ~10^6 m/s^2; neutron-star surfaces ~10^12 m/s^2, where the escape velocity is a large fraction of light speed.

Accelerations in machines: the LHC's protons circling at near light speed undergo centripetal acceleration equivalent to ~10^14 m/s^2 — vastly more than any gravitational field in the solar system, but it is magnetic, not gravitational. Ultracentrifuges reach 10^6 g; human tolerance is ~5-10 g sustained. Tidal accelerations — the differences that disrupt — get their own treatment in the tidal scenarios: the Roche limit, spaghettification near black holes (public GR).

Why it matters: gravitational-wave detectors must isolate against seismic and Newtonian noise at 10^-19 m scales; drop towers and parabolic flights simulate 0 g for instrument tests; and every cosmic-collider scenario's dynamics are gravity's. The database records each facility's gravitational environment against the range.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000006 — Electromagnetic spectrum — the full dial

FACTOR: Electromagnetic frequency / wavelength UNIT: hertz (Hz); metres (m) RANGE: 10^0 Hz (power lines) to 10^20 Hz (gamma rays); radio to gamma across 20 orders of magnitude; CMB at 160 GHz; LHC-relevant: RF cavities at 400 MHz

Every collider both uses and produces the full electromagnetic spectrum. The range, 20+ orders of magnitude: power-line 50/60 Hz; radio telescopes down to kHz; the cosmic microwave background peaks at 160 GHz; the LHC's superconducting RF cavities run at 400 MHz, topping up the beams' energy each turn; visible light is the narrow 400-700 nm band; X-rays (10^16-10^19 Hz) image crystals and stars; gamma rays above 10^19 Hz include annihilation's 511 keV photons (1.2 x 10^20 Hz) and the LHC's Higgs-to-two-photon signal.

Why it matters per machine: synchrotron light sources are deliberately built to harvest the radiation rings waste — the ESRF, APS, and SPring-8 turn GeV electrons into brilliant X-ray beams for biology and materials. Detectors are spectrum instruments: calorimeters measure photon and electron energies, Cherenkov detectors clock particle speeds via emitted light, and radio antennas (like those proposed for neutrino detection in ice) listen at the low end. The database records each facility's spectral operating points — what it uses, what it emits, what it detects — against the full dial.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000007 — Matter density — from void to neutron star

FACTOR: Mass density UNIT: kg/m^3 RANGE: 10^-27 kg/m^3 (intergalactic medium) to 10^18 kg/m^3 (neutron-star core); air 1.2; water 1000; LHC beam: ~10^11 protons in 16-micron bunches

Density determines what a collision hits. The range: the intergalactic medium is ~10^-27 kg/m^3 (about one atom per cubic metre); interstellar clouds ~10^-18; the best lab vacuums ~10^-14; air at sea level 1.2; water 1,000; Earth's core ~13,000; the Sun's core 150,000; white-dwarf matter 10^9; neutron-star cores 10^17-10^18 — a teaspoon weighing a billion tonnes.

Collider densities: an LHC bunch packs ~10^11 protons into a needle 16 micrometres wide at the collision point — a local density far above solid matter, but only for an instant and only electromagnetically interacting. Heavy-ion collisions compress nuclear matter to several times normal nuclear density (~2.3 x 10^17 kg/m^3), the regime where the QGP forms and where neutron-star cores live — the public connection between the laboratory and the stars.

Why it matters: target density sets fixed-target luminosity; beam-beam density sets collider luminosity; and shielding design is density engineering (lead, water, concrete per MeV). Each machine's density regime is recorded against the range.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000008 — Time scales — from attoseconds to gigayears

FACTOR: Time / lifetime / period UNIT: seconds (s) RANGE: 10^-25 s (W/Z/top lifetimes) to 10^-22 s (Higgs) to 13.8 x 10^9 yr (universe age); LHC bunch spacing 25 ns; Moon recession 3.8 cm/yr

Colliders span more time scales than any other human enterprise. The short end: the W, Z, and top quark live ~10^-25 s; the Higgs ~10^-22 s — they decay before traveling a proton's width, so detectors never see them, only their debris. B mesons live ~1.5 ps, flying millimetres at LHC energies (LHCb's vertex detectors resolve this). The LHC's bunches cross every 25 ns; its magnets ramp over minutes; a fill lasts hours; runs last years; the program spans decades.

The long end: the proton's lifetime exceeds 10^34 years (public lower bound — no decay ever seen); the universe is 13.8 billion years old; the Milky Way-Andromeda merger is due in ~4.5 billion; the Moon recedes 3.8 cm per year. Geological clocks (U-238's 4.5-billion-year half-life) bridge human and cosmic time.

Why it matters: detector timing resolution (tens of picoseconds in modern TOF systems) decides what can be distinguished; trigger latencies are budgeted in nanoseconds; and data preservation spans decades (CERN Open Data). Each record's characteristic times are listed against the range — the database's way of showing that a collider is a time machine in both directions.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000009 — Particle flux — from rare to relentless

FACTOR: Particle flux UNIT: particles per cm^2 per second RANGE: 10^-6 /cm^2/s (deep-underground dark-matter labs) to 10^9 collisions/s (LHC interaction points); cosmic-ray sea-level muons ~1/cm^2/min

Flux — how many particles arrive per area per time — decides whether an experiment waits or drowns. The quiet end: deep-underground dark-matter detectors see backgrounds of ~10^-6 events/cm^2/s after kilometres of rock shielding; neutrino observatories wait years for a handful of events. Sea-level cosmic-ray muons arrive at ~1 per cm^2 per minute — the constant rain every surface detector lives in. The Sun's neutrino flux at Earth is ~6 x 10^10 per cm^2 per second, passing through everything unnoticed.

The loud end: the LHC's interaction points see ~10^9 collisions per second at full luminosity; the trigger system discards all but ~1,000 per second — a billion-to-one selection, the most extreme data triage in science. Synchrotron beamlines deliver 10^12-10^15 photons per second to samples. Solar flares can raise satellite-orbit fluxes by orders of magnitude in hours (space-weather alerts are public).

Why it matters: flux sets shielding, trigger design, sensor occupancy, and data rates — the entire experimental method is flux management. Each facility's flux regime is recorded against the range.

JAH-R20-ENVF-000010 — Composite environments — putting factors together

FACTOR: Multiple factors jointly UNIT: varies RANGE: LHC interaction point to interstellar medium to neutron-star merger (see body)

No factor acts alone. This record harvests the lane's signature composite environments — each a point in the multi-dimensional factor space. The LHC interaction point: 1.9 K magnets, 10^-10 mbar vacuum, 8.33 T fields, 10^9 collisions/s, in a tunnel under 100 m of rock shielding from cosmic rays. A heavy-ion collision fireball: 5.5 x 10^12 K, several times nuclear density, living 10^-23 s. The interstellar medium: 10-100 K, 10^-18 kg/m^3, nanotesla fields, threaded by cosmic rays. A neutron-star merger: 10^12 m/s^2 gravity, 10^17 kg/m^3 density, up to 10^11 T fields, millisecond dynamics, gravitational-wave loud.

The database's method: each composite is tabulated as a factor vector, so that two environments can be compared factor by factor — which is how engineers port designs (what changes moving a detector from a test beam to the LHC?) and how scientists find analogies (heavy-ion fireball vs early universe: same temperature and density, different size and lifetime — the comparison the field actually makes, public).

The generator extends the table with new composites as the lane grows: every new machine or scenario gets its factor vector, keeping the environment index complete. That is the "full listing" promise: every factor, every range, every composite, cross-linked.
