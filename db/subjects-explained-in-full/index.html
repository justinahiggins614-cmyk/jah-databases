Science — What Science Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Science is a way of finding out how the world works by watching carefully, forming ideas that can be tested, and checking those ideas against evidence. It is both a body of knowledge, such as the laws of motion and the theory of evolution, and a method for gaining that knowledge. Science deals with the natural world: things that can be observed, measured, and tested. It does not settle questions of value or meaning, which belong to other kinds of inquiry. What makes a claim scientific is that it can be checked, challenged, and corrected when new evidence appears. Science also has a social side: it is done by people working together, sharing results, and checking one another's work, which is why trust in science rests on the openness of its methods as much as on any single result.

History: People have studied nature for thousands of years. In ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, priests and scribes recorded the movements of the stars and the flooding of rivers, and used that knowledge to build calendars. In ancient Greece, thinkers such as Thales and Aristotle tried to explain nature by reason rather than myth. During the Islamic Golden Age, from about the 8th to the 14th century, scholars in Baghdad, Cairo, and Cordoba preserved Greek learning and added to it; Ibn al-Haytham studied optics with controlled experiments around the year 1000. In Europe, the Scientific Revolution of the 1500s and 1600s changed everything: Copernicus placed the Sun at the center of the solar system in 1543, Galileo used the telescope to observe the moons of Jupiter, and Isaac Newton published his laws of motion and gravity in 1687. In the 1800s science became a profession, with laboratories, journals, and universities. In the 1900s it grew into large teams and big instruments, from particle accelerators to space telescopes, and produced the theories of relativity and quantum mechanics, the structure of DNA, and modern computing. In the 1800s, Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection, published in 1859, and the work of Pasteur on germs changed biology and medicine. In the 1900s, Marie Curie studied radioactivity, Albert Einstein reshaped physics, and the discovery of the structure of DNA in 1953 opened modern genetics. Science also became organised on a large scale: governments funded laboratories, and international teams built telescopes and particle accelerators together.

Key concepts: A hypothesis is a testable idea, a specific guess about how something works. A theory in science is not a guess; it is a well-tested explanation that fits a large body of evidence, such as the theory of evolution or the germ theory of disease. An experiment is a controlled test designed to check a hypothesis, changing one factor at a time while keeping the rest steady. Observation is careful watching and measuring, the raw material of science. Peer review means other experts check new work before it is published. Falsifiability, a term from the philosopher Karl Popper, means a scientific claim must be open to being proved wrong by evidence. Reproducibility means other researchers can repeat a result and get the same answer. A variable is a factor that can change, such as temperature or time, and a controlled experiment holds all variables steady except the one being tested. A control group is the group that does not receive the treatment, so its results can be compared with the group that does. A model is a simplified description, often mathematical, that predicts how a system will behave.

How it works: Science works as a cycle. It begins with an observation, something noticed in the world, such as a pattern in data or an unexpected result. The scientist forms a hypothesis, a testable explanation for what was observed. From the hypothesis, predictions are drawn: if the idea is right, then a particular measurement should come out a certain way. An experiment or further observation tests the prediction, and the results are measured and recorded. If the results match the prediction, the hypothesis gains support; if they do not, the hypothesis is revised or dropped. Results are shared through publications so other scientists can check, repeat, and build on them. Over time, hypotheses that survive many tests are gathered into theories, and theories that keep working become the framework for new questions. The cycle never truly ends, because each answer opens new questions. Statistics play a large part: scientists use them to judge whether a result is likely to be real or merely chance. A result is usually called significant when the chance of it happening by accident is small, often less than one in twenty, though the exact standard varies by field.

Worked examples: The germ theory of disease began with observations that people near contaminated water fell ill, and was tested by experiments showing that micro-organisms cause infection. Gravity was tested by measuring how objects fall and how planets move, and the measurements matched Newton's equations. In the 1920s, Alexander Fleming noticed that mould killed bacteria in a dish, an observation that led, through years of testing, to the antibiotic penicillin. Climate science tests its models by checking whether they can reproduce past temperatures and by measuring present warming against predictions made decades ago. The prediction of a solar eclipse shows science at its most exact: astronomers can say, years in advance, the day, the hour, and the place where the Moon's shadow will fall. The discovery of the planet Neptune in 1846, found by calculation before it was seen, is another famous case.

Real applications: Science underlies modern medicine, from vaccines to imaging machines. It powers technology: the physics of semiconductors made computers possible, and chemistry made new materials for aircraft and phones. Agriculture uses plant science and soil science to grow more food on less land. Science informs public decisions about health, safety, and the environment, such as how to respond to epidemics or manage water supplies. It also satisfies curiosity, answering questions about the origin of the universe, the history of life, and the workings of the human brain. Science also guides the response to natural disasters: seismology warns of earthquakes, meteorology tracks storms, and oceanography follows tsunamis.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is science the same as technology? No. Science seeks understanding, while technology applies knowledge to make tools; the two help each other, but they are different aims. Can science prove things with absolute certainty? In practice it provides the best-supported explanation available, always open to revision if better evidence appears. That is a strength, not a weakness. Is a theory just a guess? In everyday speech the word theory can mean a guess, but in science a theory is a tested explanation supported by much evidence. Does science have all the answers? No. Many questions remain open, and some questions, such as what is morally right, are not scientific questions at all. Why do scientists disagree? Because evidence can be incomplete, methods can differ, and new data can change the picture; disagreement is part of how science moves forward, not a sign that it has failed. Can science tell us what to do? It can tell us what will happen if we act in a certain way, but the choice of what to do also involves values, which science alone does not supply.

History — What History Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: History is the study of the past: what happened, when it happened, why it happened, and what it means. Historians work from evidence left behind, such as documents, letters, objects, buildings, and the memories of witnesses, and they use that evidence to build accounts of earlier times. History covers everything from the lives of ordinary people to the rise and fall of empires. It is not simply a list of dates; it is an attempt to explain change over time, and to understand how the present grew out of the past. History also includes the study of how the past is remembered: monuments, museums, and national holidays all show which pasts a society chooses to honour and which it chooses to forget.

History: The writing of history began in the ancient world. In Greece, Herodotus wrote about the wars between Greece and Persia in the 400s BCE, gathering stories from many sources, while Thucydides wrote a stricter account of the Peloponnesian War based on evidence and speeches he had checked. In China, Sima Qian wrote the Records of the Grand Historian around 100 BCE, a history spanning more than two thousand years. In the 1300s, the North African scholar Ibn Khaldun wrote about the rise and fall of dynasties and the forces that drive them. In the 1800s, the German historian Leopold von Ranke urged historians to show the past as it actually was, working from original documents in archives. In the 1900s, the Annales school in France widened history to include economics, geography, and everyday life, and later historians added the study of women, workers, enslaved people, and colonised peoples whose stories had been left out. Today history also uses archaeology, statistics, and digital archives. In the 1900s, history changed again. After the Second World War, historians began to study the lives of ordinary people, using records such as tax lists, letters, and oral interviews, a movement sometimes called history from below. The study of empires, slavery, and colonisation grew, and historians debated how to write about violence and injustice without taking sides. The digital age brought new tools: scanned archives, searchable newspapers, and databases of millions of records, which let historians ask questions that were impossible before.

Key concepts: A primary source is evidence from the time itself, such as a letter, a law, or a photograph. A secondary source is a later work that interprets primary sources, such as a history book. Chronology is the order of events in time, the backbone of every historical account. Causation is the study of why things happened, including causes that were immediate, such as a battle, and causes that were deep, such as a failing harvest. Historiography is the study of how history itself has been written, and how different historians have disagreed. Bias means a source or an account favours one side, and historians learn to spot it rather than pretend it is absent. Context means judging people of the past by the conditions of their own time, not only by the standards of today. An archive is a place where original documents are kept, from government papers to family letters, and it is the historian's main workshop. Periodisation is the dividing of the past into periods, such as the Middle Ages or the Industrial Revolution, which is a choice made by historians, not a fact of nature. Continuity and change are the two poles of historical thinking: what stayed the same, and what changed, and why.

How it works: A historian begins with a question, for example why a revolution happened or how a city changed. The next step is to gather sources: documents in archives, published records, objects, and sometimes interviews. Each source is examined for authenticity and reliability: who made it, when, why, and what they could have known. Sources are then compared, because one account alone can mislead, and the historian looks for agreement and contradiction. From the evidence, the historian builds an argument, an explanation supported by citations, and writes it as a narrative or analysis. Other historians read the work, check the sources, and may offer rival explanations. Over time, the best-supported accounts win acceptance, until new evidence or a new question reopens the debate. A historian must also judge the silence of the sources: some people left no records, and their absence must be noticed rather than ignored. Footnotes and citations are the historian's way of showing the evidence, so that any reader can check the argument against the sources.

Worked examples: The fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE is explained not by one cause but by many: invasions, civil wars, economic strain, and the division of the empire. The printing press, invented in Europe around 1450, spread books so widely that literacy, science, and religious debate all changed within a century. The French Revolution of 1789 began with a debt crisis and a bad harvest, and ended with the overthrow of the monarchy and a new idea of citizenship. The history of the steam engine shows how a technical invention, trade, coal, and labour combined to change whole societies. The history of the Black Death, which reached Europe in 1347, shows how a disease could kill a third of a population and change wages, farming, and religion in a single generation. The history of the printing press shows how one invention could spread new ideas faster than any ruler could control them.

Real applications: History informs public decisions: leaders who know how past crises were handled make better choices in new ones. It supports citizenship, helping people understand their laws, institutions, and rights. It preserves memory, keeping alive the stories of communities and families. Museums, archives, and heritage sites use history to explain the world to visitors. History also trains a practical skill: judging evidence, spotting weak claims, and weighing competing explanations, which is useful in law, journalism, business, and everyday life. History is also used in the law: courts look at the history of a law to understand what it was meant to do. It is used in medicine, where the history of a disease helps doctors understand how it spreads.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is history just a list of facts? No. Facts are the material, but history is the explanation built from them, and historians can disagree while using the same facts. Can history repeat itself? Events never repeat exactly, but patterns recur, such as the way debt crises or epidemics have unfolded more than once. Is history written by the winners? Often the records that survive come from the powerful, which is why modern historians work hard to recover the voices of ordinary people. Can we trust old sources? Only after checking them: a source can be honest, mistaken, or deliberately misleading, and the historian's job is to tell the difference. Is history useful if it cannot predict the future? Its defenders say it teaches judgement, which is more useful than prediction: a person who knows how past crises unfolded is better prepared for the next one. Can history be objective? Historians aim for fairness, but they also admit that every account is written from a point of view, and that the best defence is to show the evidence openly.

Mathematics — What Mathematics Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Mathematics is the study of number, shape, pattern, and change, worked out by exact reasoning. It begins with simple ideas, such as counting and measuring, and builds them into systems of rules that can be proved true. A mathematical result is not an opinion: once it is proved, it holds for everyone, everywhere. Mathematics is both a practical tool, used to build bridges, balance accounts, and guide spacecraft, and a subject studied for its own beauty, because its patterns are deep and surprising. Mathematics also has a language of its own, made of symbols such as plus, equals, and pi, which lets mathematicians write complex ideas in a few marks and share them across languages.

History: Mathematics began with counting and measuring. In ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, people used arithmetic and geometry to survey fields, build temples, and track the calendar, and Babylonian scribes solved quadratic equations around 1800 BCE. In Greece, mathematicians such as Euclid, writing around 300 BCE, organised geometry into a system of definitions and proofs in his Elements, a book used for more than two thousand years. In India, mathematicians developed the decimal system and the idea of zero, which reached Europe through Islamic scholars; the word algebra comes from the Arabic al-jabr, the title of a book by al-Khwarizmi written around 830 CE. In the 1600s, Descartes joined algebra to geometry, and Newton and Leibniz invented calculus, the mathematics of change. In the 1800s and 1900s, mathematics expanded into new fields: statistics, set theory, topology, and the mathematics of computers. In 1994, Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem, a problem that had stood open for more than 350 years. In the 1900s, mathematics faced a crisis and a triumph. Kurt Godel showed in 1931 that no system of mathematics can prove all its own truths, a result that changed how mathematicians thought about their subject. At the same time, computers, built on mathematical logic, became the most powerful tools mathematics had ever had. In 1994, Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem, and in the 2000s, the Poincare conjecture, one of the great problems of topology, was solved by Grigori Perelman.

Key concepts: A number is an idea for counting or measuring; numbers include whole numbers, fractions, negative numbers, and irrational numbers such as pi. An axiom is a starting assumption accepted without proof, from which other truths are derived. A proof is a chain of reasoning that shows a statement must be true, given the axioms. A function is a rule that turns each input into exactly one output, such as the rule that doubles a number. A theorem is a statement that has been proved. Geometry is the study of shape, size, and space. Algebra is the study of symbols and the rules for moving them around. Statistics is the study of collecting and interpreting data. A set is a collection of things, such as the set of all even numbers. Probability is the study of chance, and it measures how likely an event is, on a scale from zero to one. A proof by contradiction assumes the opposite of what is to be proved and shows that it leads to nonsense, which is how Euclid proved that there are infinitely many prime numbers.

How it works: Mathematics works by starting from clear definitions and axioms, then drawing out their consequences by logic. A mathematician notices a pattern, for example that the sum of the first few odd numbers is always a square, and states it as a conjecture, a claim that seems true but is not yet proved. The next step is to find a proof, a step-by-step argument that leaves no gap. A proof may be direct, working forward from the axioms, or indirect, showing that the opposite of the claim leads to a contradiction. Once a result is proved, it becomes a tool for proving further results, so mathematics grows layer by layer. Checking is essential: a single counterexample destroys a claim, while a proof secures it forever. Mathematicians also work by analogy, seeing that two different problems have the same structure, so a solution to one gives a solution to the other. Collaboration is common: a proof may be the work of many people over many years, and it is checked by referees before it is published, just as in the sciences.

Worked examples: The sum of the first 100 whole numbers is 5050, which can be found by adding them one by one or by the formula n times n plus 1, divided by 2. Pythagoras' theorem says that in a right-angled triangle, the square of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides, which is why builders use a 3-4-5 triangle to check a right angle. Prime numbers, numbers divisible only by 1 and themselves, are used to keep internet messages secret. The number pi, about 3.14159, is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, and it appears in fields far from circles, including probability. The Fibonacci sequence, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and so on, in which each number is the sum of the two before it, appears in the spirals of sunflowers and pine cones. The idea of a limit, central to calculus, lets mathematicians handle quantities that grow without end, such as the sum of a series that goes on forever but adds up to a finite number.

Real applications: Engineers use mathematics to design bridges, aircraft, and electrical grids, and to test them before they are built. Doctors use statistics to judge whether a new treatment works. Banks use mathematics to price loans and manage risk. Computer graphics, weather forecasting, and space flight all depend on mathematical models. Even music and art use mathematics: rhythm is counted in fractions, and perspective in painting follows the rules of geometry. Without mathematics, modern life would stop. Mathematics is also used in voting systems, to decide how seats are shared out fairly, and in medicine, to model how a disease spreads through a population.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is mathematics invented or discovered? Mathematicians disagree: some say its truths exist independently and are found, others say they are built by human minds. Both sides agree the results are exact either way. Do you need to be fast at arithmetic to be good at mathematics? No. Arithmetic is a small part of the subject; the heart of mathematics is reasoning about patterns. Is mathematics only useful for science and engineering? No. It is also used in economics, linguistics, music, art, and the study of games. Can mathematics be wrong? A proof can contain an error, and errors are sometimes found years later, but a correct proof is final. Why is mathematics so useful in science? This is a puzzle that the physicist Eugene Wigner called the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics: the same equations that describe a falling apple also describe the orbit of the Moon. Is mathematics a language? It is like a language, with its own symbols and grammar, but it is also more than that, because its statements can be proved true or false.

Language — What Language Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Language is a system of sounds, signs, or written marks that people use to share meaning. Every human community has a language, and every language has a vocabulary, a set of words, and a grammar, a set of rules for combining them into sentences. Language lets people pass on knowledge, tell stories, give orders, ask questions, and express feelings. Humans also use language to think: much of our reasoning takes the form of words in our heads. About 7,000 languages are spoken in the world today, and about half the world's people speak one of the 20 largest. Language also changes over time: words gain new meanings, sounds shift, and grammar simplifies or grows more complex, so the English of a thousand years ago would be hard for a modern speaker to understand.

History: Spoken language is far older than writing, and its origins are not directly recorded; scholars estimate that fully developed speech existed tens of thousands of years ago, though the exact date is disputed. Writing appeared around 3400 BCE in Mesopotamia, where cuneiform signs pressed into clay recorded trade, and in Egypt, where hieroglyphs recorded royal deeds. Alphabets, in which each sign stands for a sound, spread from the Phoenicians to the Greeks around 800 BCE and then to Rome. In the 1800s, scholars discovered that languages such as Sanskrit, Latin, Greek, and English descend from a single ancestor, Proto-Indo-European, and the science of historical linguistics was born. In the 1900s, Ferdinand de Saussure studied language as a system, and Noam Chomsky argued that children are born with an ability to learn grammar. Today, linguists record endangered languages and study how language is processed in the brain. In the 1900s, linguistics grew into a science of its own. In 1957, Noam Chomsky argued that the ability to learn language is built into the human mind, a claim that sparked decades of debate. In the same century, the study of dying languages became urgent, as linguists raced to record languages spoken by only a few elders. The rise of computers brought a new field, computational linguistics, which teaches machines to translate, to recognise speech, and to generate text.

Key concepts: A phoneme is a unit of sound that changes meaning, such as the difference between the p and b in pat and bat. A morpheme is the smallest unit of meaning, such as the s that makes a word plural. Syntax is the set of rules for arranging words into sentences. Semantics is the study of meaning. Grammar is the whole system of rules of a language. A dialect is a variety of a language spoken in a region or by a social group; dialects are complete languages in their own right, not broken versions of a standard form. A language family is a group of languages descended from a common ancestor. A word is the smallest unit of language that can stand alone, though what counts as a word differs between languages. Pragmatics is the study of how meaning depends on context, such as when a speaker says it is cold in here and means please close the window. A language family, such as Indo-European, groups languages that share a common ancestor.

How it works: Language works because a community agrees, without ever voting on it, that certain sounds mean certain things. A child learns a language by listening, imitating, and being corrected, and by the age of five can produce sentences never heard before, following rules nobody taught explicitly. In conversation, a speaker chooses words and arranges them by the grammar, the listener decodes them, and both use context to fill in what is left unsaid. Meaning also depends on tone, gesture, and shared knowledge: the same sentence can be a question, a joke, or a command. Written language fixes speech in marks that can travel across time and distance, and reading reverses the process, turning marks back into meaning. Writing adds a new layer: a reader must decode marks into sounds and sounds into meaning, and different writing systems do this differently. An alphabet records sounds, a syllabary records syllables, and a system such as Chinese characters records meaning as well as sound. Learning to read takes years, and it changes the brain, which is why reading is one of the most studied skills in psychology.

Worked examples: English is a Germanic language with a large vocabulary borrowed from Latin and French; its word order is usually subject, verb, object, as in the cat sat. Mandarin Chinese uses tones, so the same syllable said with a different pitch can mean mother, hemp, horse, or scold. American Sign Language is a full language expressed by hands, face, and body, with its own grammar, and it is not signed English. The word set in English has hundreds of meanings, showing how much meaning depends on context. Place names often preserve older languages: London comes from a name used before the Romans arrived. The English word goodbye comes from God be with ye, shortened over centuries, which shows how language wears down with use. In Japanese, the same verb can be said in different levels of politeness, depending on who is speaking to whom, which shows how grammar carries social meaning.

Real applications: Language is the medium of law, government, education, and science: contracts, constitutions, textbooks, and research papers are all made of words. Translation lets people read books and use medicines whose instructions were written in another language. Language teaching opens jobs and travel. Speech technology, from voice assistants to translation software, is built on linguistics. The study of language also helps doctors diagnose speech disorders and helps historians trace how peoples moved and mixed in the past. Language is also the material of diplomacy: treaties must be translated with care, because a single word can change an agreement. In health care, clear language saves lives, when a doctor explains a treatment in words a patient can understand.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is one language better than another? No. Every language can express the full range of human thought; some have more words for a topic, such as snow or kinship, because their speakers need them. Do animals have language? Animals communicate, and some, such as bees and whales, have rich systems, but no animal system known so far has the open-ended grammar of human language. Is sign language a real language? Yes. Sign languages have grammar and vocabulary and are learned by children in the same way as spoken languages. Will English take over the world? English is widely used, but most people still grow up speaking another language, and many languages are gaining speakers. Do some languages have no grammar? No. Every language has rules, even if they are not written down, and a language without grammar could not be learned by children. Can a person think without language? People can think in images, in music, and in numbers, and some thoughts, such as the feeling of a colour, have no words at all, which shows that thought is wider than language.

Music — What Music Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Music is sound organised in time. It is made of notes, which are sounds of definite pitch, arranged into melodies, rhythms, and harmonies, and it is performed by voices and instruments. Music exists in every known human culture, from lullabies to symphonies, and it is used for celebration, worship, storytelling, work, and pleasure. Unlike speech, music does not need words to carry meaning: a melody can sound joyful or sorrowful without saying anything at all. Music is also a social act: people sing together in choirs, dance together at festivals, and listen together at concerts, and the shared experience is part of what music is for.

History: Music is ancient. Bone flutes more than 40,000 years old have been found in caves in Germany, and every early culture sang, drummed, and danced. In ancient Greece, music was studied as a branch of mathematics, because the ratios between notes follow simple numbers. In medieval Europe, monks developed musical notation, a way of writing music down so it could be repeated exactly; the modern staff of five lines took shape by the 1600s. In the 1700s and 1800s, composers such as Bach, Mozart, and Beethoven wrote symphonies, operas, and sonatas that are still performed today. In the 1900s, recording changed music forever: Thomas Edison's phonograph of 1877 let a performance be heard again and again, radio and records spread jazz, rock, and pop around the world, and later the digital file and streaming made almost all recorded music available anywhere. Rock and roll, from the 1950s, joined rhythm and blues with country music and became the sound of a generation. In the 2000s, digital tools let anyone record and share music from a bedroom, and streaming changed how music is sold and heard.

Key concepts: Pitch is how high or low a note sounds, set by the frequency of its vibration. Rhythm is the pattern of sounds in time, including the beat and the lengths of notes. Rhythm organises sounds in time, grouping beats into patterns such as three beats or four beats per measure. Melody is a sequence of notes that the ear follows as a tune. Harmony is the sound of several notes together, forming chords that can feel stable or tense. The ear itself is part of the system: the eardrum vibrates, tiny bones pass the vibration to the inner ear, and hair cells turn it into signals the brain reads as sound. The brain then groups the sounds into patterns, which is why a listener can follow a melody through many notes and hear a wrong note at once.

Does music need to be written down? No. Much of the world's music is passed on by ear, and the written score is only one way of preserving it.

Art — What Art Is

Definition: Art is the making of things that are valued for their form, their beauty, or the ideas and feelings they carry, rather than only for practical use. Painting, sculpture, drawing, photography, and architecture are all arts, and so are crafts such as weaving and pottery when they are made with skill and imagination. Art is found in every human culture, from cave paintings to modern installations. What counts as art has changed over time and differs between cultures, but everywhere it is a way of seeing the world and showing what is seen to others. Art is also a record of its time: a painting can show the clothes, the tools, and the beliefs of a society that has left no written history, which is why archaeologists study art as evidence.

History: Art is as old as modern humans. The earliest known paintings, made more than 40,000 years ago in caves in Indonesia and Europe, show animals and handprints. In ancient Egypt, art served religion and the state, with strict rules for how figures were drawn. In Greece, sculptors learned to carve the human body in lifelike form, and in Rome art spread across an empire. In the Middle Ages, art was mostly religious: icons, illuminated manuscripts, and cathedral sculpture. The Renaissance, from the 1300s to the 1600s, brought a new interest in nature and perspective, and artists such as Michelangelo and Raphael became famous in their own lifetimes. In the 1800s, the Impressionists, including Monet and Renoir, painted outdoors and tried to catch the changing light, breaking the rules of the academy. In the early 1900s, Picasso and Braque invented Cubism, showing objects from many angles at once, and later artists explored abstraction, the dream world of Surrealism, and the everyday objects of Pop Art. In the 2000s, art moved online, with digital images, video, and installations that invite the viewer to take part.

Style is the distinctive way an artist, period, or culture makes art, such as the style of the Renaissance or of Cubism. Perspective is a technique for showing three-dimensional space on a flat surface. Colour is made of hue, the name of the colour, value, how light or dark it is, and saturation, how intense it is. A genre is a type of subject, such as portrait, landscape, or still life. Light and shade, called chiaroscuro, model form on a flat surface, making a painted face look round. How it works: Art begins with a maker, an idea, and a medium, the material used, such as paint, stone, clay, or light. The artist arranges the elements of the medium, line, colour, shape, texture, and space, into a composition, the arrangement of parts within the work. The viewer then encounters the work, and meaning arises from the meeting of the two: the artist's choices and the viewer's experience, knowledge, and feelings. Some art aims to imitate the world, some to express emotion, some to explore form itself, and some to make a point about society. Because the viewer brings their own experience, the same work can mean different things to different people, and to the same person at different times. The making of art also involves the body: a painter's hand, a sculptor's chisel, a potter's wheel, and the skill of the hand is learned over years. The viewer, in turn, does not only look: the eye moves across a work, following lines and contrasts, and the brain builds a picture from those movements.

Worked examples: The Mona Lisa, painted by Leonardo da Vinci in the early 1500s, is famous for the sitter's expression and for Leonardo's technique of soft shading, called sfumato. The cave paintings of Lascaux in France, made about 17,000 years ago, show horses, bulls, and deer painted with mineral pigments on rock. A modern installation, such as a room filled with mirrors and lights, can be art even though it contains no paint or stone at all. The ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, painted by Michelangelo between 1508 and 1512, covers more than five hundred square metres and shows scenes from the Bible, including the famous image of God reaching out to Adam. The Terracotta Army, buried in China more than two thousand years ago, contains thousands of life-size clay soldiers, each with a different face.

Real applications: Art is displayed in museums and galleries, where millions of people visit it each year. It is used in public spaces, from statues in squares to murals on city walls, to mark identity and memory. Art is part of design: the look of books, websites, clothes, and cars all draws on artistic skill. Art therapy uses drawing and painting to help people express feelings that are hard to put into words. Art also preserves history: a painting can show how people dressed, worked, and worshipped centuries ago, in a way no written record can match. Art is also used in the design of money, stamps, and flags, where a small image must carry the identity of a whole country, and in the design of public buildings, where a mural or a sculpture can make a place feel welcoming.

Not quite: most definitions require intention, skill, or a shared context, and the question of what counts as art is itself debated by philosophers. Is a photograph art? It can be. A snapshot is a record, but a photograph made with attention to composition, light, and meaning is widely accepted as art. Do you need training to understand art? No. Looking carefully and asking what the artist did and why is enough to begin; training adds context. Is modern art a trick? Some works are jokes or provocations, but most are serious experiments with form and meaning, and they reward close looking. Is graffiti art? Some of it is, when it is made with skill and intention, and cities now commission murals from artists who began as graffiti writers. Does art need an audience? A work made in private is still art, but most art is made to be seen, and the audience completes it.

Law — What Law Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000007 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Law is a system of rules made and enforced by a community or a state, to order society, settle disputes, and protect people and property. Laws tell people what they must do, what they must not do, and what happens if they break the rules. Law is different from custom or morality: it is written down or formally recognised, and it is backed by institutions such as courts and police. Law covers crime, contracts, property, family, work, and government itself, and it applies to everyone within its jurisdiction, including, in a system based on the rule of law, those who govern. Law is also a record of values: the laws a society makes show what it cares about, from the protection of children to the ownership of land, and changes in law often mark changes in what a society believes.

History: Law is one of the oldest human inventions. The Code of Hammurabi, set up in Babylon around 1754 BCE, carved nearly 300 laws into stone, covering prices, wages, marriage, and punishments. In ancient Rome, law became a profession: Roman jurists wrote commentaries, and the emperor Justinian's codification of the 500s CE preserved Roman law for later Europe. In England, the common law grew from the decisions of royal courts after the Norman Conquest of 1066, and the principle that judges should follow earlier decisions, called precedent, spread to much of the English-speaking world. In continental Europe, law was built on written codes, a tradition that produced the Napoleonic Code of 1804. In the 1700s and 1800s, constitutions and bills of rights limited the power of rulers, and in the 1900s international law grew, with the United Nations founded in 1945 and international courts to try war crimes. Today, new laws are made constantly for new problems, such as data privacy and artificial intelligence. In the 1900s, law changed in two large ways. First, human rights law grew: the Universal Declaration of Human Rights of 1948 set out rights that belong to everyone, and later treaties gave them legal force. Second, new fields appeared: environmental law, to protect nature; consumer law, to protect buyers; and data protection law, to protect privacy in the age of computers. International courts, such as the International Court of Justice, founded in 1945, and the International Criminal Court, founded in 2002, were created to settle disputes between states and to try the gravest crimes.

Key concepts: A statute is a law made by a legislature. Precedent is the rule that courts follow earlier decisions in similar cases. A contract is an agreement the law will enforce. Rights are freedoms or entitlements the law protects, such as freedom of speech or the right to a fair trial. Jurisdiction is the area or subject over which a court or a law has authority. Criminal law deals with offences against society, such as theft; civil law deals with disputes between people, such as a broken contract. A constitution is the highest law of a state, setting out how it is governed. Liability means legal responsibility for harm or for a debt. Evidence is the material, such as documents, testimony, and objects, that a court uses to decide what happened. A trial is the formal hearing in which evidence is presented and a decision is made. An appeal is a request to a higher court to review a decision. Negligence is the failure to take reasonable care, which can make a person liable for harm.

How it works: Law works through institutions. A legislature, such as a parliament, makes new laws, called statutes, by debating and voting. Courts interpret and apply the law: a judge or jury hears the facts of a dispute, the lawyers argue how the law applies, and the court gives a decision, which becomes a precedent for later cases in common-law systems. The executive, including the police, enforces the law by investigating offences and carrying out court orders. Citizens can also use the law themselves, by signing contracts, making wills, or bringing a claim to court. Because law is written and public, people can know the rules in advance, and because courts give reasons, their decisions can be checked and appealed. The whole system rests on the rule of law: the idea that no one, not even a ruler, is above the law. The law also works through lawyers, who advise clients and argue cases, and through legal aid, which helps people who cannot pay for a lawyer. In many systems, a jury of ordinary citizens decides the facts of a case, while the judge decides the law, a division of labour that dates back centuries.

Worked examples: A contract is a law-backed agreement: if two people agree that one will build a wall for a price, and the wall is not built, the court can order payment or completion. A will is a legal document that says how a person's property should be divided after death; without one, the law decides. A speed limit is a law that sets the fastest speed allowed on a road, and breaking it brings a fine. A constitution, such as that of the United States, written in 1787, is a law that sets out how a state is governed and what rights its citizens hold. A lease is a legal agreement that lets a person live in a home owned by someone else, setting out the rent, the length of the stay, and the duties of each side. A patent is a legal right that gives an inventor control over an invention for a limited time, in return for sharing how it works.

Real applications: Law governs everyday life: contracts make trade possible, traffic laws keep roads safe, and family law settles questions of marriage, children, and inheritance. Criminal law protects people from harm and sets punishments. Constitutional law limits what governments can do. International law governs relations between states, including treaties on trade, war, and the environment. Businesses rely on law for employment, property, and patents. In short, wherever people need to settle disputes fairly and predictably, law is the tool they use. Law is also used to protect the environment: laws limit pollution, protect endangered species, and set rules for the use of land and water.

A law can be unfair, and people can disagree about what is just; law is a tool for justice, and it sometimes needs to be changed. Why are there so many laws? Because societies are complex, and each new situation, from cars to the internet, creates new disputes that need rules. Can a law be ignored if it seems wrong? In most systems, no: the remedy is to challenge it in court or to change it through the legislature, not to ignore it. Do all countries have the same law? No. Legal systems differ, and a thing that is legal in one country may be illegal in another, which is why travellers and businesses must check local law. Is the law only for lawyers? No. Ordinary people use law every day, in contracts, housing, work, and family life. Why do lawyers argue both sides? Because the system relies on each side being argued as strongly as possible, so that the judge or jury can weigh both and reach a fair decision. Can a law be unjust and still be law? Philosophers disagree: some say an unjust law is still a law, though it should be changed, while others say a grossly unjust rule is not truly law at all.

Government — What Government Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000008 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Government is the set of institutions through which a community organises itself, makes decisions, and enforces them. It includes the people and bodies that make laws, carry them out, and judge disputes: legislatures, executives, courts, and the civil service. Government is found at many levels, from a village council to a national state to international bodies such as the United Nations. Its basic jobs are to keep order, protect people and property, provide services that individuals cannot easily provide alone, such as roads and defence, and to settle disagreements by agreed rules rather than by force. Government is also the keeper of records: birth certificates, land registers, and census data are all kept by governments, and they are the basis for planning, taxation, and the protection of rights.

History: Government began when people settled in towns and needed rules beyond the family. The first city-states appeared in Mesopotamia around 3000 BCE, ruled by kings who collected taxes and raised armies. In ancient Greece, Athens developed democracy in the 400s BCE, with male citizens voting directly on laws, though women, enslaved people, and foreigners were excluded. Rome moved from a republic, in which citizens elected officials, to an empire ruled by one man. In medieval Europe, kings ruled with the help of nobles and the Church, and in 1215 the English barons forced King John to accept Magna Carta, a first step toward limiting royal power. In the 1600s and 1700s, thinkers such as Locke and Montesquieu argued for government by consent and for the separation of powers. Revolutions in America in 1776 and France in 1789 put those ideas into practice. In the 1900s, most states became republics or constitutional monarchies, women won the vote in most countries, and international organisations were created to manage peace and trade. In the 1900s, government grew larger and more complex. The welfare state, built after the Second World War in many countries, gave governments new roles in health, education, and pensions. Decolonisation, from the 1940s to the 1970s, created dozens of new states, each of which had to build a government of its own. The European Union, founded in the 1950s, created a new level of government above the nation, in which states share decisions on trade, law, and the environment. Key concepts: Sovereignty is the authority of a state to govern itself without outside interference. Legitimacy is the acceptance by the people that a government has the right to rule. Separation of powers means dividing government into branches, legislature, executive, and judiciary, so that no one branch holds all power. The rule of law means that everyone, including the government, must obey the law, and that laws are made openly and applied fairly. Accountability means that those who hold power must answer for how they use it, through elections, courts, and the press. The judiciary, the courts, settles disputes and checks that the other branches act within the law. The civil service, the body of officials who run departments, provides continuity when politicians change. Money comes mainly from taxes, and the budget decides how it is spent. Citizens take part by voting in elections, and in a democracy the government can be removed at the next election if it loses support. In an authoritarian system, power is held without free elections, and the same institutions may exist on paper but work very differently in practice. Government also works through money: the budget is the plan for what is collected and what is spent, and it is debated in the legislature before it is approved. Worked examples: In the United States, the federal government has three branches: Congress makes laws, the President carries them out, and the courts interpret them, a design set out in the Constitution of 1787. Real applications: Government affects daily life through schools, hospitals, roads, water, and policing. It sets taxes and spends them on services. The quality of government, whether it is honest, capable, and fair, shapes the health and prosperity of a whole society. Government is also used to protect the environment: it sets standards for clean air and water, creates national parks, and signs treaties to limit pollution across borders.

Most states today describe themselves as democracies, but scholars debate how democratic each one really is, and some argue that other values, such as stability or prosperity, matter too. Do citizens have to obey the government? In a lawful system, citizens are expected to follow the law, but they also have rights to criticise, protest, and vote for change. What is the difference between a government and a regime? A government is the group currently in power, while a regime is the whole system of rule, including its institutions and its rules about who can hold power. Can a government be legitimate without elections? Some argue that a government can be legitimate if it rules well and with the consent of the people, but most modern theory ties legitimacy to free and fair elections.

Definition: This record is a history of medicine: how people in different times and places understood illness and tried to deal with it. Key concepts: The humoral theory held that health depended on the balance of four bodily fluids, and it guided treatment in Europe and the Islamic world for over a thousand years. Anatomy is the study of the structure of the body, rebuilt by dissection from the 1500s onward. The germ theory holds that micro-organisms cause many diseases, a view established in the 1800s. Vaccination is the practice of exposing the body to a weakened or related disease agent to build protection, first tested against smallpox in 1796. Antisepsis is the practice of preventing infection during surgery, introduced in the 1860s. A clinical trial is a planned comparison of treatments, with patients assigned by chance to one group or another, so that the result can be judged fairly. Public health is the organised effort to protect the health of whole populations, through clean water, sanitation, and vaccination programmes. Epidemiology is the study of how diseases spread through populations, and it grew from the work of John Snow, who traced a cholera outbreak in London in 1854 to a single water pump. Quarantine is the separation of the sick or the exposed from others, a practice used since the Middle Ages. A pandemic is a disease that spreads across many countries.

How it works: The history of medicine is studied by tracing how ideas about the body changed and how those changes were tested. Historians read medical texts, hospital records, and the writings of practitioners and patients, and they compare them with what archaeology and modern science can show. A key pattern repeats: a new observation challenges an old theory, a test is made, and practice changes slowly as evidence accumulates. Another pattern is the growth of institutions: from the lone healer to the hospital, the medical school, and the public health service. A third is the rise of statistics: from the 1800s onward, doctors began to count results, comparing how many patients recovered with one approach and how many with another, which made it possible to judge treatments by evidence rather than by reputation alone. Medical history is also written from the patient's side: diaries, letters, and oral histories show what illness felt like, not only what doctors thought about it, and this has become an important part of the field.

Worked examples: In ancient Greece, Hippocrates, who lived around 400 BCE, taught that disease had natural causes rather than being sent by the gods, and that diet, rest, and cleanliness mattered; his followers wrote case histories that are still read as examples of careful observation. In 1543, the Flemish anatomist Andreas Vesalius published a detailed atlas of the human body based on dissection, correcting errors that had stood for a thousand years. In 1796, Edward Jenner tested vaccination against smallpox, and the disease was eventually declared eradicated in 1980. In 1928, Alexander Fleming observed that a mould killed bacteria, a finding that led to the antibiotic penicillin in the following decade. Florence Nightingale, working in the Crimean War in the 1850s, showed by careful records that more soldiers died from disease and poor sanitation than from wounds, and her work changed how hospitals were built. Joseph Lister, in the 1860s, used carbolic acid to clean wounds and instruments, and deaths after surgery fell sharply.

Real applications: The history of medicine matters because it explains how modern health care came to be, and why its practices look the way they do. It teaches how evidence replaced guesswork in one field after another, a lesson used in science education and in public health. It explains the origins of hospitals, medical schools, and the idea of the clinical trial. It also records mistakes, such as treatments once believed in and later abandoned, which is a warning against certainty. Museums and medical schools use this history to train new practitioners and to remind them that today's knowledge will also be revised. The history of medicine is used in public health, where the story of past epidemics guides the response to new ones, and in medical education, where students learn that today's knowledge will also be revised.

Common questions and misconceptions: Did people in the past know nothing about medicine? No. They knew a great deal about herbs, wounds, childbirth, and care, even when their theories about why things worked were wrong. Was medicine once only superstition? Some practices were, but the record also shows careful observation, honest case notes, and real skill, especially in surgery and midwifery. Why did old theories last so long? Because they explained what people saw, they were taught by respected authorities, and there was no better test available until new instruments and methods appeared. Is the history of medicine a story of steady progress? Largely it is a story of progress, but with setbacks, wrong turns, and practices later abandoned, which is why historians study the failures as well as the successes. Does this record give medical advice? No. It describes the past only; questions about health belong to qualified professionals and to current medical sources. Why did doctors once bleed their patients? Because the humoral theory said that illness came from too much blood, and bleeding seemed logical within that theory; the practice lasted until the theory was replaced by better evidence. Is the history of medicine only about doctors? No. It is also about nurses, midwives, healers, and patients, and about the families and communities that cared for the sick.

Engineering — What Engineering Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000010 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Engineering is the use of science and mathematics to design and build things that solve practical problems. Engineers make structures, machines, systems, and processes: bridges that carry traffic, circuits that carry signals, software that carries information, and water systems that carry clean water to cities. Engineering is different from science: science seeks to understand the world, while engineering seeks to change it usefully. An engineer works within limits, such as cost, safety, materials, and time, and must find the best solution that fits them all. Engineering is also a social activity: an engineer must listen to the people who will use what is built, because a bridge that nobody wants to cross, or a machine that nobody can repair, is a failure, however clever its design.

History: Engineering is ancient. In Egypt, engineers built the pyramids around 2560 BCE, moving blocks of stone weighing tonnes without wheels or iron tools. In Rome, engineers built aqueducts that carried water for kilometres, roads that lasted two thousand years, and the dome of the Pantheon, still the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. In China, engineers built the Grand Canal and the Great Wall. In the Middle Ages, engineers built cathedrals, windmills, and water mills. The Industrial Revolution, beginning in Britain in the 1700s, changed engineering: steam engines, iron bridges, railways, and factories needed new kinds of calculation and new materials. In the 1800s, engineering split into branches: civil, mechanical, electrical, and chemical. In the 1900s, new branches appeared, including aeronautical, electronic, and software engineering, and engineers built aircraft, computers, and space rockets. Today, engineering includes robotics, biomedical engineering, and environmental engineering. In the 1900s, engineering reached new scales. The Hoover Dam, completed in 1936, held back a river and made electricity for a region. The Apollo programme, which landed people on the Moon in 1969, joined thousands of engineers in a single project. The Channel Tunnel, opened in 1994, joined Britain and France under the sea. In the 2000s, engineers built the tallest building in the world, the Burj Khalifa in Dubai, completed in 2010, and began to build machines that learn, such as self-driving cars.

Key concepts: Design is the plan for a solution, balancing what it must do against what it may cost. A constraint is a limit, such as budget, weight, or safety rules, that the design must respect. A material is what a thing is made of, such as steel, concrete, or silicon, and each material has properties such as strength and weight. A safety factor is the margin between the load a part is expected to carry and the load it can actually carry. A system is a set of parts working together, such as a power station or a railway. A prototype is an early version built for testing. A model is a simplified version, physical or mathematical, used to predict how the real thing will behave. Maintenance is the care that keeps a built thing working over its lifetime. Tolerance is the small amount by which a part may differ from its design and still work, and it matters because no part can be made perfectly. Efficiency is the share of the energy put into a machine that comes out as useful work. A load is a force acting on a structure, such as the weight of traffic on a bridge or the wind on a tower.

How it works: Engineering follows a design cycle. It begins with a problem: a river to cross, a message to send, a disease to diagnose. The engineer studies the requirements, what the solution must do, and the constraints, what it must not cost, what materials are available, and what safety rules apply. Then comes design: sketching, calculating, and modelling possible solutions, often with computer simulation. A prototype is built and tested, and the results are measured against the requirements. The design is revised, sometimes many times, until it works well enough. Then the solution is built, installed, and maintained, and its performance is watched over its lifetime. At every step, the engineer checks safety, because a failure can cost lives; this is why engineering uses safety factors, designing parts to carry much more than the expected load. Engineering also uses standards: agreed rules for sizes, materials, and safety, so that a bolt made in one country fits a nut made in another, and so that a building is safe wherever it is built. Testing is continuous: a new aircraft is flown for thousands of hours before it carries passengers, and a new bridge is loaded with weights before it opens.

Worked examples: A bridge must carry the weight of traffic and its own weight, resist wind, and stand for decades; engineers calculate the forces in every beam and cable before it is built. A water pump in a village must lift enough water each day, use power that is available locally, and be repairable with local parts. A mobile phone contains a radio, a battery, a screen, and a processor, each designed by a different kind of engineer, and all fitted into a pocket-sized case. The International Space Station, built by many countries, is an engineering system in which air, water, power, and structure must all work together in orbit. The Forth Bridge in Scotland, completed in 1890, is a railway bridge of steel cantilevers, and it was the first large structure built of steel. The Panama Canal, opened in 1914, lets ships pass between the Atlantic and the Pacific through locks that lift them over the land.

Real applications: Engineering builds the world people live in: homes, offices, factories, roads, bridges, water systems, and power grids. It makes transport possible, from bicycles to aircraft, and communication possible, from the telegraph to the mobile phone. In medicine, engineers design imaging machines, prosthetic limbs, and hospital systems. In agriculture, they design irrigation and storage. In space, they design rockets and satellites. Engineering also protects people: flood defences, earthquake-resistant buildings, and safety systems in cars and aircraft are all engineering work. Engineering is also used in disaster relief: engineers design shelters that can be built quickly, water systems that can be set up in days, and bridges that can be carried in parts and assembled on site.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is engineering the same as science? No. Science explains the world, engineering changes it; engineers use science, but they also use judgement, experience, and economics. Can an engineer work alone? Rarely. Large projects need teams of many kinds of engineer, plus architects, builders, and managers. Do engineers only build big things? No. Engineering includes tiny things, such as the circuits inside a hearing aid, and invisible things, such as software. Is engineering only about maths? Maths is essential, but engineering also needs creativity, communication, and an understanding of people. Can engineering solve every problem? No. Some problems are social or political, and engineering can help only if the problem is framed clearly and the limits are honestly stated. Do engineers need to be good at drawing? They need to be able to read and make drawings, which are the language of engineering, though much of that work is now done by computer. Can engineering be creative? Yes. The best engineering is creative, because it finds a new way to solve an old problem within tight limits.

Geography — What Geography Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000011 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Geography is the study of the Earth and its places: where things are, why they are there, and how places differ and connect. It covers the physical Earth, mountains, rivers, climates, and soils, and the human Earth, cities, farms, borders, and trade. Geography asks simple questions with deep answers: why is this city here and not there, why does this region grow wheat while that one grows rice, and how does a change in one place affect another? It uses maps, measurements, and fieldwork, and it links the natural sciences with the human sciences. Geography also studies the future of places: how a coastline will change as the sea rises, how a city will grow, and how a forest will recover after a fire, using models built from past data.

History: Geography began with travellers and mapmakers. In ancient Greece, Eratosthenes measured the circumference of the Earth around 240 BCE, using the angle of the Sun's shadow in two cities, and got a figure close to the true one. Ptolemy, writing around 150 CE, made a map of the known world with a system of latitude and longitude. In the Islamic Golden Age, geographers such as al-Idrisi drew maps that combined Greek, Arab, and travellers' knowledge. In the 1400s and 1500s, European voyages mapped the Atlantic and the Americas, and globes and atlases spread the new knowledge. In the 1800s, Alexander von Humboldt travelled in South America and showed how climate, altitude, and vegetation are linked, founding modern physical geography. In the 1900s, aerial photography, then satellites, gave geographers a view of the whole Earth, and the geographic information system, or GIS, made it possible to layer maps of soil, population, and roads on a computer. Today, geographers use satellite data to track deforestation, urban growth, and climate change in near real time. In the 1900s, geography split into two main branches that still shape it: physical geography, which studies nature, and human geography, which studies people. The quantitative revolution of the 1960s brought statistics and computers into the subject, and the cultural turn of the 1980s brought questions of meaning, identity, and power. The invention of the Global Positioning System in the 1970s and 1980s, and of the web map in the 2000s, put geography in the pocket of every person with a phone.

Key concepts: Place is a location with meaning, such as a home town or a holy site, not only a set of coordinates. Scale is the level at which something is studied, from local to global. A map is a representation of an area, using symbols to show features such as roads, rivers, and borders. A region is an area with shared features, such as the Sahel or the Amazon basin. Spatial pattern is the arrangement of things across space, such as the clustering of cities along rivers. Latitude and longitude are the grid of lines used to fix a position on the Earth. A geographic information system, or GIS, is a computer system that stores, analyses, and displays geographic data in layers. Climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a place, as distinct from the weather on a single day. A watershed is the area of land that drains into a river, and it is the natural unit for managing water. Urbanisation is the growth of towns and cities, and the movement of people into them. A border is a line that marks where one state ends and another begins, and it can be a river, a mountain, or a line drawn on a map.

How it works: Geography works by locating, mapping, and explaining. The first step is to find where something is, using coordinates, latitude and longitude, or an address. The second is to map it, showing its pattern in space: where things cluster, where they thin out, and what boundaries they follow. The third is to explain the pattern, asking what causes it: a river, a mountain, a law, a market, or a climate. Geographers also work at different scales, from a single street to the whole planet, because a pattern visible at one scale may vanish at another. Fieldwork, going to a place and observing it directly, remains essential, and it is now combined with satellite images, surveys, and computer models. The result is often a map that makes a complex pattern visible at a glance. Geographers also use remote sensing, the study of the Earth from aircraft and satellites, which can show heat, vegetation, and water, not only visible light. A satellite image can show a drought before it is visible on the ground, because the plants change colour as they dry out.

Worked examples: The Nile flows north through desert, and its annual flood once made farming possible in Egypt, which is why the great cities of Egypt grew along its banks. The city of Venice was built on a lagoon, so its streets are canals and its transport is by boat. The Sahara is the largest hot desert in the world, and its size and dryness shape the lives of the people on its edges. The Rhine has been a trade route for two thousand years, and the cities along it, such as Cologne and Rotterdam, grew rich from that trade. A map of world languages shows that Spanish is spoken across a whole continent, because of colonisation, while other languages are spoken in a single valley. The Great Barrier Reef, off Australia, is the largest living structure on Earth, and it can be seen from space. The city of Amsterdam was built below sea level, and its canals and pumps are a geographical answer to a geographical problem.

Real applications: Geography is used to plan cities, deciding where to build homes, roads, and parks. It guides farming, showing which crops suit which soils and climates. It helps manage water, forests, and coastlines. In emergencies, geographers map floods, fires, and earthquakes to guide rescue. In business, geography guides where to open shops and how to move goods. In school, geography teaches people to read maps, understand other countries, and see how places are connected by trade, migration, and climate. Geography is also used in the study of disease: maps of where cases occur can show how a disease spreads, as John Snow showed with cholera in London in 1854.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is geography only about maps? Maps are its main tool, but geography is about understanding places and the connections between them, and maps are the way that understanding is shown. Is geography a science or a humanity? It is both: physical geography studies nature with scientific methods, while human geography studies people, culture, and politics. Why do geographers study cities? Because most people now live in cities, and cities are where trade, migration, and pollution meet. Can geography predict the future? It can model what is likely, such as where a city will grow or where a flood may strike, but predictions depend on assumptions and are always uncertain. Is the Earth still being mapped? Yes. New maps are made constantly, of ocean floors, of changing coastlines, and of places seen from space for the first time. Is the world getting smaller? In a sense, yes: faster transport and instant communication have made distant places feel closer, a process geographers call time-space compression. Can geography help with climate change? Yes. Geographers map the places most at risk, study how people adapt, and help plan where to build and where not to build.

Religion — What Religion Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000012 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Religion is a set of beliefs and practices shared by a community, usually centred on what its members hold sacred: a god or gods, a spiritual reality, or a path to a good life. Religions typically include stories about the origin and purpose of the world, teachings about right and wrong, rituals such as prayer or festivals, and a community that gathers to practise them. Religion is studied descriptively here: this record describes what different traditions teach and do, without preaching any one of them and without ranking them. The world's major traditions include Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, Christianity, Islam, and Sikhism, alongside many Indigenous and folk traditions. Religion is also studied by scholars who do not belong to the tradition they study, and their work is descriptive: it aims to understand what people believe and do, and why, rather than to judge whether those beliefs are true.

History: Religion is as old as human culture. Burials with grave goods tens of thousands of years old suggest early beliefs about death and the afterlife, though their meaning is not certain. The first written religious texts appear in Egypt and Mesopotamia more than four thousand years ago, recording gods, myths, and rituals. Hinduism grew over centuries in India, without a single founder. Judaism took shape in the ancient Near East, and its scriptures were written over many centuries. Buddhism and Jainism arose in India in the 500s BCE, in a time of questioning old rituals. Christianity spread from the Roman Empire across Europe and then the world, and Islam spread from Arabia across the Middle East, Africa, and Asia in the centuries after the 600s CE. Sikhism arose in the 1400s. In the last two centuries, religion has changed rapidly: migration has made most cities multi-faith, secular states have separated religion from government in many countries, and new religious movements have appeared. Scholars estimate the numbers of adherents differently, and exact counts are disputed; the figures commonly quoted come from surveys such as those of the Pew Research Center. In the 1900s, religion changed in public life. In many Western countries, fewer people attended services, and secular ideas spread, while in other parts of the world, religion grew, especially in Africa and Asia. Migration made cities such as London, New York, and Toronto home to many faiths at once. The study of religion also grew: universities created departments for the comparative study of religions, and scholars recorded the beliefs of Indigenous peoples, whose traditions had often been ignored. Today, religion is also present online, where communities gather, teach, and worship in new ways.

Key concepts: Belief is what members of a religion hold to be true about the world and the sacred. Ritual is a repeated action with symbolic meaning, such as prayer, fasting, or a ceremony. Scripture is a sacred text, such as the Bible, the Quran, the Torah, or the Vedas. A community is the group that practises a religion together. Monotheism is belief in one god; polytheism is belief in many gods; some traditions, such as some forms of Buddhism, are non-theistic, not centred on a god. Ethics are the teachings about right and wrong. A festival is a holy day or season that marks an event in the tradition. A pilgrimage is a journey to a sacred place. A denomination is a recognised branch within a religion, such as the Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches within Christianity. A sect is a smaller group that has separated from a larger one, often over doctrine or practice. Sacred means set apart as holy, whether a place, a text, or an object. Prayer is communication with the sacred, in words, in silence, or in song.

How it works: A religion works through shared belief, shared practice, and shared community. Beliefs are passed on through scriptures, stories, and teaching, and they answer questions such as where the world came from and how people should live. Practices, called rituals, mark the rhythm of life: daily prayer, weekly gatherings, yearly festivals, and rites for birth, marriage, and death. Community gives these beliefs and practices a home: a congregation, a temple, a mosque, a church, or a synagogue, where members support one another and teach the young. Calendars matter too: many religions follow a lunar or solar calendar, so festivals move through the seasons or stay fixed to them. Ethics link belief to behaviour: most traditions teach honesty, generosity, and care for others, though they differ in their reasons and their rules. Religion also works through teaching: children learn their tradition at home, in school, and in worship, and adults study its texts and its history. Conversion, the joining of a new faith, is a change of belief, of practice, and of community, and it is a major event in a person's life.

Worked examples: Hinduism, with roots in the Indus valley more than three thousand years ago, includes many gods, the ideas of dharma and karma, and festivals such as Diwali. Buddhism, founded by Siddhartha Gautama in India around the 500s BCE, teaches the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. Judaism, one of the oldest monotheistic faiths, centres on the covenant with Abraham and the Torah, and marks the Sabbath from Friday evening to Saturday evening. Christianity, founded on the life and teachings of Jesus in the first century CE, is the world's largest religion by number of adherents, according to widely used estimates. Islam, founded in the seventh century CE in Arabia, is based on the Quran and the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad, and its five pillars include prayer, fasting, and pilgrimage. Sikhism, founded in Punjab in the 1400s, teaches one God, equality, and service. Indigenous traditions, found on every continent, often centre on ancestors, the land, and the spirits of nature. The Hajj, the pilgrimage to Mecca, is one of the largest gatherings in the world, drawing millions of Muslims each year. The Western Wall in Jerusalem is a place of prayer for Jews, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, in the same city, is a place of pilgrimage for Christians.

Real applications: Religion shapes art, music, architecture, and literature in every culture: cathedrals, mosques, temples, and synagogues are among the most visited buildings in the world. Religious festivals mark the calendar, and religious charities run schools, hospitals, and food programmes. Religious law and ethics have influenced the laws of many states. The study of religion helps diplomats, teachers, and journalists understand communities and avoid misunderstanding. For many people, religion is also a source of personal meaning, comfort, and community, and that role is part of why it matters. Religion is also studied in schools, where religious education teaches about different faiths, and in the law, where courts must balance religious freedom with other rights.

Common questions and misconceptions: Do all religions teach the same thing? No. They share some ideas, such as compassion and honesty, but they differ on the nature of the divine, the purpose of life, and the way to live. Is religion only about belief? No. Practice, community, and identity matter as much as belief, and some people belong to a religion mainly through family and culture. Are religious people less rational than others? That is a claim, not a fact, and scholars of religion disagree about it; many religious thinkers have used careful reasoning, and many non-religious people hold beliefs they have not examined. Is religion declining? In some countries it is, while in others it is growing, and worldwide the number of people who identify with a religion remains large, though estimates vary. Can different religions live together? In many cities they do, and interfaith dialogue, shared charity work, and common civic life are well established in many places. Are all religions equally old? No. Some, such as Hinduism, have roots more than three thousand years old, while others, such as Sikhism, are about five hundred years old. Do religions always agree within themselves? No. Every large religion contains different schools, denominations, and opinions, and disagreement within a faith is normal. Is atheism a religion? It is not a religion in the usual sense, because it does not involve belief in the sacred, but it is a position about religion, and it is studied alongside religion in many courses.

Philosophy — What Philosophy Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000013 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Philosophy is the study of the most general questions: what is real, what can we know, what is right, and how should we live. The word comes from Greek, meaning love of wisdom. Philosophers work mainly by reasoning: they examine ideas, test arguments, and look for the assumptions hidden inside them. Philosophy has no laboratory and no final authority; its method is careful thought, clear definition, and honest argument. Its main branches are logic, the study of reasoning; ethics, the study of right and wrong; epistemology, the study of knowledge; and metaphysics, the study of what exists. Philosophy is also a way of reading: a philosopher reads a text slowly, asking what each word means, what is assumed, and what follows, and this close reading is a skill that transfers to law, to science, and to everyday argument.

History: Philosophy began in several places at once. In Greece, Thales, in the 600s BCE, asked what the world is made of and gave a natural answer, water, rather than a myth. Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, in the 400s and 300s BCE, built systems covering logic, politics, ethics, and nature. In China, Confucius and Laozi, in the 500s BCE, taught about society and the way of nature. In India, the Buddha and the writers of the Upanishads asked about suffering and the self. In the Middle Ages, philosophers in the Islamic world, such as Ibn Sina and Averroes, and in Christian Europe, such as Aquinas, joined philosophy to theology. In the 1600s, Descartes, Locke, and Hume debated knowledge and the mind. In the 1700s, Kant tried to settle those debates. In the 1800s and 1900s, philosophy split into many schools, including the analytic tradition, which stresses clear language, and the continental tradition, which stresses history and experience. Today, philosophy covers new questions raised by science and technology, such as the nature of consciousness and the ethics of artificial intelligence. In the 1900s, philosophy faced new questions from science. The theory of relativity raised questions about time, quantum mechanics raised questions about what is real, and the study of the mind raised questions about consciousness and free will. In the late 1900s, philosophy also became more global: Western philosophers began to study Indian, Chinese, and African philosophy more seriously, and the idea that philosophy belongs to one tradition was challenged. Today, philosophy is taught in most universities, and its questions appear in public debate, from the ethics of artificial intelligence to the meaning of a just society.

Key concepts: Logic is the study of correct reasoning, including the difference between a valid argument, whose conclusion follows from its premises, and a sound argument, which is valid and has true premises. Ethics is the study of right and wrong, including questions about duties, consequences, and character. Epistemology is the study of knowledge: what it is, how we get it, and when a belief counts as justified. Metaphysics is the study of what exists, including questions about time, causation, and the mind. A premise is a statement used as a starting point in an argument. A conclusion is what the argument claims to prove. A counterexample is a case that shows a claim is false. An argument is a set of statements offered as reasons for a conclusion. A thought experiment is an imagined case used to test an idea, such as asking what a person would do if they could see the future. A distinction is the separation of two ideas that are often confused, such as the difference between what is true and what is believed. An assumption is a statement taken for granted in an argument, and a good philosopher looks for the assumptions that are hidden.

How it works: Philosophy works by argument. A philosopher starts with a question and states it as clearly as possible, defining the key terms. Then an argument is built: a set of premises, statements taken as starting points, leading to a conclusion. The argument is tested: are the premises true, does the conclusion really follow, and are there counterexamples, cases that break the argument? Objections are raised and answered, and the argument is revised. A famous tool is the thought experiment, an imagined case that tests an idea, such as asking what would happen if a person were a brain in a vat. Another is the distinction, separating two ideas that are often confused, such as knowing something and believing it. Over time, weak arguments are dropped and strong ones survive, though many questions remain open after thousands of years. Philosophy also uses dialogue: the Socratic method, named after Socrates, is a conversation in which questions are used to test a person's beliefs, until the weak ones fall away and the strong ones remain. In a seminar, students practise this by arguing with one another and with the text.

Worked examples: The philosopher Socrates, in Athens in the 400s BCE, questioned people in the marketplace, showing that many who claimed to know what justice or courage meant could not define it. Rene Descartes, writing in 1641, argued that even if everything else could be doubted, the fact that he was thinking proved he existed, in the phrase I think, therefore I am. Immanuel Kant, writing in the 1700s, argued that people should act only on rules they would accept as rules for everyone. A modern example is the trolley problem: if a runaway trolley will kill five people unless you pull a lever to divert it, killing one, should you pull it? Philosophers use the case to test ideas about moral responsibility. The Ship of Theseus is a puzzle: if every plank of a ship is replaced, one by one, is it still the same ship? Philosophers use it to ask what makes a thing the same over time. The veil of ignorance, proposed by John Rawls in 1971, asks people to design a society without knowing what place they will hold in it, as a test of fairness.

Real applications: Philosophy is used in law, where judges and lawyers argue about rights and justice. It is used in medicine, where ethics committees decide hard questions about treatment. It is used in computing, where questions about privacy, responsibility, and artificial intelligence need clear thinking. It is used in education, where teachers ask what should be taught and why. In daily life, philosophy trains the habit of asking for reasons, spotting bad arguments, and thinking before judging, which is useful in every job and every argument. Philosophy is also used in business, where ethics committees advise on questions such as honest advertising and the treatment of workers, and in technology, where philosophers help to write the rules for artificial intelligence.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is philosophy only about opinions? No. Philosophers argue with reasons, and a good argument can be checked, even when the conclusion is disputed. Does philosophy have any answers? It has some, such as the finding that certain arguments are invalid, but many of its biggest questions are still open. Is philosophy useful? Its defenders say it trains clear thinking, which is useful everywhere; its critics say it produces questions without answers. Both sides agree it is not a practical craft like carpentry. Can philosophy prove that God exists, or that God does not exist? Philosophers have made arguments on both sides for centuries, and there is no agreement; the arguments are studied for what they show about reasoning. Is philosophy the same as religion? No. Religion rests on faith, revelation, or tradition, while philosophy rests on argument, though the two have influenced each other deeply. Can philosophy change the world? Its defenders point to ideas such as human rights and democracy, which began as philosophical arguments and became institutions. Is philosophy a waste of time? That is a question philosophy itself can answer: it asks what counts as a good use of time, and it has argued that thinking clearly is one of the best uses of all.

Economics — What Economics Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000014 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Economics is the study of how people, businesses, and societies use scarce resources to meet their wants. Resources are scarce because there is never enough of anything, land, labour, time, or money, to satisfy every want. Economics studies how choices are made: what to produce, how to produce it, and who gets it. It covers markets, where buyers and sellers meet, and whole economies, where governments set policies. Economics uses both theory, models of how people behave, and data, measurements of what actually happens. Economics is also a study of institutions: the rules, such as property rights and courts, that let markets work, and the ways in which those rules can fail, leaving people without what they need.

History: Economic thinking is old, but economics as a separate subject began in the 1700s. Adam Smith, in The Wealth of Nations of 1776, argued that markets, guided by self-interest, could organise production better than government planning, and introduced the idea of the division of labour. In the 1800s, David Ricardo explained trade, showing that countries benefit by specialising in what they do relatively best, and Karl Marx argued that capitalism would produce conflict between workers and owners. In the 1870s, economists began to use mathematics and the idea of marginal utility, the value of one more unit. In the 1930s, John Maynard Keynes argued that governments should spend in recessions to support demand, a view that shaped policy after the Second World War. In the late 1900s, economics expanded into new areas, such as the study of behaviour, development, and the environment. Today, economists use large data sets and experiments to test their theories. In the late 1900s, economics changed again. Behavioural economics, led by Daniel Kahneman and others, showed that people do not always act as the old models assumed: they are influenced by habit, by fear, and by how a choice is presented. Development economics studied how poor countries grow, and environmental economics studied the cost of pollution and the value of nature. In the 2000s, the financial crisis of 2008 challenged many economic ideas, and economists debated how to prevent another one. Today, economists use experiments, big data, and the study of human behaviour to test their theories.

Key concepts: Scarcity means that resources are limited while wants are unlimited, so choices must be made. Supply and demand describe how much of a good sellers offer and buyers want at each price. A market is a place or system where buyers and sellers meet, in person or online. Gross domestic product, or GDP, is the total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year. Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. Unemployment is the share of people willing and able to work who cannot find a job. A monopoly is a market with only one seller. Opportunity cost is what is given up when a choice is made, such as the wages lost by going to school instead of working. A recession is a period when the economy shrinks, usually defined as two quarters of falling output. Interest is the price of borrowing money, and the interest rate is set by lenders and, in part, by central banks. Trade is the exchange of goods and services between people or countries, and a tariff is a tax on goods crossing a border.

How it works: Economics works by building models, simplified pictures of how people behave, and testing them against data. A model starts with assumptions, such as that buyers prefer lower prices and sellers prefer higher ones. From those assumptions, predictions are drawn, such as that a tax on a good will raise its price and reduce the amount sold. The prediction is then checked against real data, from markets, surveys, or experiments. If the data fit, the model is kept; if not, it is revised. Economists also use two kinds of analysis: positive economics, which describes what is, and normative economics, which argues about what should be. Markets are the central mechanism: prices adjust until the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer, a point called equilibrium. Governments also matter, because they set rules, collect taxes, and spend on services. Economists also use experiments: in a field experiment, a policy is tried in one place and not in another, so that the difference can be measured. In a laboratory experiment, people are paid to make choices in a controlled setting, which shows how they actually behave, not only how theory says they should.

Worked examples: If the price of coffee rises, people buy less of it and more of tea, which is the law of demand in action. A baker who can sell 100 loaves a day at two dollars each earns 200 dollars, but if the price rises to three dollars, fewer people buy, so the baker must weigh price against volume. When a government prints too much money, prices rise, which is inflation, as happened in Germany in 1923, when prices doubled in days. When the price of oil rises, airlines raise ticket prices, showing how a cost in one market spreads to another. A minimum wage sets a floor under pay: it raises the income of low-paid workers, and economists debate how much it affects employment. The price of a concert ticket rises when demand is high, which is why tickets for a popular band can cost many times their face value on the resale market. A country that discovers oil may find that its currency rises, making its other exports more expensive, a pattern called the resource curse.

Real applications: Economics is used by governments to set taxes, interest rates, and budgets. Central banks use it to manage inflation and employment. Businesses use it to set prices, choose how much to produce, and decide where to invest. Individuals use it, often without naming it, when they compare prices, save for the future, or choose a job. Economists also study poverty, inequality, and the environment, advising on policies such as minimum wages, carbon taxes, and trade agreements. In short, economics is the toolkit for decisions about scarce resources. Economics is also used to design auctions, such as the sale of radio spectrum to mobile phone companies, where the rules of the auction decide how much the government earns and who wins.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is economics a science? It uses scientific methods, observation, models, and testing, but it studies people, who are less predictable than atoms, so its predictions are less certain than those of physics. Can economists predict the future? They can forecast trends, but their forecasts are often wrong, especially about crises, and economists themselves disagree about this. Is economics only about money? No. It is about choices under scarcity, and it is applied to health, education, crime, and the environment, not only to money. Does economics say markets are always best? No. Economists recognise market failures, such as pollution, where markets do not work well, and they debate when government should step in. Is economics neutral? Its methods aim to be neutral, but its questions and its policy advice involve values, and economists disagree about those. Is economics only for rich countries? No. Some of its most important work is in poor countries, where small changes, such as a better seed or a loan to a small business, can change lives. Can economics measure happiness? It tries, through surveys of well-being, but happiness is hard to measure, and economists disagree about whether it should be the goal of policy.

Astronomy — What Astronomy Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000015 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Astronomy is the study of everything beyond the Earth: the Sun, the Moon, the planets, the stars, the galaxies, and the universe as a whole. It asks how these objects formed, how they move, what they are made of, and how they will change. Astronomers cannot touch the stars, so they learn by observing light and other signals that travel across space, and by building models that explain what they see. Astronomy is one of the oldest sciences, and also one of the newest, because new telescopes keep opening new views of the sky. Astronomy is also a science of time: because light travels at a fixed speed, looking far away means looking back in time, so the most distant objects we see are also the oldest.

History: Astronomy began with watching the sky. In Babylon, priests recorded the positions of the planets on clay tablets more than three thousand years ago, and in Egypt the calendar was tied to the rising of the star Sirius. In Greece, Aristarchus suggested in the 200s BCE that the Earth goes around the Sun, though most people followed Ptolemy, who put the Earth at the centre. In 1543, Copernicus revived the Sun-centred idea, and in the early 1600s Kepler showed that planets move in ellipses, while Galileo used the telescope to see the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus. In 1687, Newton explained the motions of the planets with the law of gravity. In the 1800s, astronomers learned to read starlight with spectroscopes, finding what stars are made of. In the 1920s, Edwin Hubble showed that the universe is expanding and that galaxies lie far beyond our own. In the 1900s, radio telescopes, space telescopes, and computers opened the study of black holes, exoplanets, and the cosmic microwave background, the faint afterglow of the Big Bang. In 2015, gravitational waves were detected for the first time, opening a new way of listening to the universe. In the 1900s, astronomy grew by leaps. In 1965, the cosmic microwave background was discovered, the faint heat left over from the early universe. In 1969, people walked on the Moon. In the 1990s, the Hubble Space Telescope showed that the universe contains hundreds of billions of galaxies. In 1995, the first planet around another Sun-like star was found, and thousands of exoplanets have been found since. In 2019, the first image of a black hole was published, showing a ring of light around a dark centre. In 2022, the James Webb Space Telescope began to look further back in time than any telescope before it.

Key concepts: A light-year is the distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres. An orbit is the path of one body around another, held by gravity. A galaxy is a vast collection of stars, gas, and dust held together by gravity; our galaxy is the Milky Way. A star is a ball of hot gas that shines by nuclear fusion in its core. A planet is a body that orbits a star and is large enough to be round. Redshift is the stretching of light from a distant object, showing that it is moving away. A black hole is a region where gravity is so strong that nothing, not even light, can escape. The Big Bang is the hot, dense beginning of the universe, about 13.8 billion years ago, according to current measurements. A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust in space, where new stars are born. A supernova is the explosion of a dying star, which can briefly outshine a whole galaxy. Dark matter is matter that cannot be seen directly but is inferred from its gravity, and dark energy is the unknown cause of the speeding up of the universe's expansion.

How it works: Astronomy works by collecting light and other signals. A telescope gathers light from a distant object and focuses it so it can be seen or recorded. A spectroscope splits the light into its colours, and the pattern of lines in the spectrum shows which elements are present and how fast the object is moving. Radio telescopes collect radio waves, which pass through clouds and dust that block visible light. Space telescopes, placed above the atmosphere, see light that the air absorbs. Astronomers then compare what they observe with models: mathematical descriptions of how stars, planets, and galaxies should behave. When a model predicts something new, such as a planet around another star, and the observation confirms it, the model gains support. Because the universe is so large, astronomers also use units such as the light-year, the distance light travels in a year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres. Astronomers also use computers to simulate the universe, building models of how galaxies form and evolve, and then comparing the models with what telescopes see. A model that matches the real sky is kept, and one that does not is changed.

Worked examples: The Moon goes through phases because the Sun lights it from different angles as it orbits the Earth, taking about 29.5 days to complete a cycle. The planets move across the sky in loops, which astronomers explain by the Earth overtaking them in its orbit. A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes directly between the Earth and the Sun, casting a shadow on the Earth. The Andromeda galaxy, the nearest large galaxy to our own, is about 2.5 million light-years away, so the light we see from it left before modern humans existed. The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, showed that the universe contains far more galaxies than anyone had thought. The transit method finds exoplanets by watching a star dim slightly when a planet passes in front of it. The Doppler method finds them by the wobble of a star, pulled by the gravity of an unseen planet. The rings of Saturn are made of ice and rock, and they are only about ten metres thick in places, though they stretch for hundreds of thousands of kilometres.

Real applications: Astronomy gives us the calendar, timekeeping, and navigation: the day, the month, and the year all come from the sky. It drives technology, from the optics of telescopes to the detectors in cameras and medical scanners. It warns of dangers, such as asteroids that might strike the Earth. It guides space exploration, which has produced satellites for weather, communication, and mapping. And it answers the largest questions people ask: how old is the universe, how did the Earth form, and are there other worlds that could support life. Astronomy is also used to track space debris, the thousands of dead satellites and fragments in orbit, which threaten working satellites and the International Space Station.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is astronomy the same as astrology? No. Astronomy is a science based on observation and testing; astrology is the belief that the positions of the stars and planets influence human lives, which has no support in scientific evidence. Can astronomers see the past? Yes, in a sense: because light takes time to travel, looking at a distant galaxy means seeing it as it was millions of years ago. Will we ever visit another star? Not with present technology: the nearest star is more than four light-years away, and a journey would take tens of thousands of years at current speeds. Is the universe infinite? That is not known: the observable universe has a size, about 93 billion light-years across, but what lies beyond observation is an open question. Do astronomers only work at night? No. Much of the work is done by day, analysing data from telescopes that observe automatically, and radio and space telescopes work around the clock. Are we alone in the universe? That is not known. Thousands of exoplanets have been found, and some lie in the habitable zone, where liquid water could exist, but no sign of life beyond Earth has been found so far. What is the universe made of? According to current measurements, only about five percent is ordinary matter; the rest is dark matter and dark energy, which are not yet understood.

Chemistry — What Chemistry Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000016 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Chemistry is the study of matter: what things are made of, how their parts are arranged, and how they change into other things. It studies atoms, the tiny building blocks of matter, and molecules, groups of atoms joined together. Chemistry explains why iron rusts, why wood burns, why bread rises, and how medicines work. It is a central science, linking physics, which describes the rules of matter and energy, with biology, which describes living things, and with medicine, geology, and engineering, which all use chemistry every day. Chemistry is also a science of the very small: a single drop of water contains more molecules than there are stars in the observable universe, and a chemist's work is to understand how those tiny parts make up the world we see.

History: Chemistry grew out of alchemy, the ancient practice of trying to turn base metals into gold and to find a medicine for all ills. Alchemists in China, India, and the Islamic world developed distillation, the making of acids, and the study of metals, even though their goals were not reached. In the 1600s, Robert Boyle argued that chemistry should be based on experiment, not on ancient authority. In the 1700s, Lavoisier showed that matter is conserved in chemical reactions, that nothing is created or destroyed, only rearranged, and he named oxygen and hydrogen. In 1808, John Dalton proposed that matter is made of atoms, and that each element has atoms of a particular weight. In 1869, Dmitri Mendeleev arranged the elements in a table by their properties, leaving gaps for elements not yet discovered, and the gaps were later filled, which confirmed his table. In the 1900s, chemists learned the structure of the atom, discovered the electron, the proton, and the neutron, and built the modern theory of chemical bonding. Today, chemistry includes the design of new materials, drugs, and catalysts, and it uses computers to model molecules before they are made. In the 1900s, chemistry grew into new fields. Physical chemistry joined physics and chemistry to study how reactions happen. Organic chemistry, the study of carbon compounds, made new medicines, plastics, and dyes. Biochemistry, the chemistry of life, explained how cells work. In 1953, the structure of DNA was described, and in the 2000s, chemists began to design materials atom by atom, including graphene, a single layer of carbon atoms that is stronger than steel and conducts electricity.

Key concepts: An atom is the smallest unit of an element, made of protons, neutrons, and electrons. An element is a pure substance made of one kind of atom, such as hydrogen, oxygen, or gold. A molecule is two or more atoms joined by chemical bonds. A compound is a substance made of two or more elements, such as water, which is hydrogen and oxygen. A chemical reaction is a rearrangement of atoms that makes new substances. The periodic table arranges the elements by their properties and by the structure of their atoms. An acid is a substance that gives off hydrogen ions in water, while a base takes them up. A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up itself. A bond is the link between atoms, and the main kinds are covalent, in which atoms share electrons, and ionic, in which one atom gives an electron to another. The pH scale measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is, from 0 to 14, with 7 as neutral. Oxidation is the loss of electrons, and reduction is the gain of them, and the two always happen together.

How it works: Chemistry works by studying how atoms join and separate. Atoms bond by sharing or transferring electrons, the tiny charged particles around the nucleus. When atoms rearrange, a chemical reaction happens, and energy is taken in or given off. Chemists write reactions as equations, showing what goes in and what comes out, and they balance them so that every atom is accounted for. In the laboratory, chemists mix substances, heat or cool them, and measure what happens, using instruments such as spectrometers, which identify substances by the light they absorb or emit. A key tool is the mole, a unit for counting atoms and molecules: one mole contains about 6.022 times 10 to the 23rd particles, a number called Avogadro's number. With the mole, chemists can weigh out exact amounts of reactants and predict how much product a reaction will give. Chemists also use spectroscopy, the study of how matter absorbs and emits light, to identify substances without touching them. A spectrum is like a fingerprint: each element and each molecule has its own pattern of lines.

Worked examples: When iron is left in damp air, it reacts with oxygen and water to form rust, a new substance with different properties. When vinegar, which contains acetic acid, is mixed with baking soda, it fizzes, giving off carbon dioxide gas. Burning wood is a chemical reaction: the wood combines with oxygen and gives off heat, light, carbon dioxide, and ash. Table salt is sodium chloride, a compound of sodium, a metal that reacts violently with water, and chlorine, a poisonous gas, yet together they form a safe crystal that flavours food. The air we breathe is about 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen, with small amounts of other gases. The reaction of hydrogen and oxygen makes water and releases energy, which is why hydrogen is used as a rocket fuel. The rusting of iron is slow oxidation, and it can be prevented by painting the iron or by coating it with zinc, a process called galvanising.

Real applications: Chemistry is used to make medicines, fertilisers, plastics, paints, and fuels. It purifies water, tests food, and cleans the environment. In forensics, chemistry identifies substances at a crime scene. In cooking, chemistry explains why bread rises and why meat browns. In energy, chemistry builds better batteries and solar cells. In medicine, chemistry designs drugs that target particular molecules in the body. Almost every manufactured product, from a phone screen to a pair of shoes, depends on chemistry at some point in its making. Chemistry is also used in the kitchen: baking powder releases carbon dioxide when it is heated, which makes a cake rise, and the browning of bread is a chemical reaction between sugars and proteins, called the Maillard reaction.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is chemistry the same as alchemy? No. Alchemy was a mixture of craft, philosophy, and mysticism; chemistry is a science based on experiment and measurement, though it inherited tools and ideas from alchemy. Are chemicals dangerous? Some are, and some are not: water is a chemical, and so is table salt, while some chemicals are poisonous. The word chemical does not mean artificial or harmful. Can chemistry create new elements? Yes, in a sense: elements heavier than uranium are made in laboratories and in nuclear reactors, though they are often unstable and decay quickly. Is chemistry only done in laboratories? No. Chemistry happens everywhere, in cooking, in the body, in the soil, and in the atmosphere. Do chemists only work with liquids? No. They work with solids, liquids, gases, and plasmas, and with materials at the scale of single molecules. Can chemists make gold? They can make tiny amounts of gold in a particle accelerator, by changing one element into another, but the cost is far greater than the value of the gold, so it is not a practical way to make it. Is chemistry the same as pharmacy? No. Pharmacy uses chemistry to make and dispense medicines, but chemistry is wider, covering all matter and all reactions.

Biology — What Biology Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000017 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Biology is the study of life: what living things are, how they work, how they change over time, and how they relate to one another and to their surroundings. It covers everything from a single cell to a whole forest, from a virus to a whale. Biology asks how organisms grow, reproduce, eat, move, sense, and die, and how the diversity of life, millions of species, came to be. It is a science of observation and experiment, and it draws on chemistry, physics, and mathematics to explain living systems. Biology is also a historical science: it explains the present by the past, because the features of a living thing, such as the shape of a bird's wing, are the result of a long history of change.

History: Biology began with observation. Aristotle, in the 300s BCE, described hundreds of animals and grouped them by their features. In the 1600s, the microscope revealed a new world: Robert Hooke saw cells in cork in 1665, and Antonie van Leeuwenhoek saw micro-organisms in water. In 1735, Carl Linnaeus published a system for naming and classifying all living things, still used today. In 1859, Charles Darwin published On the Origin of Species, arguing that species change by natural selection, the survival of those best fitted to their environment. In the 1860s, Gregor Mendel discovered the laws of inheritance by breeding pea plants, though his work was not recognised until 1900. In 1953, James Watson, Francis Crick, Rosalind Franklin, and Maurice Wilkins contributed to the discovery of the double helix structure of DNA, the molecule that carries genetic information. In the 1900s, biology also learned how cells make energy, how the immune system works, and how ecosystems function. Today, biology reads whole genomes, edits genes, and studies the microbiome, the community of microbes that lives in and on the human body. In the 1900s, biology became molecular. The discovery of the structure of DNA in 1953 led to the reading of whole genomes, and in 2003 the Human Genome Project completed the first full reading of human DNA. In the 1970s, genetic engineering began, letting scientists move genes between organisms. In the 2010s, the gene-editing tool CRISPR made it possible to change DNA with new precision. Ecology also grew, as biologists studied how whole systems, from coral reefs to rainforests, respond to change.

Key concepts: A cell is the smallest unit of life; all living things are made of one or more cells. Evolution is change in the inherited features of a population over generations, driven mainly by natural selection. A gene is a section of DNA that carries instructions for making a protein. An ecosystem is a community of organisms together with their physical environment. Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants use sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide to make food and oxygen. Homeostasis is the keeping of stable conditions inside the body, such as temperature and blood sugar. A species is a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. Biodiversity is the variety of life, at the level of genes, species, and ecosystems. A protein is a large molecule made of amino acids, and it does most of the work in a cell, from building structures to carrying signals. DNA is the molecule that stores genetic information, in the order of its four bases. A mutation is a change in DNA, which can be harmless, harmful, or useful, and it is the raw material of evolution.

How it works: Biology works by linking structure to function at many levels. At the smallest level, molecules such as DNA carry instructions, proteins do the work, and chemical reactions release energy. At the level of the cell, organelles, the parts inside a cell, each do a job: the nucleus holds the DNA, the mitochondria make energy, and the membrane controls what enters and leaves. At the level of the organism, organs work together in systems, such as the digestive system or the nervous system. At the level of the population, organisms compete, cooperate, and evolve, and at the level of the ecosystem, energy flows from the Sun through plants to animals and decomposers. Biologists study these levels by observation in the field, by experiment in the laboratory, and by comparison, asking how a feature in one species relates to the same feature in another. Biologists also use the comparative method: by comparing the same feature in many species, they can tell which features are old and which are new, and how a feature has changed over time. A fossil, the preserved remains of an ancient organism, is the direct evidence of that history.

Worked examples: A human red blood cell carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues and has no nucleus, which leaves more room for the haemoglobin that binds the oxygen. A bee pollinates flowers while collecting nectar, a partnership that benefits both the plant and the insect. The peppered moth changed colour in industrial England, from light to dark, because dark moths were better hidden on soot-covered trees, a visible case of natural selection. A bacterium divides into two every twenty minutes in good conditions, so one cell can become millions in a day. A forest is an ecosystem in which trees, fungi, insects, and soil organisms exchange nutrients and energy. The human heart beats about 100,000 times a day, pumping blood through about 100,000 kilometres of blood vessels. A single oak tree can support hundreds of species of insects, birds, and fungi, which is why it is called a keystone species in its ecosystem.

Real applications: Biology underlies medicine, from understanding infections to designing vaccines. It guides farming, through the breeding of crops and animals and the control of pests. It protects nature, through conservation and the management of reserves. It informs forensics, where DNA identifies people, and archaeology, where ancient DNA reveals migrations. It also helps answer everyday questions: why we get sick, why children resemble their parents, and how the food we eat becomes energy. Biotechnology, the use of living things to make products, is built on biology. Biology is also used in conservation: by studying how many individuals a species needs to survive, biologists decide how large a reserve must be and how it should be managed.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is evolution only a theory? In everyday speech, theory can mean a guess, but in science a theory is a well-tested explanation. The theory of evolution is supported by fossils, by DNA, by observed change in living populations, and by experiments. Are humans still evolving? Yes. Human populations continue to change, for example in the spread of genes for lactose tolerance in adults. Is a virus alive? That is debated: viruses reproduce and evolve, but they cannot do so on their own, and they have no metabolism, so some biologists call them non-living. Can biology explain everything about life? It explains a great deal, but questions about the meaning of life and the nature of consciousness are also philosophical questions. Is all life related? According to the evidence, yes: all known life shares the same genetic code and the same basic chemistry, which points to a common ancestor. How did life begin? That is not known with certainty. Scientists have ideas, such as life beginning in warm shallow pools or at deep-sea vents, but the exact origin of life remains an open question, and it is studied by chemistry and geology as well as by biology. Can a species come back from extinction? Not yet, though scientists have discussed bringing back the mammoth by editing the DNA of elephants, and the idea raises questions about whether it should be done.

Physics — What Physics Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000018 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Physics is the study of matter, energy, space, and time, and of the rules that govern them. It asks the most basic questions: what is the world made of, how do things move, and why do they move that way? Physics covers the very small, atoms and particles, the very large, planets and galaxies, and everything in between. It works by observation, experiment, and mathematics: physicists measure the world, build theories that explain the measurements, and test the theories with new experiments. Physics is the oldest and most fundamental of the natural sciences, and its laws underlie chemistry, biology, and engineering. Physics is also a science of scales: its laws must work for a single atom and for a whole galaxy, and the search for laws that work at every scale is one of its great goals.

History: Physics began with the Greeks, who asked what matter is made of and how things move; Aristotle's ideas about motion dominated for two thousand years. In the 1600s, Galileo showed by experiment that falling bodies accelerate at the same rate whatever their weight, and that the Earth moves around the Sun. In 1687, Isaac Newton published the Principia, giving the laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation, which explained both falling apples and the orbits of the planets. In the 1800s, James Clerk Maxwell united electricity and magnetism in four equations, predicting radio waves, and thermodynamics explained heat and energy. In the early 1900s, physics was transformed twice: Einstein's relativity changed the understanding of space, time, and gravity, in 1905 and 1915, and quantum mechanics, built by Planck, Bohr, Heisenberg, and others, described the strange rules of the very small. In the 1900s, physicists discovered the structure of the atom, the nucleus, and the particles that make up matter, and built the Standard Model. Today, physics studies dark matter, dark energy, and the search for a theory that unites gravity with quantum mechanics. In the 1900s, physics also changed technology. The transistor, invented in 1947, made modern electronics possible, and the laser, invented in 1960, is now used in medicine, in communications, and in industry. In 2012, the Higgs boson, a particle predicted in the 1960s, was found at the Large Hadron Collider near Geneva, confirming a central part of the Standard Model. In 2015, gravitational waves, predicted by Einstein a century earlier, were detected for the first time, opening a new way of observing the universe.

Key concepts: Force is a push or pull that changes the motion of an object, measured in newtons. Energy is the capacity to do work, and it takes many forms: kinetic, potential, heat, and light. Matter is what things are made of, built from atoms and their parts. A field is a region where a force acts, such as the gravitational field around the Earth or the electric field around a charge. Momentum is mass times velocity, and it is conserved in collisions. A wave is a disturbance that carries energy without carrying matter, such as a sound wave or a light wave. Entropy is a measure of disorder, and the second law of thermodynamics says that the entropy of an isolated system tends to increase. Relativity is the theory that space and time depend on the observer's motion and on gravity. A particle is a small piece of matter, such as an electron or a proton, and the Standard Model lists the particles known to physics. A wave is a pattern that carries energy through space or through a medium, and light is an electromagnetic wave. Gravity is the attraction between masses, described by Newton as a force and by Einstein as the curving of space and time.

How it works: Physics works by measuring, modelling, and testing. A physicist observes a phenomenon, such as the swing of a pendulum, and measures it carefully, recording numbers. A model is then built, usually in mathematics, that describes the pattern and predicts what will happen in new cases. The model is tested by experiment: if its predictions match the measurements, it gains support; if not, it is changed or dropped. A key tool is the controlled experiment, in which one factor is changed while others are kept fixed. Another is the thought experiment, an imagined case that tests an idea. Physicists also use large instruments, such as particle accelerators, which smash particles together at high energies, and telescopes, which gather light from distant objects. The results are shared and checked by other physicists, and over time the best models become theories, such as the theory of general relativity. Physicists also use the principle of conservation: certain quantities, such as energy, momentum, and electric charge, are never created or destroyed in a closed system, only moved or changed, and these rules are among the most reliable in all of science.

Worked examples: A ball thrown upward slows, stops, and falls back, because gravity pulls it down at about 9.81 metres per second squared near the Earth's surface. A car braking converts its kinetic energy, the energy of motion, into heat in the brakes. A magnet attracts iron because of the magnetic field around it, and an electric current in a wire creates a magnetic field, which is how electric motors work. A rainbow forms when sunlight is bent and split into colours by raindrops. A microwave oven heats food by making water molecules vibrate with electromagnetic waves. The Global Positioning System works only because engineers correct for the effects of Einstein's relativity on the clocks in satellites. A compass needle points north because the Earth has a magnetic field, made by the movement of molten iron in its core. A prism splits white light into a rainbow because different colours are bent by different amounts when they pass through glass.

Real applications: Physics is used to design bridges, aircraft, and spacecraft, to generate electricity, and to build computers, lasers, and medical scanners. It underlies weather forecasting, satellite navigation, and the internet, which began as a tool for physicists to share data. In medicine, physics gives us X-rays, MRI, and radiotherapy. In energy, it guides the design of solar cells, nuclear reactors, and fusion experiments. Physics also answers the deepest questions about the universe, from the smallest particles to the largest structures, and it trains a way of thinking that is useful in every technical field. Physics is also used in the home: the microwave oven, the refrigerator, and the light bulb all work by the laws of physics, and the study of heat, called thermodynamics, explains why a fridge can make things cold by using energy.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is physics only about mathematics? Mathematics is its main language, but physics is also about experiment: a theory that does not match measurement is wrong, however beautiful its mathematics. Can physics explain everything? It explains a great deal about matter and energy, but it does not answer questions about meaning, value, or consciousness, which belong to other fields. Is quantum mechanics just weirdness? Its rules are strange, but its predictions are the most precisely tested in all of science, and it underlies modern electronics. Will physics ever be finished? Some physicists hope for a final theory, but most expect new questions to keep appearing, as they have for four hundred years. Is physics hard? It can be, but its core ideas, such as force, energy, and waves, can be understood with everyday examples, and this record starts there. What is time? In everyday life, time seems to flow evenly, but relativity shows that time passes more slowly for a moving clock and near a strong gravitational field, and the nature of time is still debated. Is the universe deterministic? In classical physics, the future follows from the present, but quantum mechanics says that some events are genuinely random, and physicists still debate what that means.

Literature — What Literature Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000019 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Literature is writing that is valued as art: novels, poems, plays, and stories that are read for their language, their imagination, and the insight they give into human life. The word is also used more widely for all written works, but in this record it means creative writing. Literature uses the resources of language, rhythm, image, and story, to show how people live, love, suffer, and hope. It is found in every culture, in many languages, and in forms that range from a four-line poem to a thousand-page novel. Literature is also a social act: books are discussed in clubs, plays are performed for audiences, and poems are read aloud at gatherings, so that reading is often a shared experience, not a solitary one.

History: Literature began with oral storytelling, long before writing: epics, myths, and songs were passed from memory to memory. The first written literature appears in Mesopotamia, with the Epic of Gilgamesh, and in Egypt, with tales and poems on papyrus. In Greece, Homer's epics were written down around the 700s BCE, and in the 400s BCE Athens produced the tragedies of Sophocles and Euripides and the comedies of Aristophanes. In China, the Book of Songs collected poems from the 1000s to the 600s BCE, and in India the Mahabharata and Ramayana told vast stories of gods and heroes. In medieval Europe, Dante wrote the Divine Comedy in Italian in the 1300s, and Chaucer wrote the Canterbury Tales in English in the 1300s. The printing press, from about 1450, made books cheap and spread reading widely. In the 1700s and 1800s, the novel became the dominant form, with writers such as Austen, Dickens, and Tolstoy. In the 1900s, literature became global, with writers from Africa, Asia, and Latin America winning world audiences, and new forms, such as the graphic novel, appeared. Today, literature is written in many languages and read on paper and on screens. In the 1900s, literature changed with the world. The two world wars produced writing about loss, memory, and the search for meaning, and the end of empires produced new literatures in Africa, Asia, and the Caribbean, written in English, French, and local languages. In the late 1900s, writers such as Gabriel Garcia Marquez, in Latin America, mixed the ordinary and the fantastic in a style called magical realism. Today, literature is written and read on screens, and new forms, such as the web serial and the interactive story, are appearing.

Key concepts: A genre is a type of literature, such as the novel, the poem, or the tragedy. Narrative is the telling of a story, including who tells it and in what order. Character is a person in a work, created by the writer's description and dialogue. A metaphor is a figure of speech that says one thing is another, such as calling time a thief. Theme is a central idea or question of a work, such as love, power, or freedom. Plot is the sequence of events in a story, including its causes and its turning points. Style is the distinctive way a writer uses language. A narrator is the voice that tells a story, which may be a character in it or an outside observer. A symbol is an object or image that stands for something else, such as a white whale standing for obsession in Moby-Dick. Irony is the gap between what is said and what is meant, or between what is expected and what happens. A point of view is the position from which a story is told, such as first person, I, or third person, he or she.

How it works: Literature works through language shaped with care. A writer chooses words, images, and rhythms to create a world that the reader enters by imagination. A story is built from character, people with wants and flaws, plot, the sequence of events, and setting, the time and place. A poem works differently: it uses sound, image, and compression, saying much in few words. A play is written to be performed, so it works through dialogue and action on a stage. The reader completes the work: reading is an active process of filling in gaps, guessing motives, and feeling with the characters. Interpretation is the work of explaining what a text means, and readers can disagree, because a text can support more than one reading. Critics study how a text is made, what it meant in its time, and what it means now. Literature also works through sound: a poem is meant to be heard, and its rhythm, rhyme, and alliteration, the repetition of sounds, are part of its meaning. A novelist, too, pays attention to the sound of sentences, and a speech in a play must work when spoken by an actor.

Worked examples: The Epic of Gilgamesh, written in Mesopotamia more than four thousand years ago, tells of a king who seeks immortality after the death of his friend. Homer's Odyssey, composed in ancient Greece, follows Odysseus on his ten-year journey home from Troy. Shakespeare's Hamlet, first performed around 1600, is a play about a prince who cannot decide whether to avenge his father's murder. Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, published in 1813, is a novel about love, money, and reputation in English society. Chinua Achebe's Things Fall Apart, published in 1958, tells of a Nigerian village changed by colonial rule. A haiku, a Japanese poem of three lines with five, seven, and five syllables, can hold a whole season in seventeen syllables. The opening line of Pride and Prejudice, It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife, is famous for its irony, because the novel soon shows that it is the women who are in want of a husband. The first line of Moby-Dick, Call me Ishmael, is one of the most quoted openings in literature.

Real applications: Literature is read for pleasure, and it is studied in schools and universities around the world. It preserves the memory of cultures and gives voice to people whose stories would otherwise be lost. It trains empathy, by letting readers live inside other minds, and it trains language, by showing what words can do. Writers, journalists, lawyers, and politicians all learn from literature. Films, plays, and songs are often based on books, and the stories of literature shape how societies see themselves. In short, literature is one of the main ways human beings understand themselves. Literature is also used in the law: judges and lawyers study how stories are told, because a trial is, in part, a contest between two stories about what happened.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is literature only fiction? No. Poetry, drama, and essays are also literature, and some non-fiction, such as memoirs, is valued as literature too. Must literature be old to be good? No. New works become classics, and old works were once new; what matters is the quality of the writing and the depth of the insight. Can a translation be literature? Yes. A good translation is itself a work of art, though some of the original's music is always lost. Is literature only for entertainment? It entertains, but it also teaches, consoles, and challenges, and many readers value it most for what it helps them understand. Do you need to study literature to enjoy it? No. Reading for pleasure is the foundation, and study adds context, but it is not required to enjoy a good story or poem. Is a book better than its film? That is a matter of taste, but a film and a book are different arts: a film shows, while a book tells, and each can do things the other cannot. Can literature be bad? Yes. A work can be poorly written, dishonest, or dull, and critics exist to say so, though readers often disagree with them.

Architecture — What Architecture Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000020 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Architecture is the art and science of designing buildings and other structures, and of the spaces around them. An architect designs a building to serve a purpose, such as a home, a school, a temple, or a station, to stand safely, and to be beautiful. Architecture is both a practical craft, concerned with structure, materials, and cost, and an art, concerned with form, light, and meaning. It is one of the oldest arts, and its works, from huts to cathedrals, are among the largest objects human beings make. Architecture is also the design of the spaces between buildings: streets, squares, and parks are part of architecture, and a city is, in a sense, a single work of architecture made by many hands over many years.

History: Architecture began with shelter: huts of wood, mud, and stone, built to keep out weather and wild animals. In Egypt, builders raised the pyramids around 2560 BCE, the largest stone structures of their time. In Greece, architects developed the orders, systems of columns and proportions, Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian, that are still used today. In Rome, the arch, the vault, and concrete allowed larger spans, and the Pantheon, built around 125 CE, has a dome 43 metres across. In the Middle Ages, builders raised Gothic cathedrals with pointed arches and stained glass, and in the Islamic world they built mosques with domes and minarets. In the Renaissance, from the 1400s, architects such as Brunelleschi, who built the dome of Florence Cathedral, studied Roman ruins and wrote treatises on proportion. In the 1800s, iron and glass made new forms possible, such as railway stations and exhibition halls. In the 1900s, steel and concrete made skyscrapers possible, and modernist architects such as Le Corbusier and Frank Lloyd Wright argued for simple forms and honest materials. Today, architecture also concerns sustainability, designing buildings that use less energy and last longer. In the 1900s, architecture changed with new materials and new ideas. The skyscraper, made possible by steel and the lift, rose in Chicago and New York. The Bauhaus, a school in Germany in the 1920s, taught that form should follow function, and its ideas spread around the world. After the Second World War, whole cities were rebuilt, and new towns were planned from scratch. In the 2000s, architects began to design for sustainability, using sun, wind, and rain to heat, cool, and power buildings, and to design buildings that can be taken apart and reused.

Key concepts: Structure is how a building stands up, through walls, columns, beams, arches, and domes. Function is what a building is for and how people use it. Form is the shape and appearance of a building. A material is what a building is made of, such as stone, brick, timber, steel, or glass. Proportion is the relation of the parts to each other and to the whole. Light is a material in architecture: windows, courtyards, and skylights shape how a space feels. Space is the volume inside and around a building, which the architect designs as carefully as the walls. Style is the shared way of building of a period or culture, such as Gothic or modernist. An arch is a curved structure that spans an opening, and a dome is an arch turned in a circle, covering a space. A buttress is a support built against a wall, and a flying buttress is one that stands apart from the wall and reaches it by an arch. A facade is the front of a building, often its most decorated side.

How it works: Architecture begins with a brief: what the building is for, who will use it, and what it may cost. The architect studies the site, its climate, its ground, and its surroundings, and then makes a design, a plan of the spaces and their arrangement. The design must solve three problems at once: structure, how the building stands up; function, how people move through it and use it; and form, how it looks and feels. Drawings and models are made, and engineers check the structure, the services, and the safety. Then builders construct it, and the architect watches the work. After it is finished, the building is used, and its success is judged by how well it serves its users over years and decades. A building that is hard to use, expensive to run, or quick to decay is a failure, however striking it looks. Architecture also uses models: a physical model, made of wood or card, or a digital model, made on a computer, lets the architect and the client see the building before it is built, and lets the engineer test how it will stand and how it will be lit.

Worked examples: The Parthenon in Athens, built in the 400s BCE, is a temple of marble whose columns are slightly curved to look straight to the eye. The Great Mosque of Cordoba, begun in the 700s, is famous for its forest of arches, red and white, stretching in all directions. The Gothic cathedral of Chartres, built in the 1100s and 1200s, uses pointed arches and flying buttresses to raise stone walls to great heights and fill them with stained glass. The Taj Mahal, built in India in the 1600s, is a tomb of white marble set in a garden, and is one of the most visited buildings in the world. The Sydney Opera House, opened in 1973, uses shell-shaped roofs to make a concert hall that is also a landmark. The Eiffel Tower, built in Paris in 1889, is made of iron and stands 330 metres tall, and it was at first criticised as ugly, then became the symbol of the city. The Guggenheim Museum in Bilbao, opened in 1997, is a building of curved titanium that changed the fortunes of its city.

Real applications: Architecture shapes daily life: the design of a home affects comfort and health, the design of a school affects learning, and the design of a hospital affects healing. It shapes cities, through streets, squares, and skylines. It preserves memory, in historic buildings and monuments. It serves the environment, through buildings that use less energy and less water. It also serves the economy, through offices, shops, and factories. Good architecture is practical, safe, and beautiful at once, and bad architecture is felt by everyone who uses it. Architecture is also used in the design of prisons, schools, and hospitals, where the layout of the building affects how people behave and how they feel.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is architecture art or engineering? It is both: it must stand up and work, which is engineering, and it must be beautiful and meaningful, which is art. Can a building be beautiful but bad? Yes. A building that leaks, overheats, or is hard to use is a failure, even if it photographs well. Do architects only design buildings? No. They also design interiors, landscapes, and whole districts, and they advise on the reuse of old buildings. Is old architecture better than new? That is a matter of taste and of purpose: old buildings were built with different materials and for different needs, and each period has produced both fine and poor work. Can anyone be an architect? In most countries, the title is protected by law, and architects must be trained and licensed, because their work affects public safety. Should old buildings be preserved? That is debated: some argue that every old building is part of history, while others argue that only the best should be kept, and that cities must change. Can a building be too tall? Engineers can build very tall, but the cost, the wind, and the effect on the city below all set limits, and some cities limit height by law.

Education — What Education Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000021 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Education is the process by which people learn knowledge, skills, values, and ways of thinking. It happens in schools and universities, but also at home, at work, and in everyday life: a child learning to speak, a worker learning a new machine, and an adult learning a language are all being educated. History: Education is ancient. In Egypt, scribes were trained in schools attached to temples, learning to write hieroglyphs. Assessment, through tests, essays, or practical work, shows what has been learned and what needs more work. Schools also teach more than lessons: they teach how to work with others, how to follow rules, and how to take part in a community. The best education, according to many studies, combines clear teaching, regular practice, feedback, and the learner's own curiosity. This is why good teachers try to make lessons interesting and to give feedback that helps, not only marks that judge.

Worked examples: A primary school teaches a child to read, write, and count, skills used every day for the rest of life. An apprenticeship teaches a craft by working beside an experienced person, as a carpenter learns from a master. A literacy programme for adults teaches reading to people who missed school as children, opening up jobs and independence. A museum teaches by showing objects, and a library teaches by lending books to anyone who asks. A driving test is a form of assessment: it checks, by a practical test and a theory test, whether a person can drive safely. A medical degree takes years of study and practice, and it ends with examinations that test both knowledge and skill.

Real applications: Education is used everywhere: schools teach reading, writing, and arithmetic; universities train doctors, engineers, and teachers; and workplaces train new skills. It supports health, because educated people live longer and make better health choices, according to many studies. It supports democracy, because citizens need knowledge to vote well. It supports the economy, because skilled workers earn more and produce more. It also preserves culture, passing on language, history, and art to the next generation. For the individual, education opens choices: of work, of where to live, and of how to live. Education is also used in public health: campaigns that teach people about clean water, about vaccination, and about healthy food are a form of education, and they save lives.

Does more schooling always mean better education? Not necessarily: what matters is what is learned, and a short, well-taught course can teach more than years of poor teaching. Can anyone be taught anything? People differ in their talents and in how fast they learn, but almost everyone can learn a great deal with good teaching and enough time. Is education only for the young? No. Adults learn new skills throughout life, and many countries now support adult education and retraining. Does education guarantee a good job? No. It improves the chances, but jobs also depend on the economy, on connections, and on luck. Should education be free? In many countries, primary and secondary education are free, and the question of whether university should be free is debated, with some countries charging fees and others not. Is homework useful? Studies suggest that a moderate amount helps, especially for older students, but too much can be counterproductive, and the evidence is still debated.

Sport — What Sport Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000022 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Sport is organised physical activity, played by rules, usually in competition, for exercise, skill, and enjoyment. It includes games such as football, tennis, and cricket, races such as running and swimming, and contests such as boxing and gymnastics. Sport is different from play, because it has fixed rules and a clear outcome, and different from exercise, because it is usually competitive. It is played by amateurs for fun and by professionals for a living, and it is watched by billions of people around the world. Sport is one of the most widespread human activities, and it is found in every country. Sport is also a language: a goal, a try, or a home run needs no translation, and fans of different countries can watch the same match and understand it, even if they share no words.

History: Sport is ancient. In Egypt, wrestling and archery were practised thousands of years ago. In Greece, the Olympic Games began, by tradition, in 776 BCE, and were held every four years at Olympia in honour of Zeus; athletes competed in running, wrestling, and chariot racing. In Rome, gladiatorial contests and chariot races drew huge crowds. In medieval Europe, tournaments, archery, and folk football were popular. In the 1800s, sport was organised in Britain: clubs and associations wrote the rules of football, rugby, cricket, and tennis, and the modern Olympic Games were revived in Athens in 1896. In the 1900s, sport became professional and global: the football World Cup began in 1930, television brought sport into homes, and women, long excluded, won a place in most sports. Today, sport is a global industry, with leagues, sponsors, and audiences in the billions, and new sports, such as esports, are debated as sports in their own right. In the 1900s, sport became a business and a spectacle. The football World Cup, first held in 1930, became the most watched event in the world. The Olympic Games grew from a few hundred athletes in 1896 to more than ten thousand today. In the 1960s and 1970s, television money changed sport, and players began to earn large salaries. Women, long excluded from many sports, won the right to compete in the Olympic marathon in 1984, and in football, the Women's World Cup, first held in 1991, now draws large audiences. In the 2000s, the Paralympic Games, for athletes with disabilities, gained a large following.

Key concepts: Competition is the contest between players or teams to win. Rules are the agreed limits on what may be done in a sport. Training is the regular practice that builds skill and fitness. Fair play is the spirit of respecting the rules, the officials, and the opponent. A league is an organised competition over a season, with a table of results. A tournament is a contest, often with knock-out rounds, to find a champion. A referee or umpire is the official who enforces the rules during a contest. Amateur sport is played for enjoyment, while professional sport is played for pay. A team is a group of players who work together, and a captain is the player who leads them. A score is the number of points, goals, or runs in a contest, and a ranking is a list that orders players or teams by their results. Doping is the use of banned substances to improve performance, and it is against the rules of most sports.

How it works: A sport works through rules, skill, and competition. The rules say what players may and may not do, how points are scored, and what happens when a rule is broken. The players train to build the skills the sport needs: strength, speed, endurance, coordination, and tactics. In a contest, players or teams try to win within the rules, and officials, such as referees and umpires, judge what happens. Winning depends on skill, on preparation, and on chance, and the result is recorded as a score, a time, or a ranking. Leagues and tournaments organise many contests into a season, with a champion at the end. Behind the contest are institutions: clubs, federations, and governing bodies that set the rules, organise events, and settle disputes. Fair play, the idea that players should respect the rules and each other, is a central value of sport. Sport also works through money: clubs earn from tickets, from television, and from sponsors, and they spend on players, on stadiums, and on training. The balance between money and sport is a constant debate, because a club that spends too much can fail, and a sport that sells too much can lose its spirit.

Worked examples: Association football, the world's most popular sport, is played by two teams of eleven, who try to score goals by moving a ball with their feet. A marathon is a race of 42.195 kilometres, a distance set by the Olympic Games of 1908 and standardised in 1921. Tennis is scored in games and sets, with a system of 15, 30, 40 that dates back centuries, for reasons historians still debate. Cricket, played mainly in the Commonwealth, can last five days in its Test form, or three hours in its Twenty20 form. The Olympic Games, held every four years, bring together thousands of athletes in dozens of sports, from archery to wrestling. In 1954, Roger Bannister ran a mile in under four minutes, a feat once thought impossible, and within a few years many others had done the same. In 1968, Dick Fosbury won the Olympic high jump with a new technique, jumping backwards over the bar, which is now used by almost every high jumper.

Real applications: Sport is used in schools to teach children to move, to cooperate, and to handle winning and losing. It is used in health, because regular exercise protects the heart, the bones, and the mind. It is used in diplomacy, as when the Olympic Games bring together athletes from nearly every country. It is a large industry, employing players, coaches, officials, journalists, and many others. It is also a source of identity and pride: people support the teams of their city or country, and a victory can lift a whole community. Above all, sport is played for joy. Sport is also used in rehabilitation: after an injury, a patient may use sport to rebuild strength and confidence, under the guidance of a doctor or a physiotherapist.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is sport only about winning? No. Most people play sport for health, friendship, and fun, and winning is only part of it. Is chess a sport? That is debated: chess is competitive and organised, but it is not physical, so some call it a mind sport and others call it a game. Are esports sports? The same debate applies, and the answer depends on the definition used. Is sport good for health? Regular, moderate sport is good for health, but elite sport can cause injury, and the balance between benefit and risk is studied by sports medicine. Do sports build character? They can teach discipline, teamwork, and respect, but they can also teach cheating and aggression, so much depends on how the sport is coached and played. Is sport political? It can be: governments use sport to build national pride, and athletes sometimes protest, so sport and politics often meet. Is sport too commercial? Many fans think so, pointing to high ticket prices and large salaries, while others argue that money has improved stadiums, training, and the quality of play. Should children specialise in one sport early? Most experts say no: children benefit from playing many sports, and early specialisation can lead to injury and to losing interest.

Technology — What Technology Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000023 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Technology is the use of knowledge, tools, and systems to solve practical problems and to extend human abilities. A technology can be a simple tool, such as a hammer, or a complex system, such as the internet, which joins millions of computers. Technology includes the objects themselves and the knowledge of how to make and use them. It is different from science, which seeks understanding, and from engineering, which designs solutions: technology is the whole body of tools, methods, and systems that a society has at its disposal. Every human society has technology, from stone tools to smartphones. Technology is also a record of human ingenuity: every tool, from a stone axe to a satellite, shows what people knew and what they could do at the time it was made.

History: Technology began with stone tools, made more than three million years ago, and with fire, controlled by humans hundreds of thousands of years ago. Farming, from about 10,000 BCE, was a technology that changed how people lived, allowing towns and then cities. The wheel, writing, and the smelting of copper and then iron, spread across the ancient world. In the Middle Ages, the plough, the windmill, and the water mill increased food and power in Europe and Asia. The printing press, from about 1450, spread books and ideas. The Industrial Revolution, beginning in Britain in the 1700s, brought the steam engine, the factory, and the railway, and changed work, cities, and trade. In the 1800s, electricity, the telegraph, and the telephone shrank distance. In the 1900s, the aeroplane, the radio, television, the computer, and the space rocket each changed the world in turn. In the 2000s, the mobile phone and the internet put computing and communication in the pockets of billions of people, and artificial intelligence began to change how work is done. In the 1900s, technology accelerated. The aeroplane, first flown in 1903, made the world smaller. The transistor, invented in 1947, made computers possible, and the integrated circuit, invented in 1958, put thousands of transistors on a single chip. The first satellite, Sputnik, was launched in 1957, and the first person walked on the Moon in 1969. The World Wide Web, invented in 1989, made the internet easy to use, and the smartphone, from 2007, put it in the pocket of billions. In the 2020s, artificial intelligence began to write, to draw, and to drive, raising new questions about work and about truth.

Key concepts: A tool is an object used to extend human abilities, such as a hammer or a computer. A system is a set of parts working together, such as a railway or the internet. Innovation is the creation of a new technology or a new use for an old one. Adoption is the process by which people take up a new technology. A network is a set of connected points, such as computers joined by the internet. Automation is the use of machines to do work without direct human control. Digital means represented by numbers, usually in binary form, as in a computer. A platform is a technology on which other technologies are built, such as an operating system or a marketplace. A patent is a legal right that protects an invention for a limited time. A standard is an agreed rule, such as the size of a screw or the format of a file, that lets different technologies work together. An interface is the point where a person meets a machine, such as a keyboard, a screen, or a voice command.

How it works: A technology works by turning knowledge into a working system. It begins with a problem, such as moving water, sending a message, or storing food. Knowledge from science and from craft is used to design a solution, and the solution is built from materials and parts. The technology is then tested, used, and improved, often in many small steps, until it is reliable and affordable. Adoption follows: people learn to use it, and it spreads if it is useful and if the cost is right. Technologies also combine: the smartphone joins the telephone, the camera, the computer, and the satellite, and each part depends on the others. A technology can also have side effects that were not intended, such as pollution from engines or the spread of misinformation on social media, and societies then have to manage those effects with new rules and new technologies. Technology also spreads by imitation: when one country or company succeeds with a new technology, others copy it, adapt it, and improve it, which is why the same invention often appears in several places at once. The history of technology is full of such parallel inventions, and of disputes about who was first.

Worked examples: The wheel, invented more than five thousand years ago, made it possible to move heavy loads and to build carts and chariots. The printing press, from about 1450, made books cheap and spread knowledge across Europe. The steam engine, improved by James Watt in the 1700s, powered factories, trains, and ships. The telephone, patented in 1876, let people speak across cities and then across oceans. The internet, built from the 1960s onward, joins computers around the world, so that a message can travel from one side of the planet to the other in a fraction of a second. A smartphone joins a telephone, a camera, a map, and a computer in one small device. The steam locomotive, first run in 1804, could pull a load at speeds that seemed frightening at the time, and within fifty years railways joined the cities of Europe and America. The electric light bulb, developed by Edison and others in the 1870s, replaced gas lamps and changed how people lived after dark.

Real applications: Technology is used in every part of life: in medicine, to diagnose and treat; in farming, to plant and harvest; in transport, to move people and goods; in communication, to speak across the world; and in learning, to teach and to study. It creates jobs and destroys them, raises living standards, and creates new problems, such as pollution and the spread of false information. Governments use technology to provide services and to defend their countries. Individuals use it to work, to shop, to play, and to keep in touch. The study of technology is therefore also the study of how societies change. Technology is also used in the home: a washing machine, a refrigerator, and a vacuum cleaner have saved more hours of labour than almost any other inventions, and they changed who does the work of the home.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is technology always good? No. A technology can help and harm at the same time: the car gives freedom and causes pollution, and the internet gives knowledge and spreads falsehood. Does technology make life easier? Often, but it also creates new work, such as maintaining the systems it builds, and new demands, such as being reachable at all hours. Will technology replace human workers? It has replaced some jobs and created others, and economists debate how far this will go; the pattern so far is change, not the end of work. Is technology neutral? Not entirely: a technology is shaped by the people who design it and by the society that uses it, and it can carry their values and biases. Can a society refuse a technology? Sometimes: communities and governments have banned or limited technologies, such as certain weapons, but refusal is hard when a technology is widely useful. Does technology make people happier? The evidence is mixed: it has raised living standards and lengthened lives, but it has also brought new worries, from the loss of privacy to the fear of being replaced by machines. Can technology be stopped? Once a technology is useful, it is hard to stop, but societies have chosen to limit some technologies, such as chemical weapons, by treaty and by law.

Agriculture — What Agriculture Is

ID: JAH-SUBJ-000024 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Agriculture is the growing of crops and the raising of animals for food, fibre, and other products. It includes farming the land, keeping livestock, and managing soil, water, and seed. Agriculture began about ten thousand years ago, when people started to plant seeds and keep animals instead of only hunting and gathering, and it made towns, cities, and civilisations possible. Today, agriculture ranges from small family farms that feed one household to large farms that supply whole countries, and it uses science, machinery, and trade to produce food on a scale never seen before. Agriculture is also a science: it uses the knowledge of soil, of plants, of animals, and of the weather, and it is studied in universities and in research stations around the world.

History: Agriculture began in the Neolithic, about 10,000 BCE, when people in the Fertile Crescent, the region of the modern Middle East, began to plant wheat and barley and to keep sheep and goats. Farming spread to Egypt, India, China, and the Americas, where people domesticated rice, maize, and potatoes. In ancient times, farmers used the plough, irrigation, and crop rotation, planting different crops in turn to rest the soil. In the Middle Ages, the heavy plough and the three-field system increased yields in Europe. In the 1700s, new crops, such as the potato, and new methods, such as selective breeding, raised output. In the 1800s, chemical fertilisers and machines, such as the reaper, changed farming again. In the 1900s, the Green Revolution, from the 1960s, used new varieties of wheat and rice, fertilisers, and irrigation to raise yields greatly in Asia and Latin America, feeding hundreds of millions of people. Today, agriculture uses satellites, drones, and computers to manage fields, and it faces the challenge of feeding a growing population while protecting the soil, water, and climate. In the 1900s, agriculture changed faster than ever before. Tractors replaced horses, chemical fertilisers replaced manure, and pesticides protected crops from insects and disease. The Green Revolution, from the 1960s, used new varieties of wheat and rice, developed by Norman Borlaug and others, to raise yields in Asia and Latin America, and Borlaug was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. In the 2000s, precision farming used satellites and sensors to apply water and fertiliser only where they are needed, and the study of the soil's microbes opened a new field of research.

Key concepts: A crop is a plant grown for food, fibre, or other use, such as wheat or cotton. Livestock are farm animals, such as cattle, sheep, and chickens. Soil is the layer of earth in which plants grow, made of minerals, organic matter, water, and air. Irrigation is the bringing of water to land, by canals, pipes, or sprinklers. Yield is the amount of crop produced per unit of land, usually measured in tonnes per hectare. Crop rotation is the practice of growing different crops in the same field in turn, to rest the soil and control pests. Fertiliser is a substance added to the soil to supply nutrients. A harvest is the gathering of a ripe crop. A seed is the part of a plant from which a new plant grows, and a variety is a type of plant bred for particular qualities, such as yield or resistance to disease. A pest is an insect, a weed, or a disease that damages a crop, and a pesticide is a substance used to control it. Sustainability in farming means producing food without using up the soil, the water, or the climate on which farming depends.

How it works: Agriculture works by managing the cycle of growth. A farmer prepares the soil, by ploughing and adding nutrients, and plants seed at the right season. The crop grows using sunlight, water, and nutrients from the soil, and the farmer protects it from weeds, pests, and disease. At harvest, the crop is cut, gathered, and stored or sold. Livestock farming works in a similar cycle: animals are fed, sheltered, and bred, and they give milk, eggs, meat, or wool. Soil is the foundation: it holds water and nutrients, and it is alive with organisms that break down dead matter. Water is managed by rain, by irrigation, or by drainage. Modern farming adds science: soil testing, weather forecasts, and machines that plant and harvest with precision. The whole system depends on the seasons, and a bad season, drought or flood, can ruin a harvest. Agriculture also depends on the weather: a farmer watches the rain, the temperature, and the season, and the timing of planting and harvest is decided by them. A late frost can kill a crop, and a drought can ruin a harvest, which is why farming has always been a gamble with nature.

Worked examples: Wheat, grown in temperate regions, is the main food grain for much of the world, and it is milled into flour for bread. Rice, grown in flooded fields in Asia, feeds more than half the world's people. Maize, first grown in the Americas, is now grown on every continent and is used for food, animal feed, and fuel. A dairy farm keeps cows for milk, which is turned into cheese, butter, and yoghurt. A vineyard grows grapes for wine, and an orchard grows apples or oranges. Irrigation, bringing water to dry land through canals or pipes, has made farming possible in deserts, as in Egypt along the Nile. The potato, first grown in the Andes, became a staple in Europe in the 1700s, and it fed a growing population, though its failure in Ireland in the 1840s caused a famine that killed about a million people. The date palm, grown in the oases of the Middle East and North Africa, gives fruit, shade, and wood, and it has been cultivated for thousands of years.

Real applications: Agriculture feeds the world: nearly all the food people eat comes from farms. It provides work for about a quarter of the world's people, according to estimates by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations. It supplies raw materials, such as cotton for cloth, timber for building, and plants for medicines. It shapes landscapes, from rice terraces in Asia to wheat fields in North America. It also raises hard questions: how to feed a growing population, how to protect soil and water, and how to farm in a changing climate. The answers to those questions will decide the future of food. Agriculture is also used to restore land: by planting trees and by resting fields, farmers can rebuild soil that has been worn out, a practice called land restoration.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is farming only about food? No. Farms also produce cotton, timber, biofuels, and medicines, and they shape the landscape and the countryside. Can organic farming feed the world? That is debated: organic methods use fewer chemicals and protect the soil, but their yields are often lower, so many experts argue for a mix of methods. Is modern farming bad for the environment? It can be: fertilisers can pollute water, and clearing land can destroy habitats, but modern methods, such as precision farming, aim to use less and to protect more. Will there be enough food in the future? The world already grows enough food in total, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization, but distribution, waste, and poverty mean many people still go hungry, and feeding a larger population in a changing climate is a real challenge. Is farming a science? It is both a science and a craft: it uses biology, chemistry, and engineering, and it also depends on the farmer's experience and judgement. Is farming the oldest job? It is one of the oldest, and it has shaped every civilisation, because a society that cannot feed itself cannot build cities, armies, or schools. Can cities grow their own food? In part: urban gardens, rooftop farms, and vertical farms, in which crops grow indoors under lights, can supply some food, but most food still comes from the countryside.
