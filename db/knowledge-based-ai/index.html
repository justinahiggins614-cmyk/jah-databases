What Knowledge-Based AI Is JAH-KBAI-000001 · simple

Evidence: established The facts are documented in the cited public sources; the demo ledger values are this record's own model figures, labeled as such.

Knowledge-based AI is the branch of artificial intelligence that stores what it knows explicitly and reasons over it. Where a neural network stores knowledge in weights that no one can read, a knowledge-based system stores it as statements a person can inspect: facts, rules, and relations. The field has five parts: representation, how knowledge is written down; reasoning, how new facts are derived from old ones; acquisition, how knowledge gets in; explanation, how the system shows its workings; and maintenance, how the knowledge is kept correct as the world changes. Its classic forms are expert systems, ontologies, and knowledge graphs, and its modern form is often a hybrid: a knowledge graph that grounds a language model, so the model's words can be checked against stored facts.

Knowledge Representation: A History JAH-KBAI-000002 · simple

Knowledge representation began with the question of how a machine could hold what a person knows. In the 1950s the answer was logic: first-order logic as the language of thought. In the 1960s came semantic networks, graphs of concepts and relations, and in the 1970s frames, structures with slots for the parts of a stereotyped situation, and scripts, sequences of expected events. The 1980s brought expert systems, which put rules to work in medicine and industry, and description logics, which traded some of logic's power for answers that could be computed in reasonable time. The 1990s added the semantic web, Tim Berners-Lee's plan to make web data machine-readable, and the 2000s brought it to life with RDF, OWL, and the knowledge graphs of Wikidata and the Google Knowledge Graph.

First-Order Logic and Inference JAH-KBAI-000003 · simple

Evidence: established The facts are documented in the cited public sources; the demo ledger values are this record's own model figures, labeled as such.

First-order logic is the language in which knowledge-based AI first wrote what it knew. Its symbols are constants for things, such as Socrates; predicates for properties and relations, such as Human(x) or Parent(x, y); functions for values, such as father-of(x); variables for any thing; and quantifiers, for-all and there-exists, for saying something about all things or some thing. From 'All humans are mortal' and 'Socrates is human', logic derives 'Socrates is mortal' by inference, and the derivation is a proof: each step follows from the rules, and the conclusion is guaranteed if the premises are true. That guarantee is logic's power and its price: full first-order inference is undecidable in general, which is why practical systems use fragments of logic, such as description logics, that trade power for answers in reasonable time.
