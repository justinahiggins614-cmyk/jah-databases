What Affective Computing Is JAH-AFAI-000001 · simple

Evidence: established The claims in this record are documented in the cited sources; numbers in the ledger are a model, labeled as such.

Affective computing is computing that relates to, arises from, or deliberately influences emotion: systems that read emotional signals, respond to them, and sometimes try to express them. The signals are the face, the voice, the words, and the body: expressions, pitch, word choice, heart rate, and skin conductance. The goal is not to make machines feel, which is unproven, but to make them respond to human feeling with more sense than a machine that ignores it entirely.

History

Rosalind Picard founded the field with her 1997 book Affective Computing, written at the MIT Media Lab. The book argued that emotion is part of intelligence, not separate from it, and that computers would need to handle it. The field grew through the 2000s into face reading, voice analysis, sentiment analysis, and physiological sensing, and it co-founded the company Affectiva in 2009 to put emotion reading into products. The 1997 book is the origin, and the ledger counts the years since.

Emotion Recognition: Signals and Channels JAH-AFAI-000003 · simple

Evidence: established The claims in this record are documented in the cited sources; numbers in the ledger are a model, labeled as such.

Emotion recognition reads feeling from signals, and the signals come through four channels: the face, the voice, the words, and the body. The face carries expressions, coded as action units in the Facial Action Coding System. The voice carries pitch, energy, and tempo. The words carry sentiment and choice. The body carries heart rate, skin conductance, and brain activity. Each channel is noisy on its own, and systems that combine channels do better than systems that trust one.

History

The channels grew up separately: face reading from Ekman's work in the 1960s and 1970s, voice analysis from speech research, sentiment analysis from text mining in the 2000s, and physiological sensing from Picard's wearables in the 1990s. The field learned to combine them in the 2010s, when multimodal datasets let researchers measure how much each channel adds. Skeptical analysis

Forecasts of a steady field are easy to make and easy to be wrong about: a single new method, a hardware shift, or a new law can change the pace. The honest forecast is a range, not a date, and this record keeps its scenarios as scenarios, scored and labeled, not as promises.
