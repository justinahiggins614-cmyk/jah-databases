Photovoltaic Effect Basics JAH-SOST-000001

The photovoltaic effect converts light directly into electricity in a semiconductor junction: photons with enough energy knock electrons loose, and the junction's built-in electric field sweeps the freed charges apart before they recombine, producing a voltage and a current. A solar cell is that junction packaged with contacts, a protective cover, and wiring, and a panel is many cells connected to reach a useful voltage and power.

Cell efficiency depends on the semiconductor's bandgap, which sets which photons can contribute, and on losses from reflection, resistance, and recombination. No single-junction cell converts all sunlight, because photons below the bandgap pass through unused while photons far above it waste their excess as heat. Tandem cells stack junctions of different bandgaps to catch more of the spectrum, at higher manufacturing complexity.

The demo computes panel output power from area, sunlight intensity, and a stated efficiency, printing the result for a teaching example. The efficiency is an assumption for illustration.

Solar Thermal Collection JAH-SOST-000002

Solar thermal collection turns sunlight into heat rather than electricity, using absorbers that capture radiation and transfer it to water, air, or a heat-transfer fluid. Flat-plate collectors serve homes for water heating, while concentrating collectors use mirrors to focus sunlight onto a receiver at high temperature for industrial heat or for driving a turbine. The choice between them is a temperature choice: flat plates suit low temperatures, concentrators suit high ones.

Heat storage pairs naturally with thermal collection, because hot water or molten salt can hold the day's heat into the evening. That storage is simpler and cheaper per unit of energy than electrical storage at large scale, which is why thermal systems remain competitive for heating even where photovoltaics dominate electricity.

Heliostat Field Basics JAH-SOST-000003

A heliostat field surrounds a central tower with mirrors that each track the sun and reflect its light onto a receiver at the tower top. The receiver absorbs the concentrated light as heat, often in molten salt, which can then make steam for a turbine or be stored for later use. The field's advantage is concentration: hundreds of mirrors together deliver far more intense light than any single mirror could.

Each mirror needs two-axis tracking and a control system that knows the sun's position and the mirror's own position precisely, because a mirror pointed even slightly wrong sends its light past the tower. Dust, wind, and shading between mirrors all reduce the field's output, so heliostat plants are sited in clear, sunny, flat terrain and budgeted with those losses in mind.

Space-Based Solar Power Concepts JAH-SOST-000004

Space-based solar power proposes collecting sunlight in orbit, where the sun shines without night, clouds, or atmosphere, and beaming the energy to Earth as microwaves or laser light for conversion to electricity on the ground. The concept's attraction is continuous, high-intensity sunlight; its difficulties are launch cost, the size of the orbital collector and ground receiver, beam safety and regulation, and conversion losses at each step of the chain.

The concept remains at the study and small-demonstration stage: orbital solar panels power satellites routinely, but no system has delivered grid-scale power from orbit to Earth. Studies therefore report the concept with its full chain efficiency, from sunlight in orbit to electricity on the ground, because any single link quoted alone flatters the whole.

Laser and Microwave Beaming JAH-SOST-000005

Power beaming sends energy without wires, usually as a microwave beam or a laser beam, from a transmitter to a receiver that converts it back to electricity. Microwaves spread less over long distances and pass through clouds better, while lasers focus tighter but are blocked by weather and need stricter eye-safety controls. The choice between them is a distance, weather, and safety trade, and every serious proposal states all three.

Beam safety governs the whole design: the beam's intensity at any place a person could stand must stay within exposure limits, which sets the receiver size, the beam's spread, and the automatic shutoff that fires if the beam wanders. A beaming proposal without a safety analysis is incomplete, however efficient its conversion chain claims to be.

The demo computes beam spread from a stated aperture and wavelength, printing the spot size at a stated distance. It is a diffraction illustration with assumed values.

Fuel Cell Basics JAH-SOST-000006

A fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electricity by reacting a fuel, usually hydrogen, with oxygen across an electrolyte, producing electricity, water, and heat without combustion. The voltage of one cell is small, so cells stack in series to reach useful voltages, and the stack's power depends on its area, its operating point, and how well reactants and heat are managed.

Fuel cells suit applications that need quiet, clean operation at the point of use, from backup power to vehicles, but they inherit the fuel's whole supply chain: hydrogen must be produced, stored, and delivered, and each of those steps has its own efficiency and cost. A fuel-cell proposal that quotes only stack efficiency, without the fuel chain, is incomplete.

Grid Interconnection Basics JAH-SOST-000007

Grid interconnection connects generators, storage, and loads into one synchronized alternating-current network, where supply must match demand at every instant and frequency is the shared signal of balance. When demand rises, frequency dips until generators respond; when supply exceeds demand, frequency rises. Operators and automatic controls keep frequency within a narrow band, because equipment across the grid is designed for that band.

Solar and wind complicate the balance because their output varies with weather, which is why grids pair them with storage, flexible generation, demand response, and interconnection to neighboring grids. The engineering question is never whether variable sources can connect, but how much flexibility the rest of the system must provide at each level of penetration.

The demo balances a small grid from stated generation, storage discharge, and demand, printing the surplus or deficit. It is an arithmetic illustration, not a grid simulation.

Energy Storage Options JAH-SOST-000008

Energy storage options divide by timescale: batteries serve hours, pumped water and compressed air serve hours to days where geography allows, thermal storage serves heating loads, and hydrogen serves longer storage at lower round-trip efficiency. No single option wins on every axis, so real systems mix options, matching each storage duration to the gap it must fill.

Round-trip efficiency, lifetime cycles, and cost per unit of stored energy decide the mix, and all three must be quoted together: a cheap store that wastes half its energy and dies young is not cheaper than a dear store that keeps nearly all of it for decades. The comparison table, honestly filled, is the storage decision.

Satellite Power Systems JAH-SOST-000009

Satellite power systems start with solar arrays sized for the mission's orbit: sunlight in space is strong and steady outside eclipse, but arrays degrade under radiation, and eclipses force the satellite onto batteries for part of each orbit. Power budgeting therefore sizes the array for end of life, not beginning of life, and sizes the battery for the longest eclipse plus margin.

Power management on a satellite is a closed loop: the array charges the battery, the battery feeds the bus, and the bus sheds non-essential loads if the budget goes negative. That discipline is why satellites survive years with no servicing, and it is a model for any isolated power system on Earth as well.

The demo budgets a small satellite's power from stated array output, eclipse fraction, and load, printing the margin. The values are assumptions for illustration.

Solar Sail Propulsion JAH-SOST-000010

A solar sail propels a spacecraft with sunlight itself: photons carry momentum, and reflecting them off a large, light sail transfers a small push that, accumulated over months, changes the orbit without any propellant. The push per unit area is tiny, so sails must be enormous and feather-light, and their performance is quoted as acceleration at Earth's distance from the sun, where the sunlight pressure is known.

Sails trade thrust for endurance: they cannot match a rocket's kick, but they never run out of propellant, which makes them attractive for long, slow missions and for station-keeping. Steering comes from tilting the sail, changing the push direction, and the mission design is an exercise in patience and precise navigation.

Thermophotovoltaic Conversion JAH-SOST-000011

Thermophotovoltaic conversion generates electricity from heat by glowing a hot emitter and catching its infrared light with photovoltaic cells tuned to that spectrum. The emitter's temperature sets the spectrum, and the cells' bandgap sets which photons they can use, so the pair must be matched: too cool an emitter and most photons fall below the bandgap, too hot and the system wastes heat managing materials at extreme temperature.

The technology suits waste-heat recovery and storage-coupled generation, where a hot store discharges through the emitter on demand. Its efficiency is bounded by the same spectrum and recombination losses as ordinary photovoltaics, plus the emitter's own losses, so honest reporting quotes the system efficiency from heat in to electricity out.

Perovskite Solar Research JAH-SOST-000012

Perovskite solar cells use a crystal family whose composition can be tuned to absorb different parts of the spectrum, and they can be made by solution processing at low temperature, which promises cheaper manufacturing than silicon's high-temperature furnaces. Laboratory efficiencies have risen rapidly, which is why perovskites draw intense research attention, but the research frontier is stability: heat, moisture, and light degrade many perovskite formulations faster than the decades silicon panels are warranted for.

The honest status of perovskites is therefore research-stage at scale: excellent laboratory cells, promising tandems with silicon, and an open stability question that encapsulation and composition engineering are working to close. A report that quotes only record efficiency, without lifetime, misleads by omission.

Agrivoltaics and Floating Solar JAH-SOST-000013

Agrivoltaics mounts solar panels above crops so the same land produces food and electricity, with the panels shading crops from harsh sun while the crops cool the panels, which improves their efficiency. Floating solar, or floatovoltaics, mounts panels on reservoirs and lakes, where the water cools the panels and the panels reduce evaporation. Both approaches answer the land-use question that ground-mounted solar raises, by sharing land or water rather than claiming it exclusively.

Both need careful design: agrivoltaic panel spacing must leave enough light for the crop, and floating arrays must survive waves, anchoring loads, and maintenance access over water. Neither is a universal answer, but both expand the sites where solar makes sense, which is their real contribution.

Solar Water Splitting JAH-SOST-000014

Solar water splitting uses sunlight to divide water into hydrogen and oxygen, storing solar energy as a chemical fuel that can be kept, moved, and burned or fed to a fuel cell later. Photoelectrochemical cells do it directly at a semiconductor surface, while the indirect route makes electricity first and electrolyzes second. The direct route is elegant and the indirect route is mature, and the comparison turns on efficiency, durability, and cost at scale.

Hydrogen from sunlight is only as clean as its whole chain: the water, the materials, and the manufacturing energy all count, and a system that splits water efficiently but consumes rare materials wastefully may be worse than a less efficient, more durable one. Life-cycle accounting, not single-step efficiency, decides the technology's merit.

District Solar Heating JAH-SOST-000015

District solar heating collects sunlight centrally, stores the heat, often in large water tanks or pit storage, and distributes it through insulated pipes to many buildings. Central collection suits large collector fields and seasonal storage, which individual rooftops cannot match, and the network lets a whole neighborhood share the sun's summer surplus into the heating season.

The engineering challenges are heat loss along pipes, storage size for seasonal shifting, and the control system that balances collector output, storage state, and building demand through the year. The economics turn on the same balance: district systems win where building density is high and heat demand is steady, and lose where buildings are sparse and pipes run long.

Solar Resource Assessment JAH-SOST-000016

Solar resource assessment measures how much sunlight a site actually receives over a year, using ground instruments, satellite data, and models that account for latitude, tilt, orientation, shading, and weather. The assessment's output is a yield estimate with an uncertainty band, and the band matters as much as the central figure, because financing and grid planning depend on the low end as much as the average.

A good assessment states its data sources, its horizon shading survey, and its soiling and snow assumptions, because each can move the yield by several percent. The difference between a careful assessment and an optimistic one is usually not dishonesty but omitted losses, which is why loss checklists are part of the practice.
