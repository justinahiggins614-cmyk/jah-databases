Origins and History Rank 1 · simple · soccer football · beginner

Soccer, known as football outside North America, is the world's most played and watched team sport, with roots in folk ball games played in England for centuries. Its modern form dates to 1863, when the Football Association in England published the first Laws of the Game, separating soccer from rugby and giving clubs a common rulebook. From there it spread through sailors, traders, teachers, and workers to every continent, and the first World Cup in Uruguay in 1930 gave it a global championship. Today soccer is played by children in schoolyards and professionals in stadiums under the same basic laws, and its simplicity, a ball, two goals, and eleven players per side, is a large part of its appeal.

History

Modern soccer was codified in England in 1863 with the Laws of the Game, and the first World Cup was held in Uruguay in 1930.

Rules

The Laws of the Game, maintained by the International Football Association Board, govern soccer worldwide.

Competitions

The World Cup, continental championships, and club competitions now draw the largest audiences in world sport.

Laws of the Game Rank 2 · simple · soccer football · beginner

The Laws of the Game are soccer's seventeen rules, covering the field, the ball, players and equipment, the referee, the duration of play, offside, fouls, and restarts. A match lasts 90 minutes in two halves of 45, with 11 players per side including a goalkeeper, and the team scoring more goals wins. Goals count only when the whole ball crosses the goal line between the posts and under the crossbar. Players may not deliberately handle the ball, except the goalkeeper inside the penalty area, and fouls are punished with free kicks or penalty kicks. Offside prevents attackers from waiting by the opponent's goal, and misconduct is punished with yellow cards for cautions and red cards for sending-offs.

History

The Laws are maintained by the International Football Association Board, with FIFA publishing them for worldwide use.

Rules

A match lasts 90 minutes in two 45-minute halves, with 11 players per side, one goalkeeper, and goals scored when the whole ball crosses the goal line.

Techniques

Referees enforce fouls, offside, handball, and misconduct, using yellow and red cards and, in some competitions, video review.

Positions and Formations Rank 3 · simple · soccer football · beginner

Soccer positions divide the team into a goalkeeper, defenders, midfielders, and forwards, and formations describe how they are arranged. A goalkeeper guards the goal and organises the defence; defenders stop attacks and start play from the back; midfielders link defence and attack, winning the ball and passing it forward; forwards create and finish scoring chances. Common formations include 4-4-2, with four defenders, four midfielders, and two forwards, and 4-3-3, with three forwards. Formations are not fixed: a team may defend in one shape and attack in another, and coaches change shape during a match to chase a goal or protect a lead.

Rules

Teams may use substitutes within competition limits, and formations may change during a match as coaches adjust tactics.

Techniques

Goalkeeper, defenders, midfielders, and forwards each have distinct duties, and formations such as 4-4-2 and 4-3-3 describe their arrangement.

Training

Players train for their position's demands: defenders for tackling and heading, midfielders for passing and stamina, forwards for finishing.

Passing and Possession Rank 4 · simple · soccer football · beginner

Passing is soccer's basic skill and possession is its strategy. A team keeps the ball by passing accurately, moving into space to receive, and choosing the right weight and direction for each pass. Short passes build control and draw defenders out of position; long passes switch play or release a runner behind the defence; through balls split a defensive line for a teammate running onto them. Possession matters because a team with the ball cannot concede, and patient possession tires opponents and creates openings, but possession alone does not win matches: a team must turn control into chances. The best passing teams combine accuracy with movement, so the ball moves faster than any defender can run.

Techniques

Short passes, long passes, one-touch passing, and through balls move the ball and the defence, with first touch and weight of pass as core skills.

Training

Passing drills build accuracy, timing, and movement off the ball, since a pass is only as good as the run that receives it.

Statistics

Possession and pass completion are tracked as team statistics, though they measure control, not necessarily goals.

Shooting and Finishing Rank 5 · simple · soccer football · beginner

Shooting is how possession becomes goals, and finishing is the skill of choosing the right shot at the right moment. A striker may drive the ball with the instep for power, place it with the inside of the foot for accuracy, volley a ball out of the air, head a cross, or chip a goalkeeper who has come off the line. Good finishers stay calm, pick a corner, and shoot early when a chance is clear, rather than taking an extra touch that lets a defender recover. Shooting from distance is spectacular but less reliable than a close-range finish, and coaches teach players to value a high-percentage chance over a highlight. The difference between a good team and a great one is often finishing: creating chances is common, converting them is rare.

Rules

A goal counts when the whole ball crosses the goal line, and shots from inside the penalty area are more likely to score than long-range efforts.

Techniques

Shooting uses the instep for power, the inside of the foot for placement, volleys, headers, and chips, with composure deciding many chances.

Training

Finishing drills practise shooting from different angles, distances, and speeds, under pressure from defenders and the goalkeeper.

Defending and Tackling Rank 6 · compounded · soccer football · intermediate

Defending in soccer is a team skill built on positioning, tackling, and communication. A defender stays goal-side of the attacker, jockeys rather than diving in, and tackles only when the ball can be won cleanly, because a missed tackle leaves the defence exposed. Teams defend in units: a back line moves together to play offside, midfielders press the ball carrier, and forwards press from the front to force mistakes. Tackling must be fair: a tackle that endangers an opponent, uses excessive force, or comes from behind recklessly is a foul and may bring a yellow or red card. Good defending is often unnoticed, because its success is a chance that never happens.

Rules

Tackles must win the ball without endangering opponents; reckless or excessive force is a foul and may bring a card.

Techniques

Defenders jockey attackers, stay goal-side, tackle cleanly, block shots, and head clearances, with teamwork in pressing and covering.

Training

Defensive drills practise one-on-one defending, tracking runners, and organising a back line that moves as a unit.

Goalkeeping Rank 7 · compounded · soccer football · intermediate

Goalkeeping is soccer's most specialised position. The goalkeeper is the only player who may handle the ball, and only inside the penalty area, and must combine shot-stopping with organising the defence, claiming crosses, and starting attacks. A goalkeeper positions by angles, narrowing the shooter's target, dives to reach shots in the corners, and chooses between catching, parrying, and punching according to the ball's speed and spin. Distribution matters too: a quick throw or kick can launch a counterattack, while a calm pass to a defender can settle a team under pressure. Goalkeepers train separately for much of the week, because their skills, handling, diving, and decision-making in one-on-ones, differ from every other position.

Rules

Only the goalkeeper may handle the ball, and only inside the penalty area; outside it, the goalkeeper is an outfield player.

Techniques

Goalkeepers dive, catch, parry, and punch, position themselves by angles, and distribute with throws, kicks, and passes.

Training

Goalkeeper training covers footwork, handling, diving technique, crosses, one-on-ones, and communication with defenders.

Set Pieces Rank 8 · compounded · soccer football · intermediate

Set pieces are soccer's rehearsed moments: free kicks, corners, penalty kicks, and throw-ins, where play restarts from a dead ball and teams can plan. A penalty kick is taken from 12 yards with only the goalkeeper to beat, and it is the most reliable scoring chance in the game. Corners are delivered into the penalty area for headers and volleys, with attackers making planned runs and defenders marking zones or men. Free kicks near goal may be shot directly or crossed, and throw-ins, though humble, can start dangerous attacks when taken quickly. Because set pieces are planned in training, they decide many tight matches, and coaches spend a large share of practice time on them.

Rules

A penalty kick is taken from 12 yards with only the goalkeeper to beat; corners are taken from the corner arc; free kicks may be direct or indirect.

Techniques

Free kicks, corners, penalties, and throw-ins are rehearsed plays, with deliveries, runs, blocks, and shots planned in training.

Training

Teams practise set-piece routines for attacking and defending, since a large share of goals in tight matches come from them.

Tactics and Systems Rank 9 · compounded · soccer football · intermediate

Tactics in soccer describe how a team organises itself to attack, defend, and transition between the two. A pressing team hunts the ball high up the field, trying to win it near the opponent's goal; a counterattacking team defends deep and breaks quickly when it wins possession; a possession team keeps the ball and probes for openings; a low-block team defends compactly near its own goal and frustrates opponents. Coaches choose a system to suit their players and to exploit an opponent's weaknesses, and they adjust it during matches with substitutions and formation changes. Tactics work only when players understand their roles and move together, because a system is a shared plan, not a set of individual instructions.

History

Tactical ideas have evolved from early attacking formations to modern pressing and positional play, shaped by influential coaches and teams.

Techniques

Pressing, counterattacking, possession play, and low blocks are tactical systems, chosen to suit a team's players and opponents.

Training

Tactical training uses small-sided games, video analysis, and match scenarios to teach players when to press, drop, or switch play.

Training and Fitness Rank 10 · compounded · soccer football · intermediate

Soccer training prepares players for a game of repeated sprints, changes of direction, and technical skill under fatigue. A typical week mixes technical drills for passing and shooting, small-sided games that practise tactics at high intensity, strength and conditioning for power and injury prevention, and recovery sessions after matches. Coaches plan load across the season, increasing intensity gradually and easing before important matches, because overtraining causes injuries and under-training leaves players unfit. Warm-ups, cool-downs, and prevention exercises for hamstrings, groins, and ankles are standard, since muscle injuries are the most common in soccer. Fitness is position-specific: fullbacks and midfielders cover the most ground, while forwards need explosive sprinting and goalkeepers need diving power.

Techniques

Fitness demands include repeated sprints, endurance, agility, and jumping, with position-specific demands for each role.

Training

Soccer training mixes technical drills, small-sided games, strength and conditioning, and recovery, planned across a season.

Safety

Warm-ups, load management, and injury prevention exercises reduce the risk of muscle injuries, the most common soccer injuries.

Youth Development Rank 11 · compounded · soccer football · intermediate

Youth development in soccer is how children learn the game and, for a few, reach professional level. Good youth coaching uses small-sided games on smaller pitches with smaller goals, so children touch the ball often, make decisions constantly, and enjoy playing rather than standing in lines. Results and league tables are de-emphasised at younger ages, with improvement and enjoyment valued more, and players are encouraged to play several positions before specialising. Professional club academies scout talented children and provide coaching, education, and welfare support, but only a small share of academy players become professionals, so education matters as much as football. The wider goal is participation: children who enjoy soccer young are more likely to stay active and to play, coach, or support the game for life.

History

Academies run by professional clubs scout and develop young talent, with education and welfare as formal parts of academy life.

Training

Youth soccer emphasises small-sided games, many touches, and fun, with formal league tables and results de-emphasised at younger ages.

Safety

Age-group rules, qualified coaches, safeguarding, and appropriate pitch and goal sizes protect young players.

Refereeing Rank 12 · complex · soccer football · advanced

Refereeing in soccer is the work of applying the Laws fairly while a match moves at full speed. The referee controls play, awards free kicks and penalties, cautions and sends off players, keeps time, and records the score, assisted by two assistant referees who judge offside and out-of-play incidents, a fourth official who manages substitutions and technical areas, and, in some competitions, video assistant referees who review clear errors in goals, penalties, red cards, and mistaken identity. Referees train for fitness, because they cover many kilometres in a match, and for positioning, communication, and calm decision-making under pressure from players and crowds. Their decisions are final on the field, and respect for officials is a stated part of soccer's culture.

History

Refereeing developed with codified soccer, and video assistant refereeing was introduced in major competitions in the late 2010s.

Rules

The referee controls the match, applies the Laws, keeps time, and is assisted by assistant referees, a fourth official, and video review in some competitions.

Techniques

Referees train in positioning, fitness, communication, and decision-making, and are assessed after matches.

Major Competitions Rank 13 · complex · soccer football · advanced

Soccer's major competitions give the sport its calendar and its legends. The World Cup, held every four years, is the largest single-sport event in the world, with national teams qualifying through continental competitions. Continental championships, such as the European Championship, Copa America, and Africa Cup of Nations, crown regional champions, while club competitions, led by the UEFA Champions League, pit the best clubs against each other across a season. Domestic leagues and cups run alongside them, with promotion and relegation linking divisions. Tournaments typically combine a group stage, where teams play each other once or twice, with knockout rounds, where a single loss ends a campaign and tied matches go to extra time and then penalties.

History

The World Cup began in 1930, the European Championship in 1960, and continental club competitions grew through the twentieth century.

Rules

Tournaments use group stages followed by knockout rounds, with extra time and penalty shootouts deciding tied knockout matches.

Competitions

The World Cup, continental championships, and club competitions such as the UEFA Champions League are soccer's premier tournaments.

Women's Football Rank 14 · complex · soccer football · advanced

Women's football has grown from restriction to a major part of world soccer. Early women's teams drew large crowds, but England's Football Association banned women's soccer from its grounds in 1921, a ban that lasted until 1971, and similar restrictions existed elsewhere. Despite this, women kept playing, and the Women's World Cup began in 1991, followed by professional leagues in England, the United States, and elsewhere, with crowds and broadcasts growing rapidly. Women's soccer follows the same Laws as the men's game, with the same positions, tactics, and competitions structure, and its players are now full-time professionals in the leading leagues. Gaps in pay, facilities, and coverage remain in many countries, and closing them is a central task for the sport.

History

Women's soccer was long restricted, including a ban by England's Football Association from 1921 to 1971, before growing into professional leagues and major tournaments.

Competitions

The Women's World Cup, begun in 1991, and professional women's leagues now draw large crowds and broadcasts.

Statistics

Participation and audiences for women's soccer have grown sharply, though pay and coverage gaps remain in many countries.

Futsal Rank 15 · complex · soccer football · advanced

Futsal is soccer's indoor small-sided form, played with two teams of five on a hard court about the size of a basketball court, with a smaller ball that bounces less. It was developed in Uruguay in the 1930s for play in small spaces, and it is now governed by FIFA, which holds a Futsal World Cup. Matches last two 20-minute halves of stopped-clock time, substitutions roll freely, and the goalkeeper often joins the attack as an extra outfield player. Because the court is small and the ball stays on the ground, futsal rewards close control, quick passing, and rapid transitions, and many great soccer players credit futsal for their touch and decision-making as children.

History

Futsal was developed in Uruguay in the 1930s as indoor soccer for small spaces and is now governed by FIFA with a World Cup.

Rules

Two teams of five play on a hard indoor court with a smaller, lower-bounce ball, in two 20-minute halves of stopped-clock time.

Techniques

Close control, quick passing, and rapid transitions dominate, with rolling substitutions and a goalkeeper who often joins attacks.

Beach Soccer Rank 16 · complex · soccer football · advanced

Beach soccer is soccer played on sand, with two teams of five contesting three 12-minute periods on a smaller pitch with goals at each end. It grew from informal beach games, notably in Brazil, into an organised sport governed by FIFA, with a Beach Soccer World Cup. The sand makes running hard and the ball bounce unpredictably, so players use acrobatic bicycle kicks, powerful volleys, and clever free kicks, and goals are frequent, with matches often producing ten or more goals in total. Substitutions roll freely, and the game is played barefoot. Beach soccer is fast, spectacular, and accessible, needing only sand, goals, and a ball.

History

Beach soccer grew from informal beach games, notably in Brazil, into an organised sport with a FIFA Beach Soccer World Cup.

Rules

Two teams of five play on sand in three 12-minute periods, with free kicks, bicycle kicks, and a fast, high-scoring game.

Techniques

Playing on sand demands balance, acrobatic shooting, and powerful volleys, with substitutions rolling freely.
