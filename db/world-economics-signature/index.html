What Economics Is

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a choice ladder from household to government, a worked opportunity-cost table, and a design reading that treats every economic decision as a trade-off with a price.

Definition: Economics is the study of how people, businesses, and governments make choices when resources are limited. It asks how goods and services are produced, how they are shared out, and what happens when prices, wages, and interest rates change. Economics studies both small decisions, such as what a household buys, and large ones, such as how a whole country grows or why unemployment rises. It uses data, models, and history to explain these choices, and it is a social science, because it studies people, not only numbers. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Economics as a subject began with the study of trade and wealth. Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, argued that markets, guided by competition, could organise production without central direction, an idea he described as an invisible hand. In the 19th century, thinkers such as David Ricardo and John Stuart Mill developed theories of trade and value, and Alfred Marshall's Principles of Economics, published in 1890, brought supply and demand together as the core of the subject. The Great Depression of the 1930s led John Maynard Keynes to argue, in his General Theory of 1936, that governments could stabilise the economy by spending in downturns. After the Second World War, economics became more mathematical and more statistical, and in recent decades it has added psychology, experiments, and the study of data from real life. In the 20th century economics split into new fields: econometrics, which uses statistics to test theories; development economics, which studies poor countries; and behavioural economics, which studies how people actually decide. The Nobel Prize in Economics, first awarded in 1969, has recognised work in all of these fields, and the subject now uses experiments, large datasets, and computer models. Economics has also become more international: the data are global, the journals are global, and the problems, from climate change to financial crises, are global too.

Key concepts: Scarcity is the central idea: wants are unlimited, but resources are limited, so choices must be made. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up when a choice is made. A model is a simplified description of an economy, used to make predictions. Microeconomics studies individual people, firms, and markets, while macroeconomics studies the whole economy, including growth, inflation, and unemployment. An incentive is a reward or penalty that influences behaviour, and much of economics is the study of how incentives shape choices. A positive statement is a statement about what is, which can be tested, such as unemployment is 5 percent, while a normative statement is a statement about what should be, such as unemployment should be lower, which involves values as well as facts. A ceteris paribus assumption means holding other things equal, so that the effect of one change can be studied. A trade-off is a situation where gaining one thing means giving up another. Rationality, in economics, means choosing the best option given the information available, though behavioural economics shows that people often fall short of this.

How it works: Economists start by defining a question, such as why house prices rise, and then they build a model of the people and markets involved, with assumptions about how they behave. They use the model to make a prediction, for example that a tax will reduce sales, and then they test the prediction against data from the real world. If the data do not match, the model is revised. This is the same method as other sciences, except that economists usually cannot run controlled experiments on whole economies, so they use natural experiments, such as a policy change in one region but not another, and statistical methods to separate cause from coincidence. The result is not a law of nature but a tested explanation that works in some conditions and not in others. Economists also use the idea of the margin: decisions are usually about a little more or a little less, not about all or nothing. A firm decides whether to produce one more unit, a household decides whether to work one more hour, and a government decides whether to spend one more pound. The marginal cost is the cost of one more unit, and the marginal benefit is the benefit of one more unit, and the best choice is where the two are equal. This marginal thinking is one of the most useful tools in economics, because most real decisions are marginal ones.

Worked examples: Take a simple choice. A student has 20 pounds and can buy a book for 12 pounds or a meal for 8 pounds, but not both and a cinema ticket for 10 pounds as well. If she buys the book and the meal, she spends 20 pounds and gives up the cinema ticket, so the opportunity cost of the book and meal is the ticket. A second example is a firm: a bakery can use its oven to bake bread or cakes, and if it bakes 100 loaves it gives up 50 cakes, so the opportunity cost of a loaf is half a cake. A third example is a government: spending 1 billion on a road means 1 billion not spent on schools, and the opportunity cost is the schools that are not built. In each case the cost is what is given up, not only what is paid. A second worked example uses time. A person has 16 waking hours a day. If they work 8 hours, sleep 8, and have 8 hours left for other things, the opportunity cost of an extra hour of work is an hour of leisure, and the opportunity cost of an hour of television is an hour that could have been spent studying. Time, like money, is scarce, and the same logic applies. A student who spends an hour on social media gives up an hour of study, and the cost is the learning that does not happen, which is why economists say that time is the ultimate scarce resource.

Real applications: Economics is used everywhere decisions are made. Governments use it to set taxes, to design benefits, and to judge whether a new road or railway is worth building. Central banks use it to set interest rates and to control inflation. Businesses use it to set prices, to decide how much to produce, and to plan for the future. Households use it, often without realising it, when they compare prices, save for retirement, or decide whether to borrow. And the public debate about jobs, prices, and growth is conducted in the language of economics, so understanding it is part of being an informed citizen. Economics also studies the environment, health, and education, because all of them involve scarcity and choice. The economics of climate change asks how to cut emissions at the lowest cost, the economics of health asks how to allocate scarce medical resources, and the economics of education asks how to invest in skills. The subject's scope is therefore very wide, and its methods, models, data, and testing, are applied to almost every area of human life.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is economics only about money? No, it is about choice under scarcity, and it applies to time, to health, to the environment, and to anything limited. Is economics a science? It is a social science: it uses data and testing, but people are harder to predict than atoms, so its results are less certain than those of physics. Do economists agree with each other? Often not, and disagreement is normal, because economies are complex and the evidence is rarely conclusive. Is economics the same as business? No, business studies how to run a firm, while economics studies how markets and economies work, including the effects on people who are not in business at all. Is economics a hard subject? It uses mathematics and statistics, but its core ideas, scarcity, choice, and incentives, are simple, and they can be understood without advanced mathematics. Do I need to study economics to understand the news? The news is full of economic language, from inflation to interest rates, and a basic understanding of economics makes the news far easier to follow. Is economics political? Economics studies policies, and policies involve values, so economics is often political, but the analysis itself aims to be evidence-based, separating what is true from what is wished for.

Supply and Demand

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a market-clearing ladder, a worked surplus-and-shortage table, and a design reading that treats the equilibrium as a balance that markets keep returning to.

Definition: Supply and demand is the model that explains how prices are set in a market. Demand is how much of a good people want to buy at each price, and supply is how much producers want to sell at each price. When the price is high, demand is usually low and supply is high; when the price is low, demand is high and supply is low. The price where the amount demanded equals the amount supplied is called the equilibrium price, and at that price the market clears, with no shortage and no surplus. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: The idea of supply and demand was developed over centuries, but it was Alfred Marshall who brought it together in his Principles of Economics, published in 1890, with the famous supply and demand diagram, which he compared to the two blades of a pair of scissors, both needed to cut. Earlier thinkers, including Adam Smith in 1776, had described how prices respond to scarcity, and the French economist Antoine Augustin Cournot had used mathematics to describe demand in 1838. In the 20th century, the model was tested against real markets, from agriculture to housing, and it was extended to labour markets, where the wage is the price of work, and to financial markets, where the interest rate is the price of borrowing. The model remains the first tool economists reach for, even though real markets are more complicated than the diagram. The model has been extended in many directions. In the 20th century, economists studied markets where information is unequal, for example where a seller knows more about a used car than the buyer, a problem called adverse selection, which can cause the market to fail. They studied markets with externalities, such as pollution, where the price does not reflect the full cost. And they studied markets that do not clear, for example labour markets where wages are sticky, so unemployment persists. These extensions do not replace supply and demand, but they show where it needs to be supplemented, and they are the reason the model is taught alongside its limits.

Key concepts: Demand is the quantity buyers want at each price, and it usually falls as the price rises, which is called the law of demand. Supply is the quantity sellers want to offer at each price, and it usually rises as the price rises, which is called the law of supply. Equilibrium is the price and quantity where demand equals supply. A shortage happens when the price is below equilibrium and demand exceeds supply, while a surplus happens when the price is above equilibrium and supply exceeds demand. Elasticity measures how responsive demand or supply is to a change in price. A substitute is a good that can replace another, such as butter for margarine, so a rise in the price of one raises the demand for the other. A complement is a good that is used with another, such as cars and petrol, so a rise in the price of one lowers the demand for the other. A normal good is a good whose demand rises with income, while an inferior good is one whose demand falls as income rises, such as instant noodles. A shift in demand is a change in the whole demand curve, caused by income, tastes, or the price of related goods, while a movement along the curve is caused by a change in the price itself.

How it works: The model works through the responses of buyers and sellers to price. If the price of a good rises, some buyers buy less or switch to a substitute, so demand falls, while producers find it more profitable to supply more, so supply rises. If the price falls, the opposite happens. When a market is out of equilibrium, the price moves: a shortage pushes the price up, because buyers compete for the scarce good, and a surplus pushes the price down, because sellers cut prices to clear their stock. A change in something other than price, such as income or the cost of raw materials, shifts the whole curve: higher income shifts demand for most goods to the right, and higher costs shift supply to the left, and the new equilibrium is found where the shifted curves cross. The model also explains why prices differ between places and times. The price of strawberries is high in winter, when supply is low, and low in summer, when supply is high. The price of a hotel room is high at the weekend, when demand is high, and low on a weekday. The price of oil is high when demand is strong and supply is disrupted, and low when demand is weak. In each case the price is the signal that balances the two sides, and it changes as supply or demand changes, which is why prices are not fixed but move constantly.

Worked examples: Take the market for apples. Suppose at 1 pound per kilogram, buyers want 100 kilograms a day and sellers offer 100 kilograms, so the market is in equilibrium. If the price rises to 1.50 pounds, buyers want only 70 kilograms but sellers offer 130 kilograms, so there is a surplus of 60 kilograms, and the price will fall. If the price falls to 0.50 pounds, buyers want 140 kilograms but sellers offer only 60, so there is a shortage of 80 kilograms, and the price will rise. A second example is petrol: when the price of oil rises, the supply of petrol shifts left, the price rises, and the quantity sold falls. A third example is a good harvest: the supply of wheat shifts right, the price falls, and the quantity sold rises. A second worked example uses elasticity. If the price of coffee rises by 10 percent and demand falls by 5 percent, the price elasticity of demand is 0.5, which means demand is inelastic, because it changes less than the price. If the price of a luxury holiday rises by 10 percent and demand falls by 20 percent, the elasticity is 2, which means demand is elastic. Firms use elasticity to set prices: if demand is inelastic, a price rise increases revenue, because the quantity falls by less than the price rises, while if demand is elastic, a price rise reduces revenue, because the quantity falls by more.

Real applications: Supply and demand is used to understand almost every market. Governments use it to predict the effect of a tax: a tax on cigarettes raises the price, reduces the quantity sold, and raises revenue, and the model predicts how much of each. It is used to understand minimum wages, rent controls, and price caps, all of which set prices away from equilibrium and create shortages or surpluses. Businesses use it to set prices and to forecast sales, and it is the starting point for understanding international trade, where the world price is set by world supply and demand. The model is simple, but it explains a great deal. Supply and demand is also used to understand the housing market. When demand for houses rises, because incomes rise or because more people want to live in a city, and supply cannot rise quickly, because building takes time and land is limited, prices rise sharply. When supply rises, because new houses are built, prices fall. The model explains why house prices are high in cities with strong demand and limited land, and why they are lower where land is plentiful and demand is weak. It also explains why rent controls, which cap prices below equilibrium, create shortages, because demand exceeds supply at the capped price.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does supply and demand explain every price? No, some prices are set by regulation, by habit, or by market power, and the model is a starting point, not the whole story. Is the equilibrium price fair? The model does not say: equilibrium is a description of where supply meets demand, not a judgement about whether the result is just. What happens if demand rises? The demand curve shifts right, the equilibrium price rises, and the equilibrium quantity rises too, which is why a popular product becomes more expensive. Can supply and demand be used for labour? Yes, the wage is the price of labour, and the same logic applies, though labour markets have features, such as contracts and unions, that goods markets do not. Does supply and demand work for housing? Yes, but housing supply is slow to respond, because building takes years, so prices can stay high for a long time. Does it work for oil? Yes, the world price of oil is set by world supply and demand, and it moves sharply when supply is disrupted or demand changes. Is the model too simple? It is a simplification, and real markets have features it omits, such as market power, information problems, and externalities, but it is a useful starting point, and economists build on it rather than replacing it.

Markets

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a market-types ladder from a village stall to a global exchange, a price-discovery explainer, and a design reading that treats a market as a matching system with rules.

Definition: A market is a place, a system, or an arrangement where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods and services for money or for other goods. Markets can be physical, such as a farmers' market, or virtual, such as an online marketplace, and they can be local or global. A market works by letting prices carry information: a high price tells producers to supply more and buyers to buy less, and a low price tells the opposite. Markets are the main way modern economies organise production and distribution, and they range from the market for fresh bread to the market for foreign currency. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Markets are as old as trade itself: the first markets were places where farmers and craftsmen exchanged goods, and the first coins, which made markets easier, appeared in Lydia in the 7th century BCE. Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, gave the first systematic account of how markets work, arguing that competition and self-interest could produce an orderly result. In the 19th century, markets spread with railways, telegraphs, and steamships, and the first stock exchanges, such as London's, founded in the 18th century, let people trade shares in companies. In the 20th century, governments regulated markets to prevent fraud and monopoly, and in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, the internet created global online markets, from auction sites to app stores, where buyers and sellers meet without ever being in the same place. Markets have also been studied as institutions, with rules that can be designed well or badly. The economist Alvin Roth, who won the Nobel Prize in 2012, designed markets that match medical graduates to hospitals, students to schools, and kidney donors to patients, using algorithms that produce stable matches, where no one can do better by breaking the match. His work shows that markets are not only found but also made, and that a well-designed market can solve problems that a poorly designed one cannot. Market design is now a major field, applied to auctions, to school admissions, and to the allocation of scarce medical resources.

Key concepts: A market economy is an economy in which most decisions are made by buyers and sellers in markets, rather than by a central plan. Competition is the rivalry between sellers, which pushes prices down and quality up. A market failure is a situation where a market does not produce an efficient result, for example when pollution is not priced. A free market is a market with little government intervention, while a regulated market is one where the government sets rules. Market power is the ability of a firm to set prices above the competitive level, which is the concern of competition policy. A thin market is a market with few buyers and sellers, where it is hard to find a trading partner, such as the market for a rare painting. A thick market is a market with many buyers and sellers, where trading is easy, such as the market for shares in a large company. Liquidity is the ease with which an asset can be bought or sold without changing its price, and it is higher in thick markets. A two-sided market is a market that serves two groups, such as a platform that serves buyers and sellers, or readers and advertisers, and it must attract both sides to work. Network effects are the benefits of a larger network, so a platform becomes more valuable as more people use it.

How it works: A market works by matching buyers and sellers through price. A seller who charges too much loses customers to rivals, and a buyer who offers too little finds no seller willing to trade, so prices move toward the level where the two sides agree. This process, called price discovery, happens continuously in markets with many buyers and sellers, and it is faster and more accurate the more information is available. Markets also work by giving incentives: a firm that finds a cheaper way to produce can undercut its rivals and earn more, and a firm that ignores its customers loses them. The result is that resources flow toward the uses people value most, as measured by what they are willing to pay, though that is not the same as what is most valuable in every sense. Markets also need trust. A buyer must trust that the seller will deliver, and a seller must trust that the buyer will pay. Trust is built by reputation, by repeat business, by guarantees, and by law, which lets a buyer sue a seller who does not deliver. Online markets have built trust through ratings and reviews, through escrow systems that hold payment until delivery, and through guarantees that refund the buyer if the goods do not arrive. Without trust, markets break down, which is why the institutions that support trust, from courts to consumer protection, are essential to a market economy.

Worked examples: Take a farmers' market. Ten stalls sell tomatoes, and the price settles at 2 pounds per kilogram, where the total amount buyers want equals the total amount sellers offer. If one stall charges 3 pounds, buyers go elsewhere, and the stall must lower its price or go unsold. A second example is the foreign exchange market, where currencies are traded: if many people want to buy pounds, the price of the pound rises against other currencies. A third example is the labour market: if there is a shortage of nurses, hospitals raise wages to attract them, and more people train as nurses. In each case the price is the signal that balances the two sides. A second worked example uses the stock market. A share in a company is traded many times a day, and its price moves with news about the company and the economy. If a company announces higher profits, demand for its shares rises and the price rises; if it announces a loss, the price falls. The stock market is a thick market, with many buyers and sellers, so shares can be bought and sold quickly, and the price reflects the collective judgement of the market about the company's future. The market is not always right, as bubbles and crashes show, but it is a powerful mechanism for valuing companies and for allocating capital.

Real applications: Markets are used to organise almost everything that can be bought and sold, from food to housing to shares in companies. They are also used to solve problems that are not obviously about buying and selling: carbon markets let firms trade permits to emit, so the reduction in emissions happens where it is cheapest, and auctions are used to sell radio spectrum, government bonds, and even places at universities. The study of markets, called market design, has won Nobel Prizes for economists who designed better auctions and matching systems, such as the system that matches medical graduates to hospitals. Markets are tools, and like all tools they can be designed well or badly. Markets are also used to allocate scarce resources that cannot be bought and sold in the usual way. The market for radio spectrum, used by mobile phones and broadcasters, is allocated by auction, because the spectrum is limited and the auction gives it to the firm that values it most. The market for carbon permits, used to limit emissions, is allocated by trading, because trading lets the reduction happen where it is cheapest. These are designed markets, and they show that the idea of a market can be applied far beyond the buying and selling of goods.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are markets always efficient? No, markets can fail, for example when they produce pollution, when information is unequal, or when a monopoly controls the price, and governments intervene in those cases. Do markets need government? Yes, markets need rules: property rights, contracts that can be enforced, and protection against fraud, and those rules are provided by governments and by law. Is everything better left to markets? That is a matter of judgement: some things, such as health care and education, are provided partly by markets and partly by governments in most countries, and the balance is a political choice. Can a market exist without money? Yes, barter markets exist, but money makes markets far more efficient, because it avoids the need to find someone who wants exactly what you have. Are online markets the same as physical markets? They follow the same logic, but they are larger, faster, and more transparent, because buyers can compare prices instantly, which increases competition and lowers prices. Do markets work for everything? No, some things, such as clean air and national defence, cannot be sold in markets, because they are public goods, and they are provided by governments. Is a market the same as a shop? A shop is one kind of market, but a market is any arrangement where buyers and sellers meet, including an exchange, an auction, and a website.

Prices

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a price-setting ladder from a competitive market to a monopoly, a worked price-change table, and a design reading that treats price as a signal, not just a number.

Definition: A price is the amount of money asked or paid for a good or service, and it is one of the most important pieces of information in an economy. Prices tell buyers what something costs, tell sellers what they can earn, and tell producers what to make. Prices rise and fall with supply and demand, with costs, and with competition, and they are the signals that coordinate the decisions of millions of people who never meet. Understanding prices means understanding how an economy communicates with itself. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: The study of prices goes back to the earliest markets, where prices were set by haggling, and to the first coins, which standardised them. Adam Smith in 1776 distinguished between the price of a good and its value, noting the paradox that water, which is essential, is cheap, while diamonds, which are not, are expensive, a puzzle later solved by marginal utility, the idea that price depends on the value of the last unit, not the total value. In the 19th century, economists developed the theory of price under competition and under monopoly, and in the 20th century they studied how prices are set in practice, by firms with costs, with rivals, and with customers who do not always behave as the simple theory predicts. Today, prices change constantly in many markets, and online retailers can change prices many times a day. Prices are also studied in the context of inflation. The price level is the average of all prices, and its change is inflation. If the price level rises by 3 percent in a year, the average price is 3 percent higher, though individual prices may rise by more or fall. The study of prices therefore leads to the study of inflation, which is the subject of a later record, and to the study of relative prices, which guide the allocation of resources. A rise in the relative price of a good tells producers to make more of it and consumers to buy less, which is how the economy adapts to change.

Key concepts: The price level is the average level of prices in an economy, and its change over time is inflation. A relative price is the price of one good compared with another, and it is relative prices that guide choices: if the price of beef rises relative to chicken, people buy more chicken. Price elasticity of demand measures how much demand changes when the price changes. A price ceiling is a maximum price set by law, and a price floor is a minimum price, such as a minimum wage. Sticky prices are prices that change slowly, even when conditions change, because of contracts, menu costs, or habit. A menu cost is the cost of changing a price, such as reprinting a menu or updating a website, and it is one reason prices are sticky. A psychological price is a price set to influence perception, such as 9.99 pounds instead of 10 pounds, which looks cheaper even though the difference is one penny. Price discrimination is the practice of charging different prices to different customers for the same good, such as a student discount or an airline charging more for a ticket bought at the last minute. A loss leader is a product sold at a loss to attract customers, who then buy other things at a profit.

How it works: Prices are set in different ways in different markets. In a competitive market, the price is set by supply and demand, and no single buyer or seller can change it. In a market with few sellers, firms set prices by considering their costs and their rivals' prices, and they may use strategies such as discounts, sales, and loyalty schemes. In a regulated market, the government may set the price, as it does for some utilities. In all cases, the price must cover the cost of production in the long run, or the seller will stop supplying. Prices also carry information about quality: a higher price can signal higher quality, though it does not always do so, and a price that is too low can make buyers suspicious. Prices also reflect the costs of production, which include labour, materials, rent, and capital. A firm that faces higher costs must either raise its price or accept a lower profit, and if its rivals do not face the same costs, it may lose customers. The cost of production is therefore a floor for the price in the long run, though in the short run a firm may sell below cost to clear stock or to win market share. The study of costs, called cost theory, is a central part of the study of prices, and it explains why prices differ between firms and between countries.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A shop sells a coffee for 3 pounds. If the cost of beans rises, the shop's cost per cup rises from 1 pound to 1.50 pounds, and the shop may raise the price to 3.50 pounds to protect its margin. If a rival opens next door selling coffee for 2.50 pounds, the shop may have to lower its price or lose customers. A second example is a sale: a coat priced at 100 pounds is reduced to 70 pounds, a fall of 30 percent, and the shop sells twice as many, so its revenue rises from 100 pounds per coat sold at the old rate to 140 pounds for two coats. A third example is the price of oil: when the world price rises, the price of petrol rises, and the price of everything transported by road rises with it. A second worked example uses price discrimination. An airline sells a seat for 100 pounds to a passenger who books early and for 300 pounds to a passenger who books at the last minute, because the last-minute passenger is likely to be a business traveller who values the flight more and has fewer alternatives. The airline earns more by charging different prices than by charging a single price, and the passengers who book early benefit from the lower price. Price discrimination is common in transport, in hotels, and in software, where students and businesses pay different prices for the same product.

Real applications: Prices are used to make decisions at every level. Households compare prices to decide what to buy, and they use price changes as a signal to switch to cheaper alternatives. Firms use prices to decide what to produce, how much to produce, and where to sell. Governments use prices, especially the price level, to judge inflation and to set policy, and they use taxes to change relative prices, for example by taxing tobacco to raise its price and reduce smoking. The study of prices is therefore the study of how an economy talks to itself, and it is the foundation of the study of markets. Prices are also used by governments to change behaviour. A tax on tobacco raises its price and reduces smoking, a tax on sugar raises the price of sugary drinks and reduces consumption, and a subsidy for electric cars lowers their price and encourages their purchase. The use of prices to change behaviour is called a price instrument, and it is often more efficient than a ban, because it lets people decide for themselves whether the good is worth the higher price, and it raises revenue that can be used for other purposes.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why is water cheap and diamonds expensive? Because price depends on scarcity and on the value of the last unit, not on total usefulness: water is abundant, so the last litre is worth little, while diamonds are scarce, so the last diamond is worth a great deal. Do prices always reflect costs? No, prices also reflect demand, competition, and market power, and a firm with a monopoly can charge far more than its costs. Why do some prices never change? Because of contracts, because changing prices is costly, and because customers dislike frequent changes, so firms change prices only when the gain is worth the trouble. Can prices be negative? Rarely, but it has happened in electricity markets, where producers pay to supply power when there is too much of it, and in oil markets in April 2020, when the price of a futures contract briefly went negative. Why do prices end in 99? Because of psychological pricing: 9.99 pounds feels significantly cheaper than 10 pounds, even though the difference is one penny, and experiments show that it increases sales. Do firms always charge the highest price they can? No, a firm that charges too much loses customers to rivals, and a firm that charges too little leaves money on the table, so it seeks the price that maximises its profit, which depends on its costs and on the elasticity of demand. Are prices fair? Fairness is a judgement, not a fact, and economists study what people consider fair, finding that people accept price rises caused by higher costs more readily than price rises caused by higher demand, even though the effect on the buyer is the same.

Definition: Money is anything widely accepted as a means of payment, a store of value, and a unit of account. It lets people trade without barter, it lets them save for the future, and it lets them compare the prices of different things in a common unit. Modern money is mostly bank deposits, moved electronically, with coins and notes as a small part, and its value rests on trust: people accept money because they believe others will accept it too. Central banks issue the notes and coins, and they control the supply of money in the economy. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Money began as commodities: shells, grain, and cattle were used as money in early societies, and the first metal coins appeared in Lydia in the 7th century BCE. Paper money appeared in China in the 7th century, and it spread to Europe in the 17th century, when banks issued notes backed by gold. The gold standard, under which currencies could be exchanged for gold, dominated the 19th and early 20th centuries, but it was abandoned in the 1930s and finally ended in 1971, when the United States stopped converting dollars into gold. Since then, money has been fiat money, money whose value comes from the government's promise and from public trust, not from a commodity. In the 21st century, digital payments have grown rapidly, and central banks are studying digital currencies of their own. Money has also been studied as a social institution. The anthropologist and economist Karl Polanyi argued that money is not only a means of exchange but also a social relation, and that the history of money is the history of how societies organise trust. The sociologist Viviana Zelizer showed that people treat money differently depending on its source: money earned, money given as a gift, and money won are spent differently, a finding called the social meaning of money. These perspectives complement the economic view, by showing that money is not only a neutral tool but also a carrier of meaning and of social relations.

Key concepts: A medium of exchange is something accepted in trade, so a seller will take it in return for goods. A store of value is something that holds its purchasing power over time, so savings do not lose their worth. A unit of account is a standard measure in which prices are quoted, so different goods can be compared. Fiat money is money with no intrinsic value, declared legal tender by a government. A central bank is the institution that issues a country's currency and controls its money supply, such as the Bank of England, the European Central Bank, and the Federal Reserve in the United States. Broad money is a measure of the money supply that includes bank deposits as well as notes and coins, and it is the measure most often used by central banks. Narrow money is a measure that includes only notes, coins, and deposits that can be spent immediately. The velocity of money is the rate at which money circulates, measured as GDP divided by the money supply, and it falls when people hold money rather than spending it. Seigniorage is the profit a government makes from issuing money, because it costs little to produce a note that is worth much more. A bank run is a panic in which depositors withdraw their money at once, which can bring down a bank.

How it works: Money works because it solves the problem of barter, which requires a double coincidence of wants: a baker who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants bread. With money, the baker sells bread for money and uses the money to buy shoes, and the shoemaker does the same. Banks create most money in a modern economy: when a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a deposit, and that deposit is money that can be spent. The central bank influences this process by setting interest rates and by buying and selling assets, which affects how much banks lend and how much money circulates. The value of money depends on its supply relative to the goods it can buy: if the supply grows faster than the economy, prices rise and money loses value. Money also has a history of debasement. Rulers in the past reduced the precious metal in coins to pay their debts, which caused inflation, and the same logic applies today: a government that prints too much money causes inflation, because the supply of money grows faster than the supply of goods. The discipline of money is therefore a discipline on governments: a government that wants stable prices must not print money to pay its bills, and that is why central banks are given independence, to prevent the temptation to print.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A worker is paid 2,000 pounds a month, and she spends 800 pounds on rent, 400 pounds on food, 200 pounds on transport, and 300 pounds on other things, saving 300 pounds. Her money is a unit of account, because all the prices are in pounds, a medium of exchange, because she pays with it, and a store of value, because her savings of 300 pounds a month will still buy things next year, unless prices rise. A second example is inflation: if prices rise by 10 percent in a year, 100 pounds saved today will buy only about 91 pounds' worth of goods next year, because 100 divided by 1.10 is about 91. A third example is a bank loan: a bank lends 10,000 pounds, creating a deposit of 10,000 pounds, and the borrower spends it, so the money circulates through the economy. A second worked example uses the quantity theory of money. The theory says that the money supply times the velocity of money equals the price level times real GDP. If the money supply grows by 10 percent, velocity is unchanged, and real GDP grows by 2 percent, then the price level must rise by about 8 percent, because 10 minus 2 is 8. This is a simplification, and velocity changes, but the theory captures the basic link between money and prices, and it is why central banks watch the growth of the money supply, even though they now target interest rates rather than the money supply directly.

Real applications: Money is used in every transaction, and the study of money, called monetary economics, is central to policy. Central banks use interest rates to control inflation, and governments use the money supply to manage the economy. The payment system, the network of banks, cards, and electronic transfers, moves trillions of pounds every day, and its reliability is essential to modern life. The rise of digital money, from bank cards to mobile payments to cryptocurrencies, is changing how money works, and central banks are studying whether to issue digital currencies of their own, which would be a new form of money for the digital age. Money is also changing with technology. Mobile payments, contactless cards, and online banking have made cash less important, and in some countries, such as Sweden, cash is now used for only a small share of transactions. Cryptocurrencies, such as Bitcoin, launched in 2009, use cryptography and a distributed ledger to create a form of money without a central issuer, though their value is volatile and they are not widely used for everyday payments. Central banks are studying digital currencies, which would be a new form of money, issued by the central bank and held in digital wallets, and several countries have already launched them.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is money the same as wealth? No, wealth is the total value of what a person owns, including houses and shares, while money is only the part that can be spent immediately. Does printing money make a country richer? No, printing money increases the supply of money, not the supply of goods, and if it is done too fast it causes inflation, as happened in Germany in 1923 and in Zimbabwe in 2008. Are cryptocurrencies money? They are used as money by some people, but they are not widely accepted, their value is very volatile, and most central banks do not recognise them as legal tender. Why do we trust money? Because the government accepts it for taxes, because banks are regulated, and because everyone else accepts it, which is a self-reinforcing trust. Is cash disappearing? In many countries cash use is falling, but cash is still important for people without bank accounts, for small transactions, and as a backup when electronic systems fail, so most central banks say cash will remain available. Is Bitcoin money? It is used as money by some people, but it is not widely accepted, its value changes sharply, and it is not recognised as legal tender by most governments, so most economists call it a speculative asset rather than money. Does the amount of money in the economy matter? Yes, the money supply affects prices and interest rates, and central banks monitor it, though they now use interest rates as their main tool, not the money supply.

Inflation

Definition: Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices, which reduces the purchasing power of money. If prices rise by 3 percent in a year, 100 pounds buys 3 percent less than it did a year before. Inflation is measured by price indices, such as the Consumer Prices Index, which tracks the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by a typical household. A little inflation, around 2 percent a year, is the target of most central banks, but high inflation damages savings, distorts decisions, and can lead to a loss of confidence in money itself. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Inflation has been a problem throughout history. In the 16th century, the arrival of silver from the Americas raised prices across Europe, in what is called the price revolution. In the 20th century, Germany experienced hyperinflation in 1923, when prices doubled every few days and people carried money in wheelbarrows. After the Second World War, most countries experienced moderate inflation, and in the 1970s oil price rises pushed inflation above 20 percent in the United Kingdom. Central banks responded by raising interest rates, and by the 1990s most advanced economies had brought inflation down to low single figures. Inflation rose sharply again in 2021 and 2022, after the pandemic and the rise in energy prices, and central banks raised interest rates to bring it back down. Inflation is also measured in different ways. The Consumer Prices Index, or CPI, is the main measure in the United Kingdom, and it tracks the prices of a basket of about 700 goods and services. The Retail Prices Index, or RPI, is an older measure, which includes housing costs in a different way, and it is still used for some purposes, such as index-linked bonds. The GDP deflator is a broader measure, which covers all goods and services produced, not only those bought by consumers. Each measure gives a slightly different number, and economists use the CPI for most purposes, because it is the measure targeted by the central bank.

Key concepts: The Consumer Prices Index, or CPI, is the main measure of inflation in the United Kingdom and in many other countries; it tracks the prices of hundreds of goods and services. The inflation rate is the percentage change in the CPI over a year. Deflation is a fall in the general price level, which can be damaging because it encourages people to delay spending. Hyperinflation is very rapid inflation, usually defined as prices rising by more than 50 percent in a month. Real values are values adjusted for inflation, while nominal values are the raw numbers, unadjusted. Core inflation is inflation excluding food and energy, whose prices are volatile, and it is used to judge the underlying trend. Headline inflation is the full measure, including food and energy, and it is the number most people see. Stagflation is the combination of high inflation and slow growth, which happened in the 1970s and is difficult for policy, because raising rates to fight inflation worsens growth, while cutting rates to support growth worsens inflation. Disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate, which is not the same as deflation, a fall in the price level itself.

How it works: Inflation happens when the total demand for goods and services grows faster than the supply, or when the costs of production rise. Demand-pull inflation happens when spending rises, for example when the government spends more or when interest rates are low, and sellers raise prices because buyers are willing to pay more. Cost-push inflation happens when the cost of an input, such as oil or wages, rises, and firms pass the higher cost on in higher prices. Expectations matter too: if people expect prices to rise, they demand higher wages, and firms raise prices in anticipation, which can make inflation self-fulfilling. Central banks try to keep expectations anchored at their target, by setting interest rates and by communicating their plans. Inflation also affects the distribution of income. It hurts people on fixed incomes, such as pensioners, whose income does not rise with prices, and it hurts savers, whose savings lose value. It helps borrowers, whose debts are worth less in real terms, and it helps the government, whose debt is worth less, though it also raises the cost of borrowing. Inflation can also distort decisions: it makes it harder to tell whether a price rise reflects a real change in scarcity or only a general rise in prices, and it encourages people to spend money quickly rather than saving it, which can reduce investment.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A loaf of bread costs 1 pound in year one and 1.03 pounds in year two, so inflation for bread is 3 percent. If a worker's wage rises from 2,000 pounds to 2,040 pounds in the same year, a rise of 2 percent, her real wage has fallen, because prices rose faster than her pay: 2,040 divided by 1.03 is about 1,981 pounds in year-one terms. A second example is savings: 1,000 pounds in a bank account earning 1 percent interest grows to 1,010 pounds, but if inflation is 5 percent, the real value falls to about 962 pounds, because 1,010 divided by 1.05 is about 962. A third example is a loan: a borrower who borrows 10,000 pounds and repays it after a year of 10 percent inflation repays money worth less in real terms, so inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders. A second worked example uses the rule of 70 for inflation. If inflation is 3 percent a year, prices double in about 23 years, because 70 divided by 3 is about 23. If inflation is 10 percent, prices double in about 7 years, and if inflation is 2 percent, prices double in about 35 years. This shows why a small difference in the inflation rate matters over a lifetime: at 2 percent, a person's savings lose half their value in 35 years, while at 10 percent they lose half in 7 years. The rule is the same as for growth, because both are about compounding.

Real applications: Inflation matters to everyone. It affects the real value of wages, savings, and pensions, and it affects the decisions of firms, which must set prices and wages in an uncertain environment. Central banks use interest rates to control it, and governments index some payments, such as pensions, to inflation, so that their real value is protected. The study of inflation is therefore central to economic policy, and the experience of high inflation in the 1970s and again in 2021 and 2022 is a reminder that price stability cannot be taken for granted, and that it must be defended by policy. Inflation is controlled by monetary policy, by fiscal policy, and by expectations. The central bank raises interest rates to cool demand, the government can reduce its deficit to cool demand, and both can try to anchor expectations, by committing to a target and by acting consistently. The experience of the 1970s showed that allowing inflation to become entrenched is costly, because it then takes a recession to bring it down, and the experience of the 2020s showed that inflation can return quickly when supply is disrupted, even after decades of low inflation. Price stability is therefore a continuing task, not a one-time achievement.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is inflation always bad? A little inflation is normal and is the target of most central banks, because it gives room to cut interest rates in a downturn and it avoids the dangers of deflation. Is deflation good, because prices fall? No, deflation can be damaging, because it encourages people to delay spending, it raises the real burden of debt, and it is hard for central banks to fight. Does inflation affect everyone equally? No, it hurts people on fixed incomes and people with savings, while it helps borrowers, and it hurts the poor more than the rich, because the poor spend a larger share of their income on essentials whose prices rise fastest. Can inflation be stopped quickly? It can be reduced by raising interest rates, but that slows the economy and can raise unemployment, so central banks face a trade-off. Is 2 percent inflation the right target? Most central banks use 2 percent, because it gives room to cut rates in a downturn and it avoids deflation, but some economists argue for a higher target, to give more room, and others argue for a lower target, to protect savers. Does inflation cause unemployment? In the short run, high inflation can be associated with low unemployment, but in the long run the relationship is weak, and the best policy is to keep inflation low and stable, so that the economy can grow without the distortions of high inflation. Can inflation be predicted? It can be forecast, but forecasts are uncertain, especially when supply shocks, such as oil price rises, occur, which is why central banks update their forecasts regularly.

Definition: An interest rate is the price of borrowing money, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed, usually per year. If you borrow 1,000 pounds at 5 percent a year, you pay 50 pounds in interest in a year. Interest rates are set in markets, where the supply of savings meets the demand for loans, and they are influenced by central banks, which set a base rate that affects all other rates. Interest rates matter because they affect how much people save, how much they borrow, and how much firms invest. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Interest has been charged for thousands of years: the earliest loans, in Mesopotamia, charged interest in grain, and the first banks in Renaissance Italy lent at rates set by the market. In the 19th century, the Bank of England set its Bank Rate to control the gold standard, and in the 20th century central banks began to use interest rates as their main tool of economic policy. After the financial crisis of 2008, central banks in the United States, the United Kingdom, and the euro area cut rates to near zero and kept them there for years, and some, in Europe and Japan, set negative rates, charging banks to hold deposits. In 2021 and 2022, as inflation rose, central banks raised rates sharply, in the fastest tightening in decades, to bring inflation back down. Interest rates also vary by term. A short-term rate, such as the rate on a three-month loan, is usually lower than a long-term rate, such as the rate on a ten-year bond, because lending for longer is riskier. The yield curve is a graph of interest rates against the length of the loan, and it is normally upward sloping, but it can invert, when short rates rise above long rates, which has often predicted a recession. The spread between the rates on safe government bonds and on risky corporate bonds, called the credit spread, widens in a crisis, when lenders fear default, and narrows in good times. These are the signals that financial markets watch most closely.

Key concepts: The base rate, or policy rate, is the rate set by a central bank, which influences the rates banks charge each other and their customers. The real interest rate is the nominal rate minus inflation, so it measures the true cost of borrowing. Compound interest is interest paid on interest as well as on the original amount, so a sum grows faster over time. A mortgage is a loan secured on a house, and its rate is one of the most important interest rates for households. A bond is a loan made by an investor to a government or a company, and its yield is the interest rate the borrower pays. The nominal interest rate is the rate quoted, without adjusting for inflation. The real interest rate is the nominal rate minus inflation, and it is the true cost of borrowing. The effective interest rate is the rate actually paid, after fees and compounding, and it is the rate that matters for a borrower. The risk premium is the extra return a lender demands for taking a risk, such as lending to a company that might default. The term premium is the extra return for lending for a long time rather than a short time.

How it works: Interest rates work by changing the cost of borrowing and the reward for saving. When rates rise, borrowing becomes more expensive, so households borrow less for houses and cars, and firms borrow less to invest, which slows spending and cools inflation. When rates fall, borrowing becomes cheaper, spending rises, and the economy grows faster. Rates also affect the exchange rate: higher rates attract foreign savers, who buy the currency to earn the higher return, and the currency rises, which makes imports cheaper and exports dearer. Central banks set rates by judging the balance between inflation and growth, and they change them in small steps, watching the data as they go. Interest rates also affect the housing market. When rates rise, mortgages become more expensive, demand for houses falls, and house prices fall or rise more slowly. When rates fall, mortgages become cheaper, demand rises, and house prices rise. The housing market is therefore one of the main channels through which monetary policy affects the economy, and it is why changes in the base rate are felt quickly by homeowners with variable-rate mortgages, and more slowly by those with fixed rates, whose payments change only when the fixed period ends.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A loan of 10,000 pounds at 5 percent simple interest costs 500 pounds a year, so after three years the borrower has paid 1,500 pounds in interest. A second example is a mortgage: a loan of 200,000 pounds at 4 percent a year costs 8,000 pounds in interest in the first year, though the monthly payment also repays some of the capital. A second worked example uses the rule of 70 for compound interest. If savings earn 5 percent a year, they double in about 14 years, because 70 divided by 5 is 14. If they earn 2 percent, they double in about 35 years, and if they earn 10 percent, they double in about 7 years. The rule shows the power of compounding: a small difference in the rate makes a large difference over time, which is why starting to save early matters so much, and why high-interest debt grows so fast if it is not repaid.

Real applications: Interest rates are the main tool of monetary policy, and they affect every household and firm. A rise in rates makes mortgages more expensive, which reduces the money households have to spend, and it makes saving more attractive, which reduces spending further. A fall in rates has the opposite effect. Rates also affect the government's cost of borrowing, the price of shares and houses, and the exchange rate, so they are watched closely by everyone from homebuyers to investors. The study of interest rates is therefore the study of how the price of money shapes the whole economy. Interest rates are also a matter of fairness. Savers benefit from high rates, because they earn more on their savings, while borrowers suffer, because they pay more on their loans. The balance between savers and borrowers is a political issue, and central banks must consider it, though their mandate is to control inflation, not to favour one group over another. The study of interest rates is therefore not only technical but also distributional, because a change in the rate redistributes income between savers and borrowers, between young and old, and between the rich and the poor.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are interest rates the same for everyone? No, the rate a person pays depends on the risk the lender takes, on the length of the loan, and on whether it is secured, so a mortgage is cheaper than a credit card, and a borrower with a good record pays less than one with a poor record. Do higher interest rates always reduce inflation? They slow spending, which reduces inflation, but they also raise costs for firms, and they can raise unemployment, so central banks must judge the balance. Can interest rates be negative? Yes, some central banks have set negative rates, charging banks to hold deposits, to encourage lending, though negative rates are unusual and controversial. Is saving always better when rates are high? Higher rates raise the return on saving, but if inflation is higher still, the real return can be negative, so the real rate is what matters. Are interest rates the same in every country? No, they differ because inflation differs, because risk differs, and because central banks set different rates. In the 2020s, rates were near zero in Japan and in the euro area, and higher in the United States and the United Kingdom, and the differences affected exchange rates and capital flows. Do interest rates affect shares? Yes, higher rates make bonds more attractive relative to shares, so share prices often fall when rates rise, and they make borrowing more expensive for firms, which reduces their profits. Is a high interest rate a sign of a strong economy? Not necessarily: rates can be high because inflation is high, which is a sign of an overheating economy, or because risk is high, which is a sign of a weak economy.

Definition: Gross Domestic Product, or GDP, is the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a given period, usually a year or a quarter. It is the most widely used measure of the size of an economy, and its growth rate is the most widely used measure of how fast an economy is growing. GDP can be measured by adding up spending, by adding up income, or by adding up the value added at each stage of production, and in theory the three methods give the same answer. GDP per person, or GDP per capita, is GDP divided by the population, and it is a rough measure of average living standards. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: GDP was developed in the 1930s and 1940s, when governments needed to measure the economy to manage the Depression and then the war effort. Simon Kuznets, working in the United States in the 1930s, developed the first national income accounts, and John Maynard Keynes's work on the economy as a whole gave the framework. During the Second World War, the British economist Richard Stone developed the system of national accounts that became the international standard, and in 1953 the United Nations published the first System of National Accounts, which most countries now follow. Kuznets won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1971 for his work on national income, and he warned from the start that GDP was not a measure of welfare, a warning that is still repeated today. GDP is also compared across countries using purchasing power parity, or PPP, which adjusts for the different prices in different countries. A haircut that costs 10 pounds in London might cost 2 pounds in Delhi, so comparing GDP at market exchange rates understates the living standards in poorer countries, where services are cheaper. PPP adjusts for this, and on a PPP basis China's GDP is larger than that of the United States, though on a market basis the United States is larger. The choice of measure matters for comparisons, and economists use both, for different purposes.

Key concepts: Nominal GDP is GDP measured at current prices, while real GDP is GDP adjusted for inflation, so it measures the change in the volume of production. GDP per capita is GDP divided by the population. Value added is the value a firm adds to its inputs, which is its output minus the cost of the goods and services it buys in. The three approaches to GDP are the expenditure approach, adding up consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports, the income approach, adding up wages, profits, and rents, and the production approach, adding up value added across all firms. A recession is commonly defined as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP, though official definitions vary by country. Gross National Income, or GNI, is GDP plus income from abroad minus income paid abroad, and it is a better measure of the income of a country's residents than GDP, which measures production within its borders. Net Domestic Product is GDP minus depreciation, the wear and tear on capital, and it is a better measure of sustainable production, though it is harder to measure. The informal economy is production that is not recorded, such as cash-in-hand work, and it can be large in poor countries, where it may be a third or more of the economy, so official GDP understates true production. Green GDP is an attempt to adjust GDP for environmental damage, though it is not yet an official measure.

How it works: GDP is measured by statistical agencies, such as the Office for National Statistics in the United Kingdom and the Bureau of Economic Analysis in the United States, which collect data from surveys of firms and households, from tax records, and from government spending. The expenditure approach adds up four parts: consumption, the spending of households; investment, the spending of firms on buildings and equipment and of households on new houses; government spending on goods and services; and net exports, which is exports minus imports. The result is the total value of final goods and services, counting intermediate goods only once, through value added, so that a car's steel is not counted separately from the car itself. The figures are revised as more data arrive, and they are published quarterly, with a first estimate followed by revisions. GDP is measured by statistical agencies using surveys, tax records, and administrative data, and the figures are revised as more data become available. The first estimate of GDP for a quarter is published about a month after the quarter ends, and it is revised twice more in the following months, and again in annual revisions. The revisions can be large, especially in a crisis, when data are hard to collect, and the first estimate is therefore treated with caution. The measurement of GDP is a major statistical exercise, involving thousands of people and billions of data points.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A country produces 1,000 loaves of bread at 1 pound each and 500 shirts at 10 pounds each, so its GDP is 1,000 pounds plus 5,000 pounds, or 6,000 pounds. A second example is the United States, whose GDP is about 27 trillion dollars, the largest in the world, and China, whose GDP is about 18 trillion dollars, the second largest, though China's GDP per capita is much lower because its population is larger. A third example is a recession: if real GDP falls for two quarters in a row, the economy is commonly said to be in recession, though the official judgement may use more evidence. A second worked example uses PPP. A GDP of 100 billion pounds is 125 billion dollars at the market rate but 200 billion dollars at the PPP rate, a difference of 60 percent, which shows how much the choice of rate matters for comparisons. The World Bank publishes PPP figures for most countries, and they are used to compare living standards, while market rates are used to compare the size of economies in world trade.

Real applications: GDP is used to compare the size of economies, to track growth, and to set policy. Governments use it to judge whether the economy is growing or shrinking, and central banks use it to set interest rates. International organisations use it to compare living standards, though GDP per capita is only a rough guide, because it says nothing about how income is shared out or about health, education, and the environment. The limitations of GDP have led to the development of other measures, such as the Human Development Index, which combines income with health and education, but GDP remains the headline measure of economic activity. GDP is also used to judge the sustainability of debt. The debt-to-GDP ratio, the national debt divided by GDP, is the standard measure of whether a country's debt is high or low. Japan's ratio is over 250 percent, the highest in the world, while the United Kingdom's is about 100 percent, and the United States' is about 120 percent. A high ratio does not necessarily mean a crisis, because it depends on the interest rate, on growth, and on who holds the debt, but it does mean that a larger share of the budget goes on interest payments, which leaves less for other spending.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does GDP measure happiness? No, GDP measures production, not welfare, and it does not count unpaid work, such as caring for children, it does not count the value of a clean environment, and it counts some things, such as spending on pollution clean-up, as positive even though they reflect a harm. Is a bigger GDP always better? Not necessarily: GDP can grow while inequality rises, while the environment is damaged, or while people work longer hours for the same pay. Does GDP count illegal activity? In some countries, such as the United Kingdom, the official GDP includes an estimate for illegal drugs and prostitution, because the international standard says all production should be counted, though the estimates are uncertain. Is GDP per capita the same as average income? Roughly, but not exactly, because GDP includes profits that go to foreign owners and excludes some income from abroad, so national income per person is a slightly different measure. Is GDP per capita a good measure of living standards? It is a rough guide, but it does not measure inequality, health, education, or the environment, and it can be misleading: a country with high GDP per capita but high inequality may have many poor people, and a country with lower GDP per capita but better health and education may have higher wellbeing. Does GDP grow when a country pollutes? Yes, GDP counts the production that causes pollution as positive, and it counts the clean-up as positive too, but it does not subtract the damage, which is why green GDP and other measures have been proposed. Is GDP the only measure that matters? No, economists now use a dashboard of measures, including health, education, inequality, and the environment, to judge how an economy is doing.

Definition: Unemployment is the state of being without a job while being available for work and actively looking for it. The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labour force, which is the total of those in work and those looking for work, expressed as a percentage. Unemployment matters because it wastes human talent, it reduces incomes, and it can damage health and wellbeing. Economists distinguish several types of unemployment, and the causes and the cures differ by type. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Unemployment became a central concern in the Great Depression of the 1930s, when unemployment in the United States rose above 20 percent and in the United Kingdom above 15 percent. John Maynard Keynes argued in 1936 that unemployment could persist because demand was too low, and that government spending could restore it. After the Second World War, most advanced economies kept unemployment low, below 3 percent in the United Kingdom in the 1950s and 1960s, but it rose sharply in the 1970s and 1980s, reaching over 10 percent in the United Kingdom in the early 1980s. The financial crisis of 2008 pushed unemployment up again, to about 10 percent in the United States and 8 percent in the United Kingdom, before it fell back in the 2010s. The pandemic of 2020 caused a sharp but brief rise, and governments paid wages to keep people in jobs, a policy called furlough in the United Kingdom. Unemployment is also studied in relation to vacancies. The Beveridge curve is a graph of unemployment against vacancies, and it shows that when unemployment is high, vacancies are low, and vice versa, though the curve can shift if matching becomes less efficient, as it did after the financial crisis, when many workers' skills did not match the jobs available. The ratio of vacancies to unemployed people is a measure of how tight the labour market is: when there are more vacancies than unemployed people, employers must raise wages to attract workers, and when there are fewer, workers must accept lower wages. In 2022 and 2023, the United Kingdom had more vacancies than unemployed people for the first time, a sign of a very tight labour market.

Key concepts: The labour force is the total of people in work and people looking for work. The unemployment rate is the unemployed divided by the labour force, times 100. Frictional unemployment is the short-term unemployment of people moving between jobs. Structural unemployment is the longer-term unemployment caused by a mismatch between workers' skills and the jobs available, or by a decline in an industry. Cyclical unemployment is the unemployment caused by a downturn in the economy, when demand is too low. The natural rate of unemployment is the rate that remains when the economy is at its normal level, made up of frictional and structural unemployment. Underemployment is the situation where people work fewer hours than they want, or in jobs below their skills, and it is not counted in the unemployment rate, though it is a form of wasted talent. Long-term unemployment is unemployment lasting more than a year, and it is particularly damaging, because skills decay and employers become reluctant to hire. Youth unemployment is unemployment among young people, which is usually higher than the overall rate, because young people have less experience and are more likely to be in temporary jobs. Hidden unemployment is unemployment that is not counted, because people have given up looking or have taken early retirement.

How it works: Unemployment is measured by surveys, such as the Labour Force Survey in the United Kingdom and the Current Population Survey in the United States, which ask a sample of households about their work. A person is counted as unemployed if they are without a job, available to start work within two weeks, and have actively looked for work in the last four weeks. The rate is calculated by dividing the number of unemployed by the labour force. Governments try to reduce unemployment by raising demand, by training workers, and by helping people find jobs, and the appropriate policy depends on the type: demand policies help cyclical unemployment, while training and retraining help structural unemployment, and better job matching helps frictional unemployment. Unemployment also has a history of policy responses. In the 1930s, governments used public works to create jobs, in the 1940s and 1950s they used demand management to maintain full employment, and in the 1980s they used training and deregulation to make the labour market more flexible. In the 2020s, governments used furlough schemes to keep people in jobs during the pandemic, paying a share of their wages while they could not work, a policy that kept unemployment low but raised the national debt. Each response reflects the ideas of its time, and each has been judged by its results.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A labour force of 30 million people has 1.5 million unemployed, so the unemployment rate is 1.5 divided by 30, times 100, which is 5 percent. If 300,000 people find jobs, the unemployed fall to 1.2 million and the rate falls to 4 percent. A second example is the United States in the Great Depression: the labour force was about 50 million, and unemployment peaked at about 25 percent in 1933, meaning about 12.5 million people were out of work. A third example is the United Kingdom in 2024, where the unemployment rate was about 4.4 percent, close to its pre-pandemic level, though the number of people out of the labour force altogether, through sickness or early retirement, was a separate concern. A second worked example uses the Beveridge curve. Suppose the labour force is 30 million, unemployment is 1.5 million, or 5 percent, and vacancies are 1 million, so there are 0.67 vacancies per unemployed person. If vacancies rise to 2 million, the ratio rises to 1.33, and employers must compete for workers, so wages rise. If unemployment rises to 3 million and vacancies fall to 500,000, the ratio falls to 0.17, and workers must compete for jobs, so wages fall or stay flat. The ratio is a simple summary of the balance of power in the labour market.

Real applications: Unemployment is a key indicator for policy. Central banks watch it to judge how much spare capacity the economy has, and governments watch it to judge the health of the labour market. High unemployment wastes the skills of workers, reduces tax revenue, and increases spending on benefits, and it can have lasting effects: people who are unemployed for a long time find it harder to get back into work, a problem called hysteresis. The study of unemployment is therefore the study of how to keep people in work, how to help them move between jobs, and how to ensure that growth benefits everyone, not only those already in work. Unemployment is also linked to mental health. Studies show that unemployment is associated with worse health, with lower life satisfaction, and with a higher risk of depression, and that the effect is larger the longer unemployment lasts. The cost of unemployment is therefore not only economic but also human, and it is why governments aim to keep unemployment low, not only to raise output but also to protect wellbeing. The study of unemployment is therefore a study of both the economy and of human welfare.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the unemployment rate the same as the number of people without jobs? No, it counts only those who are looking for work; people who have given up looking, or who are not available, are counted as out of the labour force, not as unemployed. Does low unemployment always mean a healthy economy? Not necessarily: it can mean that many people are in low-paid or insecure work, or that people have left the labour force altogether. Can unemployment ever be zero? No, there is always some frictional unemployment, as people move between jobs, so the natural rate is above zero, usually estimated at 3 to 5 percent in advanced economies. Does unemployment cause inflation to fall? In the short run, high unemployment can hold wages down, which can reduce inflation, but the relationship is not as simple as it once seemed, and it has weakened in recent decades. Is unemployment a choice? Most economists say no: most unemployed people want to work, and they are unemployed because there are not enough jobs, or because their skills do not match, or because they are between jobs. Does unemployment benefit cause unemployment? Benefits can reduce the incentive to take a job, but they also let people search for a better match, and the evidence is that the effect is small for moderate benefits, though it grows if benefits are very generous or last a long time. Can the government guarantee a job for everyone? Some economists propose a job guarantee, where the government offers a job to anyone who wants one, but the cost and the design are debated, and no country has adopted a full job guarantee.

Definition: Trade is the exchange of goods and services between people, firms, and countries. International trade is trade between countries, and it lets each country specialise in what it does best and buy from others what it does less well. Trade increases the total amount produced, because it lets resources be used where they are most productive, and it gives consumers more choice and lower prices. Trade is governed by agreements between countries, and it is one of the most powerful forces in the world economy. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Trade is ancient: the Silk Road carried goods between China and Europe for centuries, and the spice trade shaped the history of the early modern world. Adam Smith in 1776 argued that trade benefits both sides, and David Ricardo in 1817 developed the theory of comparative advantage, showing that trade benefits a country even if it is less efficient at producing everything, as long as it specialises in what it is relatively best at. In the 19th century, free trade spread, led by the United Kingdom's repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846. After the Second World War, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, signed in 1947, and its successor, the World Trade Organization, founded in 1995, reduced tariffs around the world, and trade grew faster than the world economy for decades. In recent years, trade has become more contested, with debates about its effects on jobs and on the environment. Trade has also been studied in terms of its effects on the environment. Trade can increase emissions, by transporting goods around the world and by letting production move to countries with weaker environmental rules, a phenomenon called carbon leakage. It can also reduce emissions, by letting countries specialise in what they do efficiently and by spreading clean technology. The net effect is debated, and the policy response is to price carbon, so that the environmental cost is included in the price, and to include environmental standards in trade agreements, so that trade does not undermine climate policy.

Key concepts: Comparative advantage is the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer, and it is the basis of trade. A tariff is a tax on imports, which raises their price and protects domestic producers. A quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported. Free trade is trade without tariffs or quotas, while protectionism is the policy of protecting domestic firms from foreign competition. A trade deficit is the amount by which imports exceed exports, and a trade surplus is the opposite. The balance of payments is the record of all transactions between a country and the rest of the world. A free trade agreement is an agreement between countries to reduce tariffs and other barriers to trade. A customs union is a group of countries that trade freely among themselves and set a common tariff on imports from outside, such as the European Union's customs union. A single market is a deeper integration, where goods, services, capital, and people move freely, as in the European Union. The World Trade Organization is the global body that sets the rules of trade and settles disputes, with 164 members. A non-tariff barrier is a barrier to trade other than a tariff, such as a regulation or a quota.

How it works: Trade works through specialisation. A country that is relatively good at making wine and less good at making cloth will specialise in wine, trade it for cloth, and end up with more of both than if it tried to make both itself. The same logic applies to firms and to people: a lawyer who is also good at typing will still hire a typist, because the lawyer's time is better spent on law. Trade also works through competition: foreign competition pushes domestic firms to become more efficient, and it gives consumers lower prices and more choice. The gains from trade are real, but they are not shared equally: some workers and firms lose from trade, especially in industries that face import competition, and the debate about trade is largely about how to share the gains and help those who lose. Trade also affects the distribution of income within a country. The Stolper-Samuelson theorem, named after Wolfgang Stolper and Paul Samuelson, says that trade benefits the factor of production that a country has in abundance and harms the factor it has little of, so in a rich country trade benefits skilled workers and harms unskilled workers, while in a poor country it benefits unskilled workers. This is why trade is controversial: it raises total income, but it changes who gains and who loses, and the losers may be concentrated in particular regions and industries, where the costs are visible and the gains are spread thinly.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. Two countries, A and B, each have 100 workers. Country A can produce 10 bottles of wine or 5 metres of cloth per worker, while country B can produce 5 bottles of wine or 5 metres of cloth per worker. Country A has a comparative advantage in wine, because it gives up half a metre of cloth for a bottle, while country B gives up a whole metre, so A should specialise in wine and B in cloth, and trade. If A produces only wine, it makes 1,000 bottles, and if B produces only cloth, it makes 500 metres, and by trading they can both have more of both goods than before. A second example is the real world: China exports electronics and the United States exports aircraft, and both gain from the exchange, though the workers in the industries that shrink face real costs. A second worked example uses the gains from trade. Suppose a country can produce 100 units of wine or 100 units of cloth on its own, for a total of 200 units. By specialising in wine and trading for cloth, it can obtain 120 units of wine and 120 units of cloth, for a total of 240 units, a gain of 40 units, or 20 percent. The gain comes from specialisation, and it is shared between the trading partners, though the division depends on the terms of trade, the price at which wine is exchanged for cloth. If the terms of trade move in a country's favour, it gains more, and if they move against it, it gains less.

Real applications: Trade is used to raise living standards, to spread technology, and to build relationships between countries. It is governed by the World Trade Organization, which sets rules and settles disputes, and by regional agreements, such as the European Union's single market and the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement. Trade policy is a major political issue, because it affects jobs, prices, and the environment, and the debate about trade is one of the oldest in economics, going back to the Corn Laws in the 19th century. The study of trade is therefore the study of how the world economy fits together, and of who gains and who loses when it does. Trade is also a source of ideas. When firms export, they learn from foreign customers and competitors, and when they import, they gain access to new inputs and technologies. The spread of technology through trade is one of the main ways poor countries catch up with rich ones, and it is why open economies have grown faster than closed ones, on average, though the relationship is not simple, and the institutions and policies of a country matter as much as its openness to trade.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does trade benefit everyone? No, trade raises total income, but some workers and firms lose, and the gains are not shared automatically, so governments use taxes and benefits to share them. Is a trade deficit bad? Not necessarily: a deficit means a country is importing more than it exports, which can reflect investment from abroad or a strong economy, and the deficit must be financed by borrowing or by selling assets, which is sustainable only if the borrowing is used well. Should countries protect their industries? That is a matter of judgement: protection can save jobs in the short run, but it raises prices for consumers and can make firms less efficient in the long run. Is free trade the same as no rules? No, free trade needs rules, to prevent fraud, to protect health and safety, and to settle disputes, and the World Trade Organization provides those rules. Is trade the cause of job losses in manufacturing? Trade has contributed to the decline of manufacturing employment in rich countries, but technology has contributed more, by automating production, and the rise of services has also played a part. Should a country aim for a trade surplus? A surplus means a country exports more than it imports, which can reflect competitiveness, but it also means it is lending to the rest of the world, and a deficit can reflect investment from abroad, so neither is good or bad in itself. Is protectionism ever justified? Economists generally favour free trade, but they recognise exceptions, such as protecting an infant industry that may become competitive, or protecting national security, though both are often used as excuses for protection that benefits a few at the expense of many.

Competition and Monopoly

Definition: Competition is the rivalry between firms for customers, and it is one of the most powerful forces in an economy. When firms compete, they cut prices, improve quality, and innovate, because a firm that does not will lose customers to its rivals. A monopoly is the opposite: a single firm that supplies a whole market, with no rivals, and it can charge higher prices and supply less, because its customers have nowhere else to go. The study of competition and monopoly is the study of how market structure shapes prices, quality, and innovation. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: The study of competition began with Adam Smith in 1776, who argued that competition disciplines firms and serves the public interest. In the 19th century, the rise of large firms, such as the railways and Standard Oil in the United States, led to the first competition laws: the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 in the United States, and similar laws in other countries, which banned monopolies and cartels. In the 20th century, economists developed the theory of market structures, from perfect competition to monopoly, with oligopoly, a market with a few large firms, in between. In recent decades, competition policy has faced new questions from digital markets, where a few large platforms dominate, and from global markets, where firms compete across borders. Competition policy has also had to adapt to digital markets. A platform, such as a search engine or a social network, can become dominant because of network effects: the more people use it, the more valuable it becomes, which makes it hard for a rival to enter. The European Union's Digital Markets Act, which came into force in 2023, sets rules for the largest platforms, requiring them to let users choose their services and to share data with rivals, and the United Kingdom's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act of 2024 gives its competition authority similar powers. The debate is about whether the old tools, designed for railways and oil, work for digital markets, or whether new rules are needed.

Key concepts: Perfect competition is a market with many small firms selling identical products, where no firm can set the price. A monopoly is a market with a single seller. An oligopoly is a market with a few large sellers, who must consider each other's actions. A cartel is a group of firms that agree to fix prices or to limit output, which is illegal in most countries. Market power is the ability of a firm to charge a price above its costs. A natural monopoly is a market where one firm can supply the whole market more cheaply than two firms could, for example a water network, and it is usually regulated. A merger is the combination of two firms into one, and competition authorities can block a merger if it would reduce competition. A monopoly profit is the extra profit a monopolist earns by charging a higher price, and it is a transfer from consumers to the firm, not a gain for society. A deadweight loss is the loss of welfare caused by a monopoly charging more and selling less than the competitive level, because some trades that would benefit both buyer and seller do not happen. Contestability is the idea that a market can be competitive even with few firms, if new firms can enter easily, because the threat of entry disciplines the existing firms.

How it works: A competitive firm is a price taker: it sells at the market price, and if it charges more, it sells nothing, because buyers go to its rivals. It produces where its marginal cost, the cost of making one more unit, equals the market price, and it earns only a normal profit in the long run, because if profits were high, new firms would enter and drive prices down. A monopolist is a price maker: it faces the whole market demand, and it produces where its marginal revenue equals its marginal cost, which is less than the competitive output and at a higher price, so it earns extra profit and consumers lose out. An oligopolist must guess what its rivals will do, and the result can be competition, which benefits consumers, or collusion, which harms them, which is why competition authorities watch oligopolies closely. Competition also affects innovation. A monopolist has less incentive to innovate, because it faces no rival, but it has more profit to fund research, so the effect is ambiguous. A competitive market forces firms to innovate to survive, but it leaves them with less profit to invest. The evidence is mixed, and the relationship between competition and innovation is one of the most studied questions in economics, with the conclusion that some competition is good for innovation, but too much or too little can be harmful. The policy implication is that competition authorities should protect the process of competition, not any particular competitor.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A competitive market for bread has 100 bakeries, and the price settles at 1 pound a loaf, with each bakery making a small profit. If one bakery tries to charge 1.50 pounds, it sells nothing, because buyers go elsewhere. A monopolist bakery, the only one in town, can charge 2 pounds a loaf and sell fewer loaves, earning a larger profit but leaving buyers worse off. A second example is the oil cartel OPEC, which has at times limited output to raise the price of oil, and a third example is a natural monopoly: a water company that owns the pipes can supply a town more cheaply than two companies laying two sets of pipes, so it is regulated to keep its prices down. In each case the market structure decides the price and the quantity. A second worked example uses deadweight loss. Suppose a competitive market sells 100 units at 10 pounds each, for a total revenue of 1,000 pounds. A monopolist raises the price to 15 pounds and sells 70 units, for a revenue of 1,050 pounds, so its revenue rises, but 30 units that would have been sold at 10 pounds are not sold, and the buyers who would have bought them lose out. The deadweight loss is the value of those 30 lost trades, which is a loss to society, even though the monopolist gains. The calculation shows that a monopoly can be profitable for the firm while making society worse off.

Real applications: Competition policy is used by governments to keep markets working for consumers. Competition authorities, such as the Competition and Markets Authority in the United Kingdom and the Federal Trade Commission in the United States, can block mergers that would reduce competition, fine firms that fix prices, and order a monopoly to be broken up. The policy is also used to open markets to new entrants, for example by requiring a dominant firm to share its network with rivals. The study of competition is therefore practical as well as theoretical: it shapes the prices people pay for everything from bread to broadband. Competition policy is also used to protect consumers from unfair practices, such as misleading advertising, hidden charges, and the exploitation of vulnerable customers. The Competition and Markets Authority in the United Kingdom can investigate markets, order firms to change their behaviour, and fine them, and it has investigated markets from energy to banking to digital advertising. The study of competition is therefore not only about prices but also about fairness, about the rules of the market, and about who has power in the economy.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is competition always good? Competition usually benefits consumers, but it can also lead to a race to the bottom on wages or on safety, and it can drive firms out of business, which is why it is regulated. Is a monopoly always bad? A monopoly usually charges higher prices, but a monopoly that results from innovation, such as a patent, can reward the inventor, and a natural monopoly can be more efficient than competition, if it is regulated. Are big firms always monopolies? No, a firm can be large and still face competition, from other large firms or from new entrants, and size alone does not prove market power. Can competition be created? Yes, governments can open markets, remove barriers to entry, and require dominant firms to share their facilities, which is a central task of competition policy. Is a monopoly always a single firm? No, a monopoly can also be a group of firms that act together, a cartel, or a dominant firm with a large market share, and competition law applies to all of them. Does competition policy work? The evidence is that it lowers prices, raises quality, and increases innovation, though it is slow and expensive, and it can be hard to prove that a firm has abused its position. Should big tech firms be broken up? That is debated: some argue that breaking them up would restore competition, while others argue that their size reflects network effects and that regulation, rather than breakup, is the better answer.

Public Goods and Externalities

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000012 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A public good is a good that is non-excludable, meaning people cannot be stopped from using it, and non-rival, meaning one person's use does not reduce another's. Street lighting and national defence are classic examples: once the light is on, everyone can see by it, and one person using it does not stop another. An externality is a cost or benefit of an activity that falls on someone who is not part of the transaction: pollution is a negative externality, because a factory's smoke harms people who do not buy its products, and education is a positive externality, because an educated person benefits society as well as themselves. Markets fail to provide public goods and to price externalities correctly, which is why governments intervene. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: The idea of public goods was developed in the 20th century, notably by Paul Samuelson in 1954, who defined them and showed that markets would under-provide them, because people can enjoy them without paying, a problem called free-riding. The idea of externalities goes back to Alfred Marshall in 1890 and was developed by Arthur Pigou in 1920, who proposed taxes on polluting activities and subsidies for beneficial ones, now called Pigouvian taxes. In the 1960s, Ronald Coase argued that if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, the parties can solve an externality themselves, a result called the Coase theorem. In recent decades, externalities have become central to policy on climate change, where carbon emissions are the largest externality in history, and governments have used carbon taxes and emissions trading to price them. The tragedy of the commons is one of the most famous ideas in economics. It describes a common resource, such as a fishery or a pasture, that is open to all, where each person has an incentive to use as much as possible, because they gain the full benefit but share the cost with everyone else, so the resource is overused and eventually destroyed. The solution is to set rules: to limit the catch, to charge for use, or to give ownership, so that the user bears the full cost. Elinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel Prize in 2009, showed that communities can manage common resources themselves, without government or privatisation, if they have clear rules, monitoring, and sanctions, and her work is a central part of the field.

Key concepts: A public good is non-excludable and non-rival, such as national defence. A common resource is non-excludable but rival, such as a fishery, where one person's catch reduces the catch for others, and it can be overused, a problem called the tragedy of the commons. A private good is excludable and rival, such as a sandwich, which can be sold in a market. A negative externality is a cost imposed on others, such as pollution, while a positive externality is a benefit to others, such as a neighbour's beautiful garden. A Pigouvian tax is a tax set equal to the external cost, so the polluter pays for the harm they cause. A Pigouvian subsidy is a payment to encourage an activity with positive externalities, such as education or vaccination. A Coasean bargain is a private agreement between the parties to an externality, which can solve the problem if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap. A cap-and-trade system is a system in which the government sets a cap on total emissions and lets firms trade permits. A carbon offset is a payment to reduce emissions elsewhere, to compensate for emissions here, though the quality of offsets is debated. A green tax is a tax on pollution or on the use of natural resources, designed to change behaviour and to raise revenue.

How it works: A market fails to provide a public good because of free-riding: if everyone can enjoy street lighting without paying, no private firm can charge for it, so it will not be provided, or it will be provided too little. The government solves this by paying for the good from taxes and providing it to everyone. An externality is a market failure because the price does not reflect the full cost or benefit: a factory that pollutes does not pay for the harm, so it produces too much, and a person who is vaccinated protects others as well as themselves, so too few people are vaccinated if they pay the full cost. A tax on pollution raises the polluter's cost to the true social cost, so they produce less, and a subsidy for vaccination lowers the cost to the individual, so more people are vaccinated. The aim is to make the price reflect the full cost or benefit to society. Public goods are also provided by technology in new ways. Open-source software, such as the Linux operating system, is a public good provided by volunteers, and Wikipedia is a public good provided by contributors, both of which show that public goods can be provided without government, when people are motivated by reputation, by reciprocity, or by a shared purpose. The internet itself is partly a public good, and its governance, by organisations such as the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers, is a form of collective management. These examples show that the provision of public goods is not only a matter for governments, but also for communities and for new institutions.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A factory produces widgets and sells them for 10 pounds each, but each widget causes 3 pounds of pollution damage, so the true social cost is 13 pounds. The market produces too many widgets, because buyers pay only 10 pounds. A tax of 3 pounds per widget raises the price to 13 pounds, and the quantity produced falls to the efficient level. A second example is a lighthouse: it costs 100,000 pounds to build, and it benefits 1,000 ships, each of which would pay 200 pounds for the benefit, a total of 200,000 pounds, but no single ship will pay to build it, because it can use the light for free, so the government builds it. A third example is education: a year of schooling costs 5,000 pounds, but it benefits society by 7,000 pounds, through higher taxes and lower crime, so the government subsidises it by 2,000 pounds. A second worked example uses a fishery. Suppose a lake has 1,000 fish, and each fisher catches 100 fish a year. If there are 10 fishers, the total catch is 1,000 fish, which is sustainable if the fish reproduce at that rate. If an 11th fisher joins, the total catch rises to 1,100, which is more than the fish can replace, so the stock falls, and in the next year each fisher catches fewer fish. The 11th fisher gains, but the others lose, and the lake is overfished, which is the tragedy of the commons. A quota of 100 fish per fisher, or a licence that limits the number of fishers to 10, solves the problem by making the total catch sustainable.

Real applications: Public goods and externalities are the main justification for government intervention in the economy. Governments provide defence, law and order, and street lighting, they regulate pollution, they subsidise education and health care, and they manage common resources such as fisheries, to prevent overuse. The tools are taxes, subsidies, regulation, and the direct provision of goods, and the choice of tool depends on the problem. Climate policy is the largest application: carbon taxes, emissions trading, and subsidies for clean energy are all attempts to price the externality of carbon emissions, and they are the central economic policy challenge of the coming decades. Public goods and externalities are also central to the debate about climate change, which is the largest externality in history. The cost of carbon emissions falls on the whole world, while the benefit of emitting falls on the emitter, so no single country has an incentive to cut emissions alone, and international cooperation is needed. The Paris Agreement of 2015 is an attempt at that cooperation, with each country setting its own target, and the debate is about whether the targets are enough, and how to enforce them. The study of public goods and externalities is therefore the study of how to solve the hardest collective problems, from a local fishery to the global climate.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are public goods always provided by the government? Usually, but not always: charities, clubs, and communities provide some public goods, and new technology, such as encryption, can make some goods excludable. Is pollution the only externality? No, externalities are everywhere: a noisy neighbour, a vaccination, a beautiful garden, and a traffic jam are all externalities. Can externalities be solved without government? Sometimes, if property rights are clear and the parties can bargain, as Coase argued, but when many people are involved, bargaining is costly and government action is usually needed. Is a tax on pollution a punishment? No, it is a price: it makes the polluter pay for the harm they cause, so they have an incentive to pollute less, and the revenue can be used to cut other taxes or to help those affected. Are public goods always provided by the government? No, as open-source software and Wikipedia show, communities can provide them, though government provision is the most common for large-scale goods such as defence. Is the tragedy of the commons inevitable? No, Ostrom showed that communities can manage common resources with rules, and governments can manage them with quotas or with ownership, so the tragedy is a warning, not a fate. Can externalities be solved by markets? Sometimes, if property rights are clear, as Coase argued, but when many people are involved, markets alone are not enough, and government action is needed.

Fiscal Policy

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000013 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a fiscal-policy ladder from a single road to the whole budget, a worked multiplier example, and a design reading that treats the budget as the government's steering wheel.

Definition: Fiscal policy is the government's use of spending and taxation to influence the economy. When the government spends more or taxes less, demand rises and the economy grows faster; when it spends less or taxes more, demand falls and the economy slows. Fiscal policy is used to stabilise the economy in a downturn, to fund public services, and to redistribute income. The government's budget, the difference between its spending and its revenue, is the main instrument of fiscal policy, and a deficit, where spending exceeds revenue, must be financed by borrowing. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Fiscal policy became a central tool in the 1930s, when John Maynard Keynes argued in his General Theory of 1936 that government spending could restore demand in a depression. During the Second World War, governments spent heavily, and after the war they used fiscal policy to maintain full employment, in what is called the Keynesian era. In the 1970s, high inflation and slow growth led to a reaction against fiscal activism, and in the 1980s and 1990s governments focused on reducing deficits and debt. The financial crisis of 2008 brought fiscal policy back: governments spent heavily to rescue banks and to support demand, and the pandemic of 2020 brought the largest fiscal response in peacetime, with governments paying wages, supporting businesses, and funding health services. In the 2020s, the debate is about how to reduce the debt built up in those crises without slowing growth. Fiscal policy has also been used to redistribute income. Governments tax the rich more than the poor, through progressive income tax, where the rate rises with income, and they spend on benefits for the poor, on health, and on education, which reduces inequality. The Gini coefficient, which measures inequality from 0 to 1, is lower after taxes and benefits than before them in most countries, showing that fiscal policy reduces inequality. The debate is about how much redistribution is desirable, and about the trade-off between equality and efficiency, because high taxes can discourage work and investment, while low taxes can leave inequality high.

Key concepts: Government spending is the money the government spends on goods, services, and transfers, such as pensions and benefits. Taxation is the money the government collects, mainly from income tax, value added tax, and corporation tax. The budget deficit is the amount by which spending exceeds revenue in a year, and the budget surplus is the opposite. Government debt, or the national debt, is the total amount the government owes, built up from past deficits. An automatic stabiliser is a part of the budget that changes automatically with the economy, such as unemployment benefits, which rise in a downturn, and income tax revenue, which falls, both of which cushion the downturn without any new decision. A progressive tax is a tax where the rate rises with income, so the rich pay a higher share. A regressive tax is a tax where the rate falls with income, so the poor pay a higher share, such as a tax on tobacco, which the poor spend a larger share of their income on. A balanced budget is a budget where spending equals revenue, and a structural deficit is a deficit that remains even when the economy is at its normal level, which is a sign that the public finances are unsustainable. Fiscal sustainability is the ability of a government to keep its debt stable without raising taxes or cutting spending in the future. Austerity is a policy of reducing the deficit by cutting spending or raising taxes, often in a downturn, which is controversial because it can slow growth.

How it works: Fiscal policy works through demand. When the government spends 1 billion pounds on a road, the construction workers earn wages, they spend those wages in shops, the shops order more goods, and the initial spending multiplies through the economy, an effect called the multiplier. The size of the multiplier depends on how much of the extra income is spent rather than saved or taxed: if people spend 80 percent of each extra pound, the multiplier is 1 divided by 0.2, or 5, though in practice leakages through imports and taxes make it smaller, often estimated at 1 to 2. Tax cuts work the same way in reverse: they raise disposable income, which raises spending, though some of the cut is saved. Fiscal policy also works through supply: spending on education, infrastructure, and research raises the economy's capacity to grow in the long run. Fiscal policy also has a long-run dimension. Spending on education, on infrastructure, and on research raises the economy's capacity to grow, and it is called investment, even though it is counted as spending in the budget. Spending on pensions and on benefits, by contrast, is consumption, because it does not raise future capacity. The distinction matters for judging a deficit: a deficit that funds investment may be justified, because it raises future income, while a deficit that funds consumption may not be, because it leaves a debt without a corresponding asset. The debate about fiscal policy is therefore about both the size of the deficit and what it is spent on.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. The government spends 1 billion pounds on building schools, and the multiplier is 1.5, so total demand rises by 1.5 billion pounds. If the government cuts taxes by 1 billion pounds and people spend 80 percent of the cut, demand rises by 800 million pounds directly, and then by more as the money circulates. A second example is the budget: if the government spends 1,000 billion pounds and collects 900 billion in tax, the deficit is 100 billion pounds, which must be borrowed by selling bonds. A third example is debt: if the national debt is 2,000 billion pounds and GDP is 2,500 billion, the debt-to-GDP ratio is 80 percent, a common way of judging whether debt is high or low. A second worked example uses the Gini coefficient. Suppose the Gini coefficient before taxes and benefits is 0.50, and after them it is 0.35, a fall of 0.15, or 30 percent, which shows that fiscal policy reduces inequality substantially. In the United Kingdom, the Gini coefficient after taxes and benefits is about 0.35, while in the United States it is about 0.39, showing that the United Kingdom redistributes more. The numbers show that fiscal policy is one of the main tools for reducing inequality, alongside education and labour market policy.

Real applications: Fiscal policy is used to stabilise the economy, to provide public services, and to redistribute income. In a recession, governments increase spending or cut taxes to support demand, and in a boom they do the opposite to prevent overheating. The policy is also used to fund health, education, and defence, and to transfer income from the rich to the poor through taxes and benefits. The limits of fiscal policy are the debt it creates, which must be repaid or refinanced, and the time it takes to decide and to implement, which can mean that the spending arrives after the downturn has passed. The debate about fiscal policy is therefore about the balance between supporting the economy now and keeping the public finances sustainable in the future. Fiscal policy is also constrained by politics. Governments find it easier to raise spending and cut taxes than to do the opposite, because voters reward generosity and punish austerity, which is why deficits are more common than surpluses, and why debt tends to rise over time. The solution, adopted in many countries, is to set fiscal rules, such as a limit on the deficit or on debt, and to give an independent body, such as the Office for Budget Responsibility in the United Kingdom, the task of forecasting the public finances and judging whether the rules are met. The rules are not perfect, but they are an attempt to make fiscal policy sustainable over the long run.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is government debt the same as household debt? No, a government can borrow for longer, it can roll over its debt by issuing new bonds, and it earns revenue from taxes, so it does not need to repay its debt in the way a household does, though high debt can still be a problem if interest rates rise. Does government spending always help the economy? Not always: if the economy is already at full capacity, extra spending can cause inflation, and if the spending is wasteful, it can reduce growth. Should the budget always balance? Most economists say no: a deficit in a downturn can be justified, and a surplus in a boom can be wise, but a persistent deficit can lead to rising debt and higher interest payments. Can fiscal policy work quickly? It can be slow, because decisions take time and projects take time to start, which is why automatic stabilisers, which work immediately, are valuable. Is a budget deficit always bad? No, a deficit in a downturn can support the economy, and a deficit that funds investment can raise future growth, but a persistent deficit can lead to rising debt and higher interest payments. Should the government pay off its debt? A government does not need to repay its debt in full, because it can roll it over by issuing new bonds, but it does need to keep it stable as a share of GDP, so that interest payments do not crowd out other spending. Is austerity necessary? That is debated: some argue that reducing the deficit quickly restores confidence, while others argue that it slows growth and makes the debt harder to repay, and the evidence from the 2010s is mixed.

Monetary Policy

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000014 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a monetary-policy ladder from the base rate to the high street, a worked mortgage example, and a design reading that treats the interest rate as the central bank's steering wheel.

Definition: Monetary policy is the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence the economy, mainly to keep inflation low and stable. When the central bank raises interest rates, borrowing becomes more expensive, spending falls, and inflation cools; when it lowers rates, borrowing becomes cheaper, spending rises, and the economy grows. Most central banks have an inflation target, usually 2 percent a year, and they set policy to meet it. Monetary policy is the main tool for stabilising the economy in most advanced countries. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Monetary policy became the main tool of stabilisation in the 1980s and 1990s, when central banks were given independence from governments and targets for inflation. The Bank of England was given operational independence in 1997, with a target of 2 percent inflation, and the European Central Bank, founded in 1998, has a target of 2 percent for the euro area. The Federal Reserve in the United States has a dual mandate, to keep prices stable and to maximise employment. After the financial crisis of 2008, central banks cut rates to near zero and used quantitative easing, buying bonds to lower long-term rates, and in 2021 and 2022 they raised rates sharply to fight the return of inflation, in the fastest tightening in decades. Monetary policy has also been studied in terms of its distributional effects. A rise in interest rates hurts borrowers, especially those with mortgages, and helps savers, especially those with deposits, so it redistributes income from borrowers to savers. It also affects house prices, share prices, and the exchange rate, so it affects the wealth of different groups differently. The distributional effects are not the central bank's mandate, which is to control inflation, but they are a consequence of its actions, and they are part of the public debate about monetary policy, especially when rates change sharply, as they did in 2022 and 2023.

Key concepts: The policy rate, or base rate, is the interest rate set by the central bank, which affects the rates banks charge each other and their customers. Quantitative easing is the purchase of bonds by a central bank, which lowers long-term interest rates and increases the money supply. Forward guidance is the central bank's communication about its future policy, used to influence expectations. The transmission mechanism is the chain by which a change in the policy rate affects spending, through mortgages, business loans, the exchange rate, and asset prices. The inflation target is the rate of inflation the central bank aims for, usually 2 percent a year. The Taylor rule is a rule for setting the policy rate, proposed by John Taylor in 1993, which says the rate should rise when inflation is above target and when output is above its normal level, and fall when they are below. The neutral rate is the rate that neither stimulates nor restrains the economy, and it is estimated to have fallen in recent decades, because of slower growth and higher saving. The zero lower bound is the limit on how far rates can fall, because people can hold cash instead of deposits that pay negative rates, though some central banks have set negative rates. Unconventional monetary policy is policy other than changing the base rate, such as quantitative easing and forward guidance, used when the base rate is at its lower bound.

How it works: Monetary policy works through the cost of borrowing and the value of assets. When the central bank raises its rate, banks raise the rates they charge on mortgages and loans, so households with mortgages pay more and have less to spend, and firms borrow less to invest, so demand falls and inflation cools. Higher rates also attract foreign savers, so the currency rises, which makes imports cheaper and reduces inflation further. When the central bank lowers its rate, the opposite happens: borrowing is cheaper, spending rises, and the economy grows. The effect takes time, usually 12 to 18 months to reach inflation fully, so central banks must look ahead and act on forecasts, not only on today's data. Monetary policy also interacts with fiscal policy. When the government runs a large deficit, it borrows more, which can raise interest rates, a phenomenon called crowding out, though the central bank can offset it by buying bonds. When the central bank buys government bonds, it is said to be monetising the debt, though central banks insist that they buy bonds to control inflation, not to fund the government, and that they will sell them when the time comes. The relationship between the central bank and the government is therefore a matter of coordination and of independence, and it is governed by law and by convention.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. The Bank of England raises its base rate from 4 percent to 5 percent. A household with a mortgage of 200,000 pounds on a variable rate pays about 2,000 pounds more in interest a year, because 1 percent of 200,000 is 2,000, so it has less to spend on other things. A firm considering a loan of 1 million pounds to build a factory faces an extra 10,000 pounds a year in interest, and it may decide not to build. A second example is quantitative easing: the Bank of England bought 895 billion pounds of bonds after 2009, which lowered long-term rates and supported the economy when the base rate could fall no further. A third example is the exchange rate: higher rates in the United Kingdom attract buyers of pounds, so the pound rises, and imported goods become cheaper. A second worked example uses the Taylor rule. Suppose inflation is 4 percent, 2 points above the 2 percent target, and output is at its normal level. The Taylor rule says the policy rate should be the neutral rate, say 2 percent, plus 1.5 times the inflation gap, which is 3 points, for a total of 5 percent. If inflation falls to 2 percent, the rate should fall to 2 percent, the neutral rate. The rule is a guide, not a mechanical formula, and central banks use judgement as well as rules, but it shows the logic: rates rise when inflation is high and fall when it is low.

Real applications: Monetary policy is used to keep inflation stable, which is the foundation for growth, because stable prices let people and firms plan for the future. It is also used to support the economy in a downturn, by cutting rates, and to cool it in a boom, by raising them. The policy is set by a committee, such as the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee, which meets regularly and votes on the rate, and its decisions are explained in public, so that people can understand the reasoning and plan accordingly. The independence of central banks from governments is intended to prevent politicians from cutting rates before elections for short-term gain, at the cost of higher inflation later. Monetary policy is also conducted in a global context. Capital flows freely between countries, so a change in one country's interest rate affects others, through the exchange rate and through capital flows. A rise in US rates attracts capital from around the world, which raises the dollar and puts pressure on other countries to raise their rates, even if their own economies do not need it. Central banks therefore watch each other's decisions, and they coordinate in crises, as they did in 2008, when they cut rates together, and in 2020, when they provided liquidity to financial markets. Monetary policy is a national tool with global effects.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is monetary policy the same as fiscal policy? No, monetary policy is set by the central bank through interest rates, while fiscal policy is set by the government through spending and taxation, and the two work together, sometimes in the same direction and sometimes in opposite directions. Can central banks control inflation exactly? No, they influence it with a lag, and they are affected by shocks, such as oil price rises, that they cannot control, so they aim for the target over the medium term, not every month. What happens when rates cannot fall further? Central banks use quantitative easing and forward guidance, and governments use fiscal policy, because monetary policy alone may not be enough. Are low interest rates always good? No, very low rates can encourage excessive borrowing and asset bubbles, and they reduce the return on savings, so they have costs as well as benefits. Is the central bank's job only to control inflation? In most countries yes, though the Federal Reserve also has a mandate for employment, and some central banks have mandates for financial stability and for climate change. Can monetary policy solve all economic problems? No, it can stabilise demand and control inflation, but it cannot raise productivity, reduce inequality, or solve supply problems, which need other policies. Should central banks be more transparent? Most central banks now publish their decisions, their minutes, and their forecasts, and the trend is toward more transparency, because it helps the public understand policy and plan accordingly.

Central Banks

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000015 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a central-bank ladder from a note issue to a policy committee, a worked voting example, and a design reading that treats independence and accountability as a deliberate balance.

Definition: A central bank is the institution that issues a country's currency, sets its main interest rate, and oversees its banking system. Examples are the Bank of England, founded in 1694, the Federal Reserve in the United States, founded in 1913, and the European Central Bank, founded in 1998. Central banks are usually independent of the government in their day-to-day decisions, so that monetary policy is not used for short-term political gain, and they are accountable to the public through published decisions and reports. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: The first central banks were founded to lend to governments and to issue notes: the Bank of England was founded in 1694 to lend to the government, and the Bank of France was founded in 1800. In the 19th century, central banks became lenders of last resort, lending to banks in a crisis to prevent a panic, a role described by Walter Bagehot in 1873. The Federal Reserve was founded in 1913 after a series of banking panics in the United States. After the Second World War, central banks were often controlled by governments, but in the 1980s and 1990s they were given independence, starting with the Reserve Bank of New Zealand in 1989 and the Bank of England in 1997. The financial crisis of 2008 and the pandemic of 2020 expanded their roles, to include buying bonds, supporting financial stability, and, in some cases, considering climate change. Central banks have also become more involved in financial stability. After the financial crisis of 2008, they were given new powers to regulate banks, to set capital requirements, and to intervene in a crisis, a role called macroprudential policy. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee, created in 2011, can set rules for mortgage lending, for bank capital, and for the financial system as a whole, to prevent a build-up of risk. The European Central Bank supervises the largest banks in the euro area, and the Federal Reserve supervises banks in the United States. The aim is to prevent a crisis, rather than only to respond to one, and it is a major expansion of the central bank's role.

Key concepts: Lender of last resort is the central bank's role of lending to banks in a crisis, to prevent a bank run from bringing down the financial system. Financial stability is the condition in which the financial system works smoothly, without crises, and central banks now have a mandate to protect it. A bank run is a panic in which many depositors withdraw their money at once, which can bring down even a healthy bank. Reserves are the deposits banks hold at the central bank, which the central bank can increase or decrease. A currency is the money of a country, and the central bank issues it and defends its value. A bank is an institution that takes deposits and makes loans, and it is vulnerable to a run, because it lends for the long term and borrows for the short term. Capital is the money a bank's owners have put in, which absorbs losses, and the capital requirement is the minimum capital a bank must hold, set by the regulator. A stress test is a test of whether a bank could survive a severe downturn, conducted by the central bank, and banks that fail must raise more capital. A bailout is the rescue of a bank by the government, using public money, which is controversial because it rewards risk-taking, a problem called moral hazard.

How it works: A central bank works by setting the price of money and by being the bank for the banks. It sets the policy rate, which is the rate it charges banks to borrow, and that rate ripples through the economy to mortgages, loans, and savings. It buys and sells bonds, which affects long-term rates and the money supply. It supervises banks, setting rules for how much capital they must hold, and it can lend to a bank in trouble, to stop a panic spreading. It also holds the country's foreign exchange reserves and can intervene in currency markets, though most advanced central banks rarely do so. Its decisions are made by a committee, published with minutes, and explained in reports, so that the public can hold it to account. Central banks also act as the government's bank. They hold the government's accounts, they issue government bonds, and they manage the government's cash. They also hold the country's foreign exchange reserves, which are used to intervene in currency markets if needed, though most advanced central banks rarely intervene, because they let their currencies float. The central bank's balance sheet, which shows its assets and liabilities, grew enormously after 2008, as it bought bonds through quantitative easing, and it is now a major part of the financial system, holding trillions in assets.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee has nine members, and it meets eight times a year to set the base rate, voting by majority. If five members vote to raise the rate and four vote to hold it, the rate rises. A second example is the Federal Reserve, which has a dual mandate to keep prices stable and to maximise employment, and its Federal Open Market Committee meets eight times a year. A third example is the European Central Bank, which sets policy for the 20 countries of the euro area, with a Governing Council of the governors of the national central banks and the members of its Executive Board. The Bank of England was founded in 1694, so it has been operating for more than 330 years. A second worked example uses capital requirements. Suppose a bank has assets of 100 billion pounds and capital of 10 billion, a capital ratio of 10 percent. If it loses 5 billion on bad loans, its capital falls to 5 billion, a ratio of 5 percent, which is still positive, so it survives. If it loses 12 billion, its capital falls below zero, and it fails, unless the government rescues it. The capital requirement is designed to ensure that a bank can absorb losses without failing, and the requirement was raised after 2008, from about 2 percent to about 10 percent or more for large banks, to make the system safer.

Real applications: Central banks are used to keep prices stable, to keep the financial system safe, and to support the economy. Their independence is intended to prevent short-term political pressure, and their accountability is intended to ensure they serve the public. The study of central banks is therefore the study of how a society delegates one of its most important powers, the power to create money, to an institution that is both independent and accountable, and how that institution uses that power in the interests of the whole economy. Central banks are also studying the future of money. The rise of digital payments, of cryptocurrencies, and of stablecoins, which are cryptocurrencies pegged to a currency, has led central banks to study digital currencies of their own, called central bank digital currencies, or CBDCs. The Bank of England, the European Central Bank, and the Federal Reserve are all studying CBDCs, and China has already launched a digital yuan. The debate is about whether a CBDC would improve payments, whether it would threaten banks, and whether it would give the central bank too much information about people's spending.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are central banks part of the government? They are public institutions, but in most advanced economies they are independent in their monetary policy decisions, though the government sets the target and appoints the leadership. Do central banks print money? They issue notes and coins, and they create reserves electronically, but most money in the economy is created by commercial banks when they lend, not by the central bank. Can a central bank run out of money? In its own currency, no, because it can create reserves, but creating too much money causes inflation, so the limit is economic, not physical. Are central banks democratic? They are accountable through published decisions, through reports to parliament, and through the appointment of their leaders, but their independence is a deliberate limit on direct democratic control, to prevent short-term politics from causing inflation. Are central banks too powerful? They have great power over interest rates and over the financial system, and their independence is a deliberate limit on democratic control, justified by the need to prevent short-term politics from causing inflation. The counter-argument is that they should be more accountable, and that their mandates should be set by elected governments, which is the case in most countries: the government sets the target, and the central bank chooses how to meet it. Can a central bank fail? A central bank cannot go bankrupt in its own currency, because it can create reserves, but it can lose credibility if it fails to control inflation, and credibility, once lost, is hard to regain.

Economic Growth

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000016 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a growth ladder from a single firm to a whole economy, a worked doubling-time table, and a design reading that treats productivity as the engine of long-run growth.

Definition: Economic growth is the increase in the production of goods and services in an economy over time, usually measured as the growth rate of real GDP. It matters because it raises living standards, it funds public services, and it creates jobs. Growth comes from more workers, more capital, such as machines and buildings, and more productivity, which is producing more with the same inputs, through better technology, better skills, and better organisation. In the long run, productivity is the most important source of growth, because the number of workers and the amount of capital cannot grow forever. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Modern economic growth began with the Industrial Revolution in the late 18th century, when new machines, such as the steam engine, and new ways of organising work raised output per person for the first time in history. Before that, living standards had changed little for centuries, and growth was limited by the land. Adam Smith in 1776 explained growth through specialisation and trade, and in the 19th century economists studied the role of capital and of technology. In the 1950s, Robert Solow developed the growth model that bears his name, showing that technology, not capital alone, drives long-run growth, work that won him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1987. In the 1980s and 1990s, economists developed endogenous growth theory, which explains how investment in education, research, and ideas creates growth from within the economy. In recent decades, growth has been studied alongside its limits, including the environment and inequality. Growth has also been studied in terms of its sources. The Solow model, developed by Robert Solow in the 1950s, shows that growth comes from more capital, more labour, and more technology, and that in the long run only technology matters, because the returns to capital diminish. Endogenous growth theory, developed in the 1980s by Paul Romer and others, shows that technology is not a gift from outside the economy but is produced by investment in research, in education, and in ideas, and that ideas are non-rival, meaning one person's use does not stop another's, so they can drive growth forever. Romer won the Nobel Prize in 2018 for this work, and it is now the standard account of long-run growth.

Key concepts: Real GDP growth is the percentage change in GDP adjusted for inflation, the standard measure of growth. Productivity is output per worker, or output per hour worked, and it is the key to long-run growth. Capital is the stock of machines, buildings, and equipment used to produce, and investment is the spending that adds to it. Human capital is the skills and knowledge of workers, built by education and training. Total factor productivity is the part of growth that cannot be explained by more labour or more capital, and it is attributed to technology and to better organisation. A steady state is a situation where the economy grows at a constant rate, with capital per worker unchanged, because investment equals depreciation. Convergence is the idea that poor countries grow faster than rich ones, because they can copy technology and because the returns to capital are higher where capital is scarce, so incomes converge over time. Conditional convergence is the idea that countries converge only if they have similar institutions and policies, so a poor country with bad institutions may not catch up. Creative destruction is Joseph Schumpeter's term for the process by which new firms and technologies replace old ones, which drives growth but also causes job losses.

How it works: Growth works through the accumulation of inputs and through improvements in how they are used. A firm that buys a new machine can produce more with the same workers, and a worker who learns a new skill can produce more in the same time. An economy that invests a larger share of its output in machines, in infrastructure, and in education will grow faster, though the returns to capital diminish: the first machine helps a lot, and the hundredth helps less. Technology raises the whole economy's capacity: a new way of making steel, a new drug, or a new piece of software lets the same workers and machines produce more. Growth also needs institutions: property rights, the rule of law, and open markets, without which investment and innovation are discouraged. Growth also depends on institutions. The economists Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, who won the Nobel Prize in 2024, argued that inclusive institutions, which protect property rights and allow broad participation, lead to growth, while extractive institutions, which benefit a small elite, lead to stagnation. Their work, based on the history of colonisation, shows that the same country can follow very different paths depending on its institutions, and that institutions are the deep cause of growth, deeper than geography or culture. The policy implication is that reforming institutions is the most important task for growth, though it is also the hardest.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. An economy grows at 2 percent a year, so its GDP doubles in about 35 years, because 70 divided by 2 is 35, a rule called the rule of 70. If it grows at 3 percent, it doubles in about 23 years, and if it grows at 1 percent, it doubles in about 70 years, so a small difference in the growth rate makes a large difference over a lifetime. A second example is productivity: if output per hour rises by 2 percent a year, a worker produces about 22 percent more after ten years, because 1.02 to the power of 10 is about 1.22. A third example is China, which grew at about 10 percent a year for three decades, doubling its economy about every seven years, and lifting hundreds of millions of people out of poverty, the fastest growth in history. A second worked example uses convergence. Suppose a rich country has GDP per capita of 40,000 dollars and grows at 2 percent a year, while a poor country has GDP per capita of 4,000 dollars and grows at 6 percent a year. The poor country grows faster by 4 points a year, so the ratio of incomes, initially 10 to 1, falls by about 4 percent a year, and it will take about 58 years for the poor country to catch up, because the logarithm of 10 divided by 0.04 is about 58. The calculation shows that convergence is slow, even when the poor country grows much faster, which is why the gap between rich and poor countries persists for generations.

Real applications: Economic growth is used to raise living standards, to fund health and education, and to reduce poverty. It is the reason that people today live longer, healthier, and richer lives than their ancestors, and it is the goal of economic policy in every country. The study of growth is also the study of its limits: growth that damages the environment, that widens inequality, or that depends on borrowing may not be sustainable, and economists now study green growth, which aims to raise living standards without damaging the planet, and inclusive growth, which aims to share the gains more widely. Growth is also measured in terms of wellbeing. The Human Development Index combines income with health and education, and it shows that some countries with modest incomes have high wellbeing, because they invest in health and education, while others with high incomes have lower wellbeing, because they do not. The study of growth is therefore not only about GDP but also about what growth is for, and the best measure of growth is one that captures the improvement in people's lives, not only the increase in output.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is growth the same as development? No, growth is an increase in output, while development is a broader improvement in wellbeing, including health, education, and freedom, and growth does not always bring development. Can an economy grow forever? In terms of GDP, it can grow as long as productivity improves, but growth that uses finite resources and damages the environment faces limits, which is why sustainable growth is a central concern. Does growth make people happier? Up to a point, higher income raises wellbeing, but beyond a certain level the link weakens, and other factors, such as health, relationships, and security, matter more. Is slow growth a problem? Slow growth makes it harder to fund public services and to raise living standards, and it can lead to political discontent, which is why governments aim for steady, sustainable growth. Is growth always good for the environment? No, growth has historically increased emissions and resource use, but the link is not fixed: countries that invest in clean technology can grow while cutting emissions, as the United Kingdom has done since 1990. Does growth require inequality? No, some countries have grown with low inequality, such as the Nordic countries, while others have grown with high inequality, and the evidence is that moderate inequality can encourage effort, but high inequality can harm growth by limiting opportunity. Can a country grow without technology? It can grow by accumulating capital and labour, but the returns diminish, so long-run growth needs technology, which is why investment in research and education is essential.

Development Economics

Definition: Development economics is the study of how poor countries become richer, healthier, and better educated, and of the policies that help that process. It studies poverty, inequality, health, education, agriculture, and institutions, and it asks why some countries grow fast while others stay poor. Development is broader than growth: it includes the wellbeing of people, not only the size of the economy, and it is measured by indicators such as life expectancy, literacy, and income per person. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Development economics began as a subject after the Second World War, when many countries became independent and sought to raise living standards. In the 1950s and 1960s, economists focused on industrialisation and on aid, and the World Bank, founded in 1944, lent to poor countries for infrastructure. In the 1970s, the focus widened to health and education, and Amartya Sen argued that development should be judged by people's freedoms and capabilities, not only by income, work that won him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998. In the 1990s, the United Nations Development Programme began publishing the Human Development Index, which combines income, health, and education. In the 2000s, randomised trials, led by Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo, and Michael Kremer, who won the Nobel Prize in 2019, tested which policies work, from deworming to microfinance, and in 2015 the United Nations adopted the Sustainable Development Goals, 17 goals to end poverty and protect the planet by 2030. Development economics has also studied the role of women. The economist Esther Duflo, who won the Nobel Prize in 2019, showed that investing in women, through education, through microfinance, and through health, has larger effects on development than investing in men, because women spend more of their income on their children, on health, and on education. The World Bank's research shows that closing the gender gap in employment could raise GDP in some countries by more than 20 percent, and that girls' education is one of the most effective investments in development. The focus on women is therefore not only a matter of fairness but also of economics, because it raises growth and reduces poverty.

Key concepts: Poverty is usually measured as living on less than a threshold income, such as 2.15 dollars a day, the World Bank's extreme poverty line. The Human Development Index combines life expectancy, education, and income into a single number. Inequality is the uneven distribution of income or wealth, often measured by the Gini coefficient, which runs from 0, perfect equality, to 1, perfect inequality. Microfinance is the provision of small loans to poor people, especially women, to start businesses. Foreign aid is money and resources given by rich countries to poor countries, to support development. A randomised controlled trial is an experiment in which people are randomly assigned to a treatment or a control group, so that the effect of the treatment can be measured without bias. A poverty trap is a situation where poverty itself prevents escape from poverty, for example because a poor person cannot afford the food to work productively, or the school fees to educate their children. A middle-income trap is a situation where a country grows to middle-income level and then stalls, because it cannot compete with low-wage countries in cheap goods or with rich countries in high-tech goods. The demographic transition is the change from high birth and death rates to low ones, which raises the share of working-age people and can boost growth, a phenomenon called the demographic dividend.

How it works: Development works through several channels. Better health and education raise productivity, so workers earn more and the economy grows. Better institutions, such as secure property rights and honest courts, encourage investment, because people will invest if they believe their property is safe. Infrastructure, such as roads, electricity, and clean water, lets firms operate and lets people reach markets and schools. Trade lets poor countries sell their goods abroad and earn the foreign exchange to buy what they need. Aid can help, but it works best when it supports good policies and is not captured by corruption. The evidence from randomised trials shows that some interventions, such as deworming children and providing bed nets against malaria, are highly effective, while others, such as some microfinance programmes, have smaller effects than hoped. Development also depends on agriculture. In poor countries, most people work in farming, and raising farm productivity, through better seeds, through irrigation, and through access to markets, is the first step to development, because it raises incomes, it frees labour for industry, and it lowers food prices. The Green Revolution of the 1960s and 1970s, which introduced high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, doubled food production in Asia and prevented famine, though it also had environmental costs, and it is a model for how technology can transform a poor economy.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. Extreme poverty, defined as living on less than 2.15 dollars a day, affected about 700 million people in 2023, or about 9 percent of the world's population, down from about 36 percent in 1990, a fall of more than a billion people, mostly in China. A second example is life expectancy: in the poorest countries it is about 60 years, while in the richest it is over 80, a gap of more than 20 years, which reflects differences in health, nutrition, and sanitation. A third example is the Human Development Index: Norway scores about 0.96, while Niger scores about 0.40, a gap that reflects differences in income, in schooling, and in health. These numbers show both the progress that has been made and the distance that remains. A second worked example uses the demographic dividend. Suppose a country has 100 people, of whom 50 are of working age and 50 are children or elderly, a dependency ratio of 1. If the birth rate falls, the number of children falls to 30, and the working-age population rises to 60, the dependency ratio falls to 0.67, so each worker supports fewer dependants, and the economy can save and invest more. The dividend lasts for a generation, until the population ages, and it is a one-time boost to growth, which is why countries that use it well, such as South Korea, have grown fast, while those that do not have missed the opportunity.

Real applications: Development economics is used to design policies that reduce poverty and improve lives. It guides the work of the World Bank, the United Nations, and aid agencies, and it informs the decisions of governments in poor countries about where to spend their limited resources. The field has become more evidence-based, with randomised trials testing what works, and more focused on the people affected, through the capabilities approach of Amartya Sen. The Sustainable Development Goals, adopted by all United Nations member states in 2015, are the current framework for development, covering poverty, health, education, gender equality, and the environment. Development economics is also the study of failure. Many development projects have failed, because they ignored local knowledge, because they were captured by elites, or because they were designed in the capital without understanding the village. The field has learned from these failures, by listening to the people affected, by testing interventions, and by admitting what does not work. The best development economics is humble, evidence-based, and focused on the people it serves, rather than on the theories of the economist.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is aid effective? The evidence is mixed: aid has helped to fund health programmes, such as vaccination, and infrastructure, but it has sometimes been wasted or captured by corrupt governments, and it cannot substitute for good policies in the recipient country. Does growth reduce poverty? Usually yes, but not always: growth that benefits only the rich can leave the poor behind, so the pattern of growth matters as much as its rate. Is development the same as Westernisation? No, development is about improving wellbeing, and different countries have followed different paths, including China's state-led growth and India's democratic development. Can poverty be ended? The Sustainable Development Goals aim to end extreme poverty by 2030, and the fall from 36 percent to 9 percent since 1990 shows it is possible, though progress has slowed in recent years. Is development the same as modernisation? No, development is about improving wellbeing, and different countries have modernised in different ways, including China's state-led model and India's democratic model, and there is no single path. Does aid work? The evidence is mixed, but the best evidence is that aid works when it supports good policies, when it is targeted at health and education, and when it is accountable to the people it serves. Can poor countries catch up? Yes, many have, including South Korea, which was as poor as Ghana in 1960 and is now rich, and China, which has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty, though the path is not easy and not every country has succeeded.

Behavioural Economics

ID: JAH-ECONOS-000018 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a bias ladder from a single shortcut to a whole policy, a worked default-effect example, and a design reading that treats choice architecture as a tool for better decisions.

Definition: Behavioural economics is the study of how people actually make economic decisions, rather than how a perfectly rational person would make them. It finds that people use shortcuts, that they are influenced by how choices are presented, that they care about fairness, and that they often act against their own long-term interests, for example by procrastinating or by overeating. Behavioural economics combines psychology and economics, and it is used to design policies, such as automatic enrolment in pension schemes, that help people make better choices without forcing them. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Behavioural economics began in the 1970s, when psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman showed that people systematically violate the rules of rational choice, for example by fearing losses more than they value equivalent gains, a finding called loss aversion. Richard Thaler, who won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017, developed the field, showing that people use mental accounts, that they are influenced by defaults, and that they can be nudged toward better choices. In 2008, Thaler and Cass Sunstein published Nudge, which argued that governments can improve decisions by changing the choice architecture, the way choices are presented, without restricting freedom. In 2010, the United Kingdom set up the Behavioural Insights Team, known as the Nudge Unit, to apply these ideas to policy, and other governments followed. Kahneman won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002 for his work on judgement and decision-making. Behavioural economics has also been applied to finance. The economist Robert Shiller, who won the Nobel Prize in 2013, showed that share prices move far more than can be explained by changes in fundamentals, such as dividends, and that the excess movement reflects the psychology of investors, including herding, overconfidence, and the fear of missing out. The dot-com bubble of the late 1990s and the housing bubble of the 2000s are examples of markets driven by psychology, and the crashes that followed show the cost of ignoring it. Behavioural finance is now a major field, and it is used by regulators to understand bubbles and by investors to avoid them.

Key concepts: A heuristic is a mental shortcut, such as judging a risk by how easily examples come to mind, which is useful but can lead to errors. Loss aversion is the finding that a loss feels about twice as painful as an equivalent gain feels good. A nudge is a change in the choice architecture that steers people toward a better choice without forbidding any option, such as making pension enrolment the default. Anchoring is the influence of an initial number on a later judgement, so a high initial price makes a later price seem low. Present bias is the tendency to value the present more than the future, which leads to procrastination. A framing effect is the influence of how a choice is presented on the decision, so the same option can be chosen or rejected depending on whether it is described as a gain or a loss. Mental accounting is the practice of treating money differently depending on its source or its intended use, such as spending a tax refund more freely than salary. The endowment effect is the tendency to value something more once it is owned, so people demand more to give up an object than they would pay to acquire it. Hyperbolic discounting is the tendency to value the present far more than the future, in a way that is not consistent over time, which leads to procrastination and to regret.

How it works: Behavioural economics works by testing how people actually behave, in experiments and in the field, and by using the findings to design better choices. A classic experiment shows loss aversion: people are offered a gamble in which they can win 100 pounds or lose 100 pounds with equal probability, and most refuse, even though the expected value is zero, because the loss feels worse than the gain feels good. A policy application is pension enrolment: when enrolment is automatic, with an option to opt out, participation rises dramatically, because most people stay with the default, while when people must opt in, participation is much lower. The same logic is used in organ donor registration, where countries with opt-out systems have far higher donor-registration rates, and in tax collection, where letters that say most people pay on time increase payment rates. Behavioural economics also studies fairness. In the ultimatum game, one person proposes a split of money and the other can accept it or reject it, in which case neither gets anything. A rational person would accept any positive amount, but in experiments people reject offers below about 20 percent, because they consider them unfair, even though rejecting costs them money. The result shows that people care about fairness, not only about their own payoff, and that fairness affects markets, wages, and prices, because firms that are seen as unfair lose customers and workers.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A pension scheme with opt-in enrolment has a participation rate of about 40 percent, while the same scheme with opt-out enrolment, where people are enrolled automatically and can leave if they wish, has a participation rate of about 90 percent, a difference of 50 percentage points from a change in the default alone. A second example is loss aversion: in experiments, people demand about twice as much to give up an object as they would pay to acquire it, a ratio of about 2 to 1. A third example is anchoring: in one experiment, people who were first shown a high number estimated the price of a house higher than those shown a low number, even though the number was random. These are small effects in a single decision, but they add up across millions of people. A second worked example uses the ultimatum game. Suppose the total is 100 pounds. The proposer offers 10 pounds and keeps 90. A rational responder would accept, because 10 is better than 0, but in experiments most responders reject offers below 20 pounds, so both get nothing. The proposer, anticipating this, offers about 40 pounds, and the responder accepts, so the split is closer to equal than the simple model predicts. The experiment shows that fairness is a real constraint on behaviour, and that a model that ignores it will predict badly.

Real applications: Behavioural economics is used in policy, in business, and in personal finance. Governments use nudges to increase pension saving, to improve tax collection, to encourage healthier eating, and to reduce energy use, often at low cost. Businesses use it to design products and prices, for example by setting a high anchor price and then offering a discount. The field is also used to understand financial markets, where herding, overconfidence, and loss aversion can drive bubbles and crashes. The study of behavioural economics is therefore the study of how to design choices that work with human nature, rather than against it. Behavioural economics is also used to design better financial products. A pension that enrols people automatically, a savings account that rounds up purchases and saves the difference, and a credit card that warns the user when they are close to their limit are all nudges, designed to help people save more and borrow less. The field is also used to understand why people do not save enough for retirement, why they borrow at high rates, and why they hold on to losing investments, and to design policies that help them do better, without forcing them to do so.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does behavioural economics say people are irrational? No, it says people are predictably irrational in specific ways, and that understanding those patterns lets us design better choices. Is a nudge manipulation? A nudge changes the presentation of a choice, but it does not forbid any option, and its defenders say it preserves freedom while helping people, though critics argue that it can be used to steer people in ways they would not endorse. Can behavioural economics replace traditional economics? No, it complements it: the traditional model is a useful simplification, and behavioural findings show where it needs to be corrected. Is behavioural economics only about psychology? No, it uses experiments, field data, and theory, and it is a branch of economics that takes psychology seriously. Is behavioural economics a fad? No, it has won Nobel Prizes, it is taught in every major university, and it is used by governments and by firms around the world, and its findings have been replicated in many countries and in many settings. Does it mean people are stupid? No, it means people use shortcuts that usually work well, but that can lead to errors in specific situations, and that understanding those shortcuts lets us design better choices. Can nudges solve big problems? Nudges are useful, but they are not a substitute for regulation, for taxation, or for investment, and the best policy combines nudges with stronger tools, using each where it works best.

Game Theory in Economics

Definition: Game theory is the study of strategic interaction, where the best choice for one person depends on what others do. It is used in economics to understand competition between firms, negotiations between countries, and any situation where people must anticipate each other's moves. The central idea is the Nash equilibrium, named after John Nash, where each person's choice is the best response to the others' choices, so no one wants to change alone. Game theory explains why rivals can end up in a bad outcome even when cooperation would be better for all, and how rules and reputation can help them cooperate. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Game theory began with John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern, who published Theory of Games and Economic Behavior in 1944, and it was developed by John Nash in the 1950s, who proved that every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium, work that won him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1994. In the 1960s and 1970s, game theory was applied to oligopoly, to bargaining, and to auctions, and in the 1990s it was used to design the auctions for mobile phone spectrum, which raised billions for governments. In 2005, Robert Aumann and Thomas Schelling won the Nobel Prize for game theory, and in 2012 Alvin Roth and Lloyd Shapley won it for matching theory, which uses game theory to match students to schools and doctors to hospitals. Game theory is now a core part of economics, taught to every student, and used in business, in politics, and in computer science. Game theory has also been applied to biology. The biologist John Maynard Smith used game theory to study animal behaviour, showing that aggression, cooperation, and mating strategies can be understood as equilibria, where each animal's strategy is the best response to the others'. The hawk-dove game, in which animals can fight or share, explains why most animals do not fight to the death, because the cost of fighting is high, and it shows that a mix of hawks and doves can be stable. The application of game theory to biology, called evolutionary game theory, is a major field, and it shows that the same logic applies to genes, to animals, and to people.

Key concepts: A game is a situation with players, strategies, and payoffs, where each player's payoff depends on all players' choices. A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where no player can do better by changing alone. The prisoner's dilemma is the most famous game: two prisoners who cannot communicate each do best by confessing, even though both would be better off if both stayed silent, which shows how individual rationality can lead to a bad collective outcome. A dominant strategy is a strategy that is best no matter what others do. A zero-sum game is a game where one player's gain is another's loss, while a non-zero-sum game is one where both can gain or both can lose. A repeated game is a game played many times, where players can reward cooperation and punish defection, which can sustain cooperation even when a single game would not. A trigger strategy is a strategy that cooperates until the other player defects, and then defects forever, which can deter defection if the future matters enough. A signalling game is a game in which one player has private information and sends a signal to the other, such as a firm advertising to signal quality, or a job applicant getting a degree to signal ability. A mechanism is a set of rules designed to produce a desired outcome, such as an auction that gives a good to the person who values it most.

How it works: Game theory works by modelling a situation as a game and solving for its equilibria. In the prisoner's dilemma, each prisoner faces a choice: confess or stay silent. If both stay silent, each gets one year in prison; if one confesses and the other stays silent, the confessor goes free and the silent prisoner gets ten years; if both confess, each gets five years. Each prisoner reasons that confessing is better whatever the other does, so both confess and each gets five years, even though both staying silent would give each only one year. The game shows why cooperation is hard without trust or enforcement, and how repeated play, reputation, and contracts can help: if the game is repeated, a player who cooperates can be rewarded and a player who defects can be punished, which can sustain cooperation over time. Game theory also explains why institutions matter. A contract is a mechanism that changes the payoffs, by punishing defection, and a reputation is a mechanism that works through repeated play, by rewarding cooperation. A law is a mechanism that is enforced by the state, and a norm is a mechanism enforced by social pressure. The study of institutions is the study of how to design mechanisms that turn a prisoner's dilemma into a cooperative game, and it is one of the most important applications of game theory, from the design of constitutions to the design of markets.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. Two firms, A and B, each choose a high price or a low price. If both choose high, each earns 10 million pounds; if both choose low, each earns 5 million; if A chooses low and B chooses high, A earns 15 million and B earns 0, and vice versa. Each firm does best by choosing low, whatever the other does, so both choose low and each earns 5 million, even though both choosing high would give each 10 million. A second example is an auction: a bidder who bids their true value in a second-price auction, where the winner pays the second-highest bid, cannot do better by bidding differently, which is why that auction design is used for spectrum sales. A third example is the Cold War: the arms race was a prisoner's dilemma, where each side armed because it feared the other would, even though both would have been safer if neither had armed. A second worked example uses the repeated prisoner's dilemma. Suppose the game is played 100 times, and each player uses a tit-for-tat strategy, cooperating first and then copying the other's previous move. If both cooperate, each gets 1 year in prison per round, for a total of 100 years, while if one defects, the other defects in the next round, and the defector gains in the short run but loses in the long run. The calculation shows that cooperation can be sustained if the future is valued enough, and the strategy is simple enough for anyone to use, which is why it is one of the most famous results in game theory.

Real applications: Game theory is used to design auctions, to set competition policy, to negotiate trade agreements, and to understand how firms compete. It is used in matching, to assign students to schools and doctors to hospitals, in a way that is fair and stable. It is used in environmental policy, to understand why countries struggle to cooperate on climate change, which is a prisoner's dilemma on a global scale, and to design agreements that make cooperation more likely. The study of game theory is therefore the study of how to design rules that turn conflict into cooperation, and it is one of the most practical branches of economics. Game theory is also used in computer science, in the design of algorithms that bid in auctions, that route traffic, and that match riders to drivers. The algorithms are designed as mechanisms, with rules that make it in each participant's interest to tell the truth and to cooperate, and they are used by companies such as Google, Uber, and eBay. The study of game theory is therefore not only about people but also about machines, and it is a bridge between economics, mathematics, and computer science.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is game theory only about games? No, it is about any strategic interaction, from firms setting prices to countries negotiating treaties, and the word game is a technical term for a situation with players and payoffs. Does game theory assume people are selfish? The basic model assumes people maximise their payoffs, but the payoffs can include fairness, reputation, and the welfare of others, and experiments show that people often cooperate more than the simple model predicts. Is the Nash equilibrium always the best outcome? No, it is the outcome where no one wants to change alone, but it can be worse for everyone than another outcome, as in the prisoner's dilemma, which is why institutions matter. Can game theory predict the future? It predicts the outcome of a game given its rules and payoffs, but real situations are more complex, and the prediction is only as good as the model. Is game theory useful in real life? Yes, it is used to design auctions, to negotiate, to set prices, and to understand conflict, and its predictions have been tested in experiments and in the field. Does game theory assume people are rational? The basic model assumes rationality, but behavioural game theory studies how real people play, finding that they cooperate more than the model predicts, and that they care about fairness, which has led to richer models. Can game theory explain war? It can explain why wars happen, when each side fears the other will attack, and why arms control can work, when it changes the payoffs to make cooperation more attractive, though war also has causes that game theory does not capture.

Labour Economics

Definition: Labour economics is the study of the labour market: how wages are set, why some people earn more than others, why unemployment happens, and how education, training, and unions affect work. It studies both the demand for labour, by firms, and the supply of labour, by workers, and it asks how the two are matched. Labour economics matters because work is how most people earn their living, and because the labour market shapes inequality, wellbeing, and the economy as a whole. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Labour economics developed in the 20th century, as economists studied wages, unions, and unemployment. In the 1960s, Gary Becker developed human capital theory, showing that education and training are investments that raise earnings, work that won him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1992. In the 1970s and 1980s, economists studied discrimination, showing how prejudice can affect wages and hiring, and they studied the minimum wage, with a famous study by David Card and Alan Krueger in 1994 finding that a rise in the minimum wage in New Jersey did not reduce employment, a result that challenged the simple model and won Card the Nobel Prize in 2021. In recent decades, labour economics has studied the gig economy, the rise of remote work, and the effects of technology and trade on jobs, and it has used large datasets to track workers over their careers. Labour economics has also studied the effects of technology. The economist David Autor has shown that technology has polarised the labour market: it has replaced routine jobs, such as clerical work and assembly-line work, while increasing demand for both high-skilled jobs, such as programming, and low-skilled service jobs, such as cleaning, which cannot be automated. The result is a hollowing out of the middle, with more jobs at the top and the bottom and fewer in the middle, which has contributed to inequality. The study of technology and work is now a central part of labour economics, and it is used to predict which jobs are at risk and which skills will be valued in the future.

Key concepts: Human capital is the skills and knowledge that make a worker more productive, built by education and training. The wage is the price of labour, set by supply and demand in the labour market. A trade union is an organisation of workers that bargains collectively with employers over pay and conditions. The minimum wage is the lowest wage an employer can legally pay, set by law. Discrimination is the unequal treatment of workers based on characteristics such as gender or ethnicity, rather than on productivity. The gig economy is work arranged through digital platforms, often on a task-by-task basis, without a traditional employment contract. A compensating differential is the extra pay a worker receives for a job that is unpleasant, dangerous, or inconvenient, such as night work or work in a remote place. Efficiency wages are wages above the market level, paid by a firm to motivate workers, to reduce turnover, or to attract better workers. A union wage premium is the extra pay union members earn compared with similar non-union workers, which reflects the bargaining power of the union. The reservation wage is the lowest wage a worker will accept, below which they prefer not to work, and it depends on benefits, on savings, and on the value of leisure.

How it works: Labour economics works by modelling the decisions of workers and firms. A worker decides how much to work by balancing the wage against the value of leisure, and a firm decides how many workers to hire by comparing the wage with the value the worker adds, called the marginal product of labour. A firm hires until the wage equals the marginal product, because hiring another worker would cost more than they add. Education raises the marginal product, so educated workers earn more, and the wage gap between graduates and non-graduates, called the graduate premium, measures the return on education. Unions raise wages by bargaining collectively, and a minimum wage raises the wage floor, which can raise pay for low-paid workers but can also reduce employment if it is set too high, a trade-off that is studied empirically. Labour economics also studies discrimination. The economist Gary Becker showed that discrimination is costly to the discriminator, because they pay more for labour or lose customers, so competition should reduce it, but the evidence is that discrimination persists, because of prejudice, because of statistical discrimination, where employers use group averages to judge individuals, and because of networks that exclude outsiders. The policy response is anti-discrimination law, which bans discrimination in hiring and in pay, and affirmative action, which gives preference to disadvantaged groups, though both are debated.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A graduate earns on average about 10,000 pounds a year more than a non-graduate in the United Kingdom, a premium that reflects the higher productivity of educated workers, though it also reflects the characteristics of the people who go to university. A second example is the minimum wage: the United Kingdom's National Living Wage in 2024 was 11.44 pounds an hour, and a full-time worker on that wage earns about 22,000 pounds a year, calculated as 11.44 times 37.5 hours times 52 weeks. A third example is the gender pay gap: in the United Kingdom in 2024, the median hourly pay of women was about 13 percent lower than that of men, a gap that reflects differences in occupation, in hours, and in caring responsibilities, as well as discrimination, and it has narrowed over time but not closed. A second worked example uses the graduate premium over a career. Suppose a graduate earns 10,000 pounds a year more than a non-graduate, for 40 years, a total of 400,000 pounds, and the cost of a degree, in fees and in lost earnings while studying, is 60,000 pounds. The net gain is 340,000 pounds, a return of more than five times the cost, which is why education is often called the best investment a person can make, though the return varies by subject and by individual, and some graduates earn less than the average. The calculation shows why the graduate premium is studied so carefully, and why students and governments care about it.

Real applications: Labour economics is used to set policy on the minimum wage, on education and training, on unemployment benefits, and on discrimination. It guides the design of pension schemes, of parental leave, and of support for workers who lose their jobs. It is also used by firms to set pay, to design incentives, and to manage their workforce. The study of labour economics is therefore the study of how people earn their living, how their work is valued, and how policy can make the labour market work better for everyone. Labour economics is also the study of the future of work. Remote work, which grew sharply during the pandemic, has changed where people work and how they are managed, and it has raised questions about productivity, about the value of offices, and about the balance between work and life. The gig economy, in which work is arranged through platforms, has raised questions about employment rights, about security, and about the definition of an employee. The study of these changes is ongoing, and it is one of the most active areas in economics, because it affects how people live and work.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does education always raise pay? On average yes, but the return varies by subject, by institution, and by the individual, and some graduates earn less than some non-graduates. Does the minimum wage always cost jobs? The evidence is mixed: a modest minimum wage can raise pay without reducing employment, because firms can absorb the cost or raise prices, but a very high minimum wage can reduce employment, especially for young and low-skilled workers. Is the gender pay gap only discrimination? No, it reflects many factors, including occupation, hours, and caring, and the part that remains after accounting for those factors is the part that may reflect discrimination. Will robots take all the jobs? Technology has displaced some jobs and created others throughout history, and the evidence so far is that it changes the kind of work people do, rather than ending work altogether, though the transition can be painful for those affected. Is the labour market fair? Fairness is a judgement, and economists study what people consider fair, finding that people accept pay differences based on skill and effort more readily than those based on luck or on discrimination. Does immigration lower wages? The evidence is that immigration has a small effect on the wages of native workers, because immigrants also consume goods and services, creating jobs, and because they often do different jobs from natives, though the effect can be larger for low-skilled workers in the short run. Will the gig economy replace traditional jobs? It has grown, but it remains a small share of employment in most countries, and most workers still have traditional jobs, though the boundary between the two is blurring.

Environmental Economics

Definition: Environmental economics is the study of how the economy affects the environment and how the environment affects the economy. It studies pollution, climate change, the use of natural resources, and the value of nature, and it asks how to protect the environment at the lowest cost. Its central idea is that pollution is an externality, a cost that is not paid by the polluter, and that the solution is to make the polluter pay, through a tax, a permit system, or regulation. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Environmental economics began in the 1920s, when Arthur Pigou proposed taxes on pollution, and it grew in the 1960s and 1970s, when pollution and the limits of resources became public concerns, notably after Rachel Carson's Silent Spring in 1962. In the 1980s, the United States introduced emissions trading for sulphur dioxide, which cut acid rain at lower cost than regulation, and the idea was adopted for carbon dioxide in the European Union's Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005. In the 1990s and 2000s, the economics of climate change became central, notably in the Stern Review of 2006, which argued that the cost of acting on climate change was far lower than the cost of inaction. In 2015, the Paris Agreement committed almost all countries to limit warming, and the study of how to meet that commitment at the lowest cost is now a major part of the field. Environmental economics has also studied the value of nature. The economist Robert Costanza and his colleagues estimated in 1997 that the world's ecosystems provide services, such as pollination, water purification, and climate regulation, worth about 33 trillion dollars a year, more than the world's GDP at the time, though the estimate is uncertain and debated. The United Kingdom's Dasgupta Review of 2021, led by Partha Dasgupta, argued that nature is an asset, like capital, and that it should be included in national accounts, so that its depletion is counted as a cost, not ignored. The review has influenced policy in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, and it is a major step toward an economics that values nature.

Key concepts: An externality is a cost or benefit that falls on others, and pollution is the classic negative externality. A carbon tax is a tax on the carbon content of fuels, which raises the price of polluting and encourages cleaner alternatives. Emissions trading, or cap and trade, is a system in which the government sets a cap on total emissions and lets firms trade permits, so the reduction happens where it is cheapest. The social cost of carbon is an estimate of the damage caused by emitting one tonne of carbon dioxide. A natural resource is a material from nature, such as oil, fish, or timber, which can be renewable, like fish, or non-renewable, like oil. A renewable resource is a resource that can regenerate, such as fish or timber, if it is not overused. A non-renewable resource is a resource that cannot regenerate on a human timescale, such as oil or coal, and its use raises questions about how much to use now and how much to leave for the future. Hotelling's rule, named after Harold Hotelling, says that the price of a non-renewable resource should rise at the rate of interest, so that the owner is indifferent between extracting now and later, and it is a central result in resource economics. A biodiversity offset is a payment to protect or restore nature elsewhere, to compensate for damage here, though its effectiveness is debated.

How it works: Environmental economics works by putting a price on pollution. A carbon tax of, say, 50 pounds per tonne of carbon dioxide raises the price of petrol, of gas, and of electricity from coal, so people use less of them and switch to cleaner alternatives, and the revenue can be used to cut other taxes or to help low-income households. Emissions trading works differently: the government sets a cap, for example 1 billion tonnes, issues permits for that amount, and lets firms buy and sell them, so a firm that can cut emissions cheaply sells its permits to a firm for which cutting is expensive, and the total cut happens at the lowest cost. Both tools are more efficient than telling every firm exactly how to cut, because they let each firm find its own cheapest way, and they give a continuous incentive to innovate. Environmental economics also studies the discount rate, the rate at which future costs and benefits are valued today. A high discount rate means the future is valued little, so it is worth spending little now to prevent future damage, while a low discount rate means the future is valued more, so it is worth spending more now. The Stern Review used a low discount rate, which is why it concluded that acting on climate change is worth the cost, while other economists, who used a higher rate, concluded the opposite. The debate about the discount rate is a debate about how much we value the future, and it is as much ethical as economic.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A power station emits 1 million tonnes of carbon dioxide a year, and a carbon tax of 50 pounds per tonne costs it 50 million pounds a year, so it has a strong incentive to switch to cleaner fuel or to capture its emissions. A second example is the European Union's Emissions Trading System, which covers about 40 percent of the EU's emissions and has cut them by about 40 percent since 2005, while the price of a permit has risen from a few euros to about 70 euros per tonne. A third example is the social cost of carbon: the United States government has estimated it at about 51 dollars per tonne, though some researchers put it higher, at over 100 dollars, because the damage from climate change is uncertain and long-lasting. A second worked example uses the discount rate. Suppose climate damage of 100 billion pounds will occur in 100 years. At a discount rate of 5 percent, its present value is 100 billion divided by 1.05 to the power of 100, which is about 760 million pounds, a small sum, so it is not worth spending much now to prevent it. At a discount rate of 1 percent, its present value is about 37 billion pounds, a large sum, so it is worth spending a great deal now. The calculation shows that the discount rate, a single number, can change the conclusion by a factor of almost 50, which is why it is so contested.

Real applications: Environmental economics is used to design climate policy, to manage fisheries and forests, and to value nature. It guides the choice between taxes, trading, and regulation, and it is used to judge whether a project, such as a new road or a new wind farm, is worth its environmental cost. The field is central to the response to climate change, which is the largest market failure in history, and its tools, carbon pricing and emissions trading, are now used in more than 70 countries and regions, covering about a quarter of global emissions. The study of environmental economics is therefore the study of how to protect the planet without wasting resources, and how to share the cost fairly. Environmental economics is also used to design policies for biodiversity, for water, and for waste. The same tools, taxes, trading, and regulation, are used to protect forests, to manage water, and to reduce waste, and the same principle applies: make the polluter pay, and let the market find the cheapest way to reduce harm. The field is growing, as the environment becomes a more pressing concern, and it is now a core part of economics, taught to every student and used by every government.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is a carbon tax a punishment for polluters? No, it is a price that makes the polluter pay for the damage they cause, so they have an incentive to pollute less, and the revenue can be returned to households or used to cut other taxes. Does emissions trading work? The evidence from the EU and from the United States' sulphur dioxide programme is that it cuts emissions at lower cost than regulation, though the cap must be set tightly enough to matter. Should the environment be valued in money? Economists argue that putting a value on nature, such as the value of a forest for clean water and for recreation, helps decision-makers compare it with other uses, though some argue that nature has a value beyond money. Can growth and environmental protection go together? The evidence from countries that have cut emissions while growing, such as the United Kingdom, which has cut emissions by about half since 1990 while its economy grew, suggests that they can, if the right policies are in place. Is it possible to put a price on nature? Economists argue that it is necessary, because decisions are made with or without a price, and a price that is too low is better than no price at all, though others argue that nature has a value that cannot be captured in money. Does economic growth harm the environment? It has historically, but the link can be broken by clean technology and by policy, as the United Kingdom's experience since 1990 shows. Can the market solve climate change? The market can help, through carbon pricing, but it needs government to set the price and to enforce it, because the market alone will not price a global externality.

Definition: The history of economic thought is the story of how people have understood the economy, from the earliest writers on trade and wealth to the modern schools of economics. It is a story of ideas that were tried, that succeeded or failed, and that were replaced by better ones, and it is essential to understanding why economists disagree today: they inherit different traditions, with different assumptions about how the economy works. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: The earliest economic writing is found in ancient Greece, where Xenophon wrote on household management and Aristotle distinguished between use value and exchange value. In the Middle Ages, Thomas Aquinas discussed the just price, and in the 16th and 17th centuries the mercantilists argued that a country should export more than it imported to accumulate gold. Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations, published in 1776, founded modern economics by arguing that markets, guided by self-interest and competition, could organise production. In the 19th century, David Ricardo developed the theory of comparative advantage and of rent, Karl Marx argued in Das Kapital, published in 1867, that capitalism would be replaced by socialism, and the marginalists, including William Stanley Jevons and Leon Walras, developed the theory of supply and demand. In the 20th century, John Maynard Keynes argued in 1936 that government could stabilise the economy, Milton Friedman argued in the 1960s and 1970s that money and free markets were the key, and the field split into schools that still debate today. The history of economic thought has also been shaped by women, whose contributions were often overlooked. Joan Robinson, writing in the 1930s, developed the theory of imperfect competition, showing that most markets are neither perfectly competitive nor monopolies, but in between, and her work is now a standard part of the subject. Rosa Luxemburg, writing in the early 20th century, studied imperialism and the accumulation of capital, and her work is still read in development economics. Elinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel Prize in 2009, studied how communities manage common resources, and her work has transformed the study of public goods. Their contributions are now recognised, and the history of the subject is being rewritten to include them.

Key concepts: The classical school, from Smith to Mill, studied how markets work and how wealth is created and shared. The Marxist school, from Marx onward, studied capitalism as a system of class conflict and predicted its replacement. The neoclassical school, from the 1870s onward, used mathematics and the idea of marginal utility to study supply and demand. The Keynesian school, from Keynes in 1936, studied how demand determines output and employment, and how government can stabilise the economy. The monetarist school, led by Friedman, argued that the money supply is the main influence on inflation and on the economy. The Austrian school, from Carl Menger onward, emphasised individual choice, entrepreneurship, and the limits of central planning. A paradigm is a framework of ideas that guides a science, and a paradigm shift is a change in that framework, such as the shift from classical to Keynesian economics in the 1930s. The history of economic thought is often told as a series of paradigm shifts, though the reality is more gradual, with old ideas persisting alongside new ones. The Methodenstreit, or method dispute, was a debate in the late 19th century between the Austrian school, which emphasised theory, and the German historical school, which emphasised history and institutions, and it shaped the development of economics in both countries. The Cambridge capital controversy was a debate in the 1960s about the measurement of capital, which showed the limits of the neoclassical theory of production.

How it works: The history of economic thought advances by debate and by events. The Great Depression of the 1930s discredited the classical view that markets always return to full employment, and Keynes's theory replaced it. The high inflation of the 1970s discredited the simple Keynesian view, and monetarism and new classical economics replaced it. The financial crisis of 2008 discredited the view that financial markets are always efficient, and behavioural economics and new Keynesian economics gained influence. Each crisis has shown the limits of the prevailing theory, and each has led to a new synthesis, which is why the history of the subject is also a history of its crises. The history of economic thought is also the history of its institutions. The Royal Economic Society, founded in 1890, the American Economic Association, founded in 1885, and the Econometric Society, founded in 1930, have shaped the subject by publishing journals, by awarding prizes, and by setting standards. The Nobel Prize in Economics, first awarded in 1969, has become the highest honour in the subject, and its winners have shaped the direction of research. The history of these institutions is part of the history of the ideas, because ideas spread through journals, through conferences, and through teaching.

Worked examples: Take the numbers of the history. Smith's Wealth of Nations was published in 1776, 250 years ago, and it is still read today. Keynes's General Theory was published in 1936, 90 years ago, and its argument about demand is still central to policy. Friedman's major work on money, A Monetary History of the United States, was published in 1963, and its argument that the Great Depression was caused by a fall in the money supply changed how central banks act in a crisis. The Nobel Prize in Economics, first awarded in 1969, has been won by economists from many schools, including Keynesians, monetarists, and behavioural economists, which shows that the subject has no single orthodoxy, but a continuing debate. A second worked example uses the length of the subject. Economics as a modern subject is about 250 years old, from Smith in 1776 to today. In that time, the world economy has grown enormously: world GDP per person is about 15 times higher in real terms than in 1776, and life expectancy has more than doubled, from about 30 years to over 70. The ideas of economics have both described that growth and helped to cause it, by guiding policy, by shaping institutions, and by spreading the knowledge of how markets work. The history of the subject is therefore also a history of the modern world.

Real applications: The history of economic thought is useful because it shows that economic ideas are not timeless truths but products of their time, shaped by the problems of the day, and that they must be judged by their evidence and by their results. It teaches humility: the confident predictions of one generation are often overturned by the next, and the best economists are those who learn from the past without being bound by it. It also preserves the diversity of the subject: the classical, Marxist, Keynesian, monetarist, and behavioural traditions each capture part of the truth, and a good economist knows all of them, even if they favour one. The history of economic thought is useful for judging new ideas. A new idea that resembles an old one that failed should be treated with caution, while a new idea that builds on an old success should be taken seriously. The history also shows that economics is not a set of timeless truths but a conversation across centuries, in which each generation adds its own insights and corrects the errors of the last. The best economists are those who know the history, because they know what has been tried, what has worked, and what has not.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is economics a single science with one right answer? No, it is a subject with several schools, each with its own assumptions, and the debate between them is part of the subject. Was Marx right? His prediction that capitalism would collapse has not come true, but his analysis of inequality and of power in the economy remains influential, and his work is still studied. Was Keynes right? His argument that demand matters and that government can stabilise the economy is now accepted by most economists, though the details are still debated. Does the history of economics matter for policy today? Yes, because the policies of today, from central bank independence to fiscal stimulus, are the products of that history, and understanding where they came from helps to judge when they apply and when they do not. Was economics always mathematical? No, the early economists wrote in words, and the mathematisation of economics began in the late 19th century and accelerated in the 20th, though some economists argue that it has gone too far and that economics should pay more attention to history and to institutions. Is the history of economic thought still taught? It is taught less than it was, because the subject has become more technical, but it is still taught in many universities, and its defenders argue that it is essential to understanding the subject. Does the history matter for policy? Yes, because the policies of today are the products of the ideas of the past, and understanding their origins helps to judge when they apply and when they do not.

Famous Economists

Definition: Famous economists are the thinkers whose ideas have shaped how we understand the economy, from Adam Smith, who founded the subject in 1776, to the Nobel Prize winners of today. This record introduces the most influential economists, what they argued, and why their ideas still matter. It is not a ranking, and it does not include living people in detail beyond their public work, because the subject is still being written. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice.

History: Adam Smith, a Scottish philosopher, published The Wealth of Nations in 1776, arguing that markets and competition could organise production, and that the division of labour raises productivity. David Ricardo, an English stockbroker, published On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation in 1817, developing the theory of comparative advantage and of rent. John Stuart Mill, writing in the 19th century, combined economics with philosophy and argued for liberty and for the education of women. Karl Marx, writing Das Kapital in 1867, argued that capitalism exploits workers and will be replaced. Alfred Marshall, whose Principles of Economics appeared in 1890, brought supply and demand together and founded the neoclassical school. John Maynard Keynes, whose General Theory appeared in 1936, argued that government could stabilise the economy, and his ideas shaped policy after the Second World War. Milton Friedman, who won the Nobel Prize in 1976, argued that free markets and stable money were the keys to prosperity, and his ideas shaped policy in the 1980s. Amartya Sen, who won the Nobel Prize in 1998, argued that development should be judged by people's freedoms, and his work on famine showed that famines are caused by a failure of entitlements, not only by a shortage of food. Famous economists have also included the founders of new fields. Gary Becker, who won the Nobel Prize in 1992, founded the economics of the family, of crime, and of discrimination, showing that economic ideas could be applied far beyond markets. Robert Lucas, who won the Nobel Prize in 1995, founded the new classical school, arguing that expectations matter and that policy cannot systematically fool people, an idea called the Lucas critique. Paul Krugman, who won the Nobel Prize in 2008, founded the new trade theory, showing that trade can be driven by increasing returns and by consumer love of variety, not only by comparative advantage. These are examples of how a single economist can create a whole field, and how the subject grows by the work of individuals.

Key concepts: Classical economics is the tradition from Smith to Mill, which studied markets, trade, and the distribution of income. Neoclassical economics is the tradition from Marshall onward, which used mathematics and marginal analysis to study supply and demand. Keynesian economics is the tradition from Keynes, which studies demand, unemployment, and the role of government. Monetarism is the tradition led by Friedman, which emphasises the money supply and the limits of government. Behavioural economics is the tradition from Kahneman and Thaler, which studies how people actually decide, rather than how a rational person would. Development economics is the tradition from Sen and others, which studies poverty and the wellbeing of people in poor countries. A school of thought is a group of economists who share assumptions and methods, such as the Keynesian school or the Austrian school. A heterodox economist is one who works outside the mainstream, such as a Marxist or an institutionalist, and heterodox economics is a diverse field that challenges the mainstream. The mainstream, or neoclassical, school is the dominant approach, which uses optimisation, equilibrium, and marginal analysis, and it is taught in most universities. The debate between the mainstream and heterodox economics is a continuing one, and it is part of the vitality of the subject.

How it works: Each economist's ideas can be understood as an answer to a question of their time. Smith answered the question of how a market economy could work without central direction. Ricardo answered the question of why countries trade, even when one is better at everything. Marx answered the question of why workers are poor in a rich economy. Marshall answered the question of how prices are set, by bringing supply and demand together. Keynes answered the question of why unemployment persists in a depression, and how government can end it. Friedman answered the question of why inflation happens, and how to control it. Sen answered the question of what development is for, and his answer, that it is for freedom, has shaped the United Nations' approach to development. The ideas are linked: each new thinker built on, and often criticised, the ones before. Famous economists have also been public figures. Keynes was a government adviser and a negotiator at the Bretton Woods conference in 1944, which created the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. Friedman was a public intellectual, whose television series Free to Choose in 1980 brought his ideas to a wide audience. Sen has been a public voice on famine, on development, and on justice, and his work has influenced the United Nations and governments around the world. The public role of economists is a reminder that economics is not only an academic subject but also a part of public life, and that economists have a responsibility to communicate their ideas clearly and honestly.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. Smith's Wealth of Nations was published in 1776, and it is 250 years old. Keynes's General Theory was published in 1936, 90 years ago. The Nobel Prize in Economics was first awarded in 1969, and it has been awarded to more than 90 economists, including the first woman, Elinor Ostrom, in 2009, and the youngest, Esther Duflo, who won in 2019 at the age of 46. The number of economists in the world is in the hundreds of thousands, but the ideas of a few dozen have shaped the subject, and their books are still read, debated, and tested today. A second worked example uses the Nobel Prize. The prize was first awarded in 1969 to Ragnar Frisch and Jan Tinbergen, for their work on econometrics and on economic policy. Since then it has been awarded to more than 90 economists, including 3 women, Elinor Ostrom in 2009, Esther Duflo in 2019, and Claudia Goldin in 2023, who won for her work on women's labour market outcomes. The prize is awarded by the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, and it is the highest honour in the subject, though it is not one of the original Nobel Prizes, but a prize in memory of Alfred Nobel, established by Sweden's central bank in 1968.

Real applications: Famous economists matter because their ideas shape policy and public debate. The arguments about markets, about government, about trade, and about inequality that fill the newspapers today are the same arguments that Smith, Ricardo, Marx, Keynes, and Friedman had, in different forms, and understanding their ideas is the best way to understand the debate. The study of famous economists is therefore not hero-worship but an introduction to the ideas that still drive the subject, and a reminder that economics is a human activity, carried out by people with values and with blind spots, whose ideas must be judged by evidence, not by authority. Famous economists are studied not only for their ideas but also for their methods. Smith used history and observation, Ricardo used logic and models, Keynes used intuition and policy experience, Friedman used data and statistics, and Sen used philosophy and fieldwork. The diversity of methods is a strength of the subject, because different questions need different methods, and the best economists use the method that fits the question, rather than forcing every question into one method. The study of famous economists is therefore also a study of how to do economics, and of the craft of the subject.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is Adam Smith the most important economist? He is often called the father of economics, because he founded the subject, but the judgement depends on what is valued: Keynes is more important for policy, Marx for the critique of capitalism, and Sen for development. Are economists' ideas still relevant? Yes, the ideas of Smith, Ricardo, Keynes, and Friedman are still taught, still debated, and still used in policy, which is a measure of their importance. Do economists agree on who is most important? No, and the disagreement is part of the subject: different schools value different thinkers, and the list of famous economists is itself a matter of debate. Can a new economist become famous? Yes, the Nobel Prize continues to be awarded, and new ideas, from behavioural economics to the economics of climate change, are shaping the subject today. Are famous economists always right? No, even the most famous have been wrong, and their errors are part of the history of the subject: Smith was wrong about the value of labour, Marx was wrong about the collapse of capitalism, and Keynes was wrong about some details of his theory, though his main argument has stood the test of time. Should students read the original works? Yes, the originals are often clearer and more interesting than the textbooks, and they show how the ideas were developed, though they can be hard to read, and a good textbook is a useful guide. Is economics a young subject? It is about 250 years old as a modern subject, which is young compared with mathematics or philosophy, and it is still developing, with new fields and new ideas appearing every year.

Economics in Everyday Life

Definition: Economics in everyday life is the use of economic ideas to understand the choices people make every day: what to buy, how much to save, whether to take a job, where to live, and how to spend time. Economics is not only about governments and markets; it is about the trade-offs in ordinary life, and the same ideas, scarcity, opportunity cost, incentives, and supply and demand, apply to a household budget, to a career, and to a holiday. This record is public knowledge only; it is not personal financial advice, and it does not recommend any particular financial product or decision.

History: Everyday economics has always been practised, even when it was not called economics. Households have always budgeted, farmers have always decided what to plant, and traders have always compared prices. The formal study of everyday decisions began with the household economics of the 19th century, and it grew in the 20th century with the study of consumer behaviour, of saving, and of the family. Gary Becker, who won the Nobel Prize in 1992, applied economics to the family, to marriage, and to crime, showing that economic ideas could explain behaviour far beyond markets. Behavioural economics, from the 1970s onward, showed how people actually decide in everyday life, with shortcuts, with procrastination, and with a concern for fairness. Today, everyday economics is taught in schools, in personal finance courses, and in the media, where the language of economics is used to explain the news. Everyday economics also includes the economics of time. A person who works 40 hours a week and sleeps 56 hours has 72 hours left for other things, and how they spend those hours is an economic decision, because time is scarce. The value of time is measured by the wage: an hour is worth what it could earn, though leisure is also valuable, and the trade-off between work and leisure is one of the most important decisions in life. The rise of remote work has changed the value of time, by saving commuting time, and the rise of the gig economy has changed it by making work more flexible, though also less secure. The economics of time is therefore a central part of everyday economics.

Key concepts: A budget is a plan for income and spending, which shows what is affordable and what must be given up. Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative given up, and it applies to time as well as to money: an hour spent watching television is an hour not spent working or studying. An incentive is a reward or penalty that changes behaviour, such as a discount that encourages buying or a fine that discourages parking. A sunk cost is a cost that has already been paid and cannot be recovered, and it should be ignored when making a decision, though people often do not. Compound interest is interest on interest, which makes savings grow faster over time and debts grow faster too. A time budget is a record of how time is spent, used by economists to study work, leisure, and household production. The household production function is the idea that households produce goods and services, such as meals and childcare, using time and market goods, and that the value of that production is not counted in GDP, though it is real. The value of leisure is the value of time not spent working, and it is measured by what a person would pay to have more of it. The opportunity cost of time is the value of the best alternative use of that time, and it is the reason that time, like money, must be budgeted.

How it works: Everyday economics works by making trade-offs explicit. A household with an income of 3,000 pounds a month that spends 1,000 on rent, 500 on food, 300 on transport, and 500 on other things saves 700 pounds, and it can see what would happen if it spent more on one thing and less on another. A person deciding whether to take a job that pays 5,000 pounds more but requires an hour's commute each way must weigh the extra pay against the extra time, which is worth something, even if it is not paid in money. A shopper who compares the price per kilogram of two brands is using the idea of relative prices, and a saver who puts money in a pension is using compound interest, which grows the money over decades. The method is the same as in the rest of economics: define the choice, count the costs and benefits, and choose the option with the best balance. Everyday economics also includes the economics of health. A person who exercises, eats well, and does not smoke invests in their health, which is a form of human capital, and the return is a longer, healthier life. The economics of health studies how people make these investments, how health care is funded, and how to allocate scarce medical resources. The pandemic of 2020 showed the importance of health economics, as governments weighed the cost of lockdowns against the cost of illness, and the value of vaccines against the cost of producing them. Health is an economic good, because it is scarce, it is valued, and it involves trade-offs, and the study of it is a growing field.

Worked examples: Take a simple example. A household earns 3,000 pounds a month and spends 2,300, saving 700, a saving rate of about 23 percent. If it saves 700 pounds a month for a year, it saves 8,400 pounds, and if it invests that at 5 percent a year, it grows by 420 pounds in a year. A second example is time: a worker who earns 20 pounds an hour and spends an hour commuting each way spends 40 pounds of time a day, or 200 pounds a week, on commuting, which is a real cost, even though it is not paid in cash. A third example is a sunk cost: a person who has paid 50 pounds for a concert ticket and then feels ill should decide whether to go based on how they feel now, not on the 50 pounds, because the money is gone either way, though many people find that hard to do. A second worked example uses the value of time. Suppose a worker earns 20 pounds an hour and spends 10 hours a week commuting, a total of 200 pounds of time a week, or about 10,000 pounds a year, assuming 50 working weeks. If remote work saves half that time, it saves 5,000 pounds a year in time, which is a real benefit, even though it is not paid in cash. The calculation shows why remote work is valued by workers, and why firms that offer it can attract staff without raising pay, because time is part of the compensation, not only money.

Real applications: Economics in everyday life is used to make better decisions, to understand the news, and to take part in public debate. A person who understands inflation understands why their pay rise may not make them better off, a person who understands interest rates understands why their mortgage payment changes, and a person who understands supply and demand understands why prices rise when a product is scarce. The study of everyday economics is therefore a form of practical literacy, as important as reading and writing, and it is the reason economics is taught in schools and discussed in the media. Everyday economics is also the economics of relationships. A person who helps a neighbour, who shares a meal, or who volunteers is producing a good that is not sold in a market, but it is real, and it is valued by the people involved. The economics of the family, founded by Gary Becker, studies marriage, children, and the division of labour in the household, and it shows that economic ideas can explain behaviour that seems far from markets. The study of everyday economics is therefore the study of how people live, not only how they earn and spend, and it is a reminder that economics is about people, not only about money.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is economics useful if I am not an economist? Yes, the ideas of scarcity, opportunity cost, and incentives apply to every decision, and understanding them helps people make better choices, even if they never study economics formally. Should I follow economic advice about my money? This record gives general knowledge only, not personal advice, and personal financial decisions should be made with care, using regulated advice where needed. Is everyday economics the same as personal finance? Personal finance is the practical application of economics to household money, while everyday economics is broader, covering time, work, family, and health as well as money. Can economics explain everything in everyday life? No, people also act from love, from habit, and from values that economics does not capture, and a good economist knows the limits of the subject. Is economics relevant to my daily life? Yes, every decision, from what to buy to how to spend time, involves scarcity, opportunity cost, and incentives, and understanding those ideas helps people make better decisions. Should I study economics? A basic understanding is useful for everyone, and it is taught in schools and in the media, though a formal study is not necessary to benefit from its ideas. Can economics make me richer? It can help people make better financial decisions, by understanding compound interest, by avoiding debt, and by saving for the future, though it cannot guarantee wealth, and this record is general knowledge only, not personal financial advice.
