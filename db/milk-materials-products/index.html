Casein Bioplastic Sheet and Button Stock simple · bioplastics · beginner

Casein bioplastic is a hard, horn-like material made from the protein of milk. Skim milk is curdled, the curd is washed and dried to casein powder, and the powder is pressed with a little water and plant-derived plasticizer into sheets that can be cut, drilled, and polished into buttons, beads, and small ornaments. This record covers the basic sheet material and its product grades. The historical industrial version, galalith, was hardened with formaldehyde; the formulation here hardens by pressing and slow drying instead, and it is not the formaldehyde process. For the older craft method of casein glue and milk paint, see the milk-uses records on casein glue and milk paint; this record covers the material and its product specifications, not the craft method.

Materials

  • Skim milk or dried casein powder
  • Food-grade acid for curdling (vinegar or citric acid solution)
  • Water for washing the curd
  • Plant-derived glycerol as plasticizer
  • Flat molds or a press with smooth plates
  • Fine sandpaper and polishing cloth
  • Kitchen scale

Method

  1. Curdle skim milk with acid, drain the whey, and wash the curd in several changes of clean water until it no longer smells sour.
  2. Dry the curd fully, then grind it to a fine, even powder and sieve out any lumps.
  3. Mix the powder with a small amount of water and glycerol until it forms a stiff, even dough.
  4. Press the dough into a flat mold in a thin, even layer and leave it under light pressure while it dries slowly for several days.
  5. Trim, drill, sand, and polish the hardened sheet into buttons or ornaments, and seal the finished piece with a plant-oil finish to slow moisture uptake.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Slow drying matters: a sheet dried too fast warps and cracks, and a sheet used in wet conditions softens. Keep drying sheets flat and out of direct heat.

Yield

A 600 g batch of dough yields about 12 button blanks; see the demo.

Variations

  • Natural ivory sheets for buttons.
  • Tinted sheets with mineral pigment mixed into the dough.
  • Thin sheets for beads and inlay.
  • Thick stock for buckles and small plaques.

Milk Paint: Basic Powder Formulation simple · paint · beginner

Milk paint is a paint whose binder is milk protein. The basic product is sold as a dry powder of milk-derived casein, lime, and mineral pigment; the user mixes it with water to a creamy paint that dries to a flat, chalky, breathable finish on wood, plaster, and raw furniture. This record gives the powder formulation and its product grades. The milk-uses record on casein glue and milk paint covers the historical craft method; this record covers the paint as a formulated product, with mix ratios, coverage, and grade differences.

Materials

  • Casein powder (milk protein)
  • Hydrated lime (builders lime)
  • Mineral pigments
  • Clean water for mixing
  • Mixing bucket and stirrer
  • Paintbrush or sprayer
  • Kitchen scale

Method

  1. Weigh casein powder, lime, and pigment in the ratio given in the demo and blend the dry powders until the color is perfectly even.
  2. Add water slowly while stirring until the paint is the consistency of single cream, then let it stand for 30 minutes.
  3. Stir again before use and strain out any lumps through a fine sieve.
  4. Brush onto clean, raw, or primed wood in thin coats, allowing each coat to dry before the next.
  5. Seal the finished surface with a plant-oil or wax topcoat where washability is wanted, since unsealed milk paint is not washable.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Lime is caustic when wet: wear gloves and eye protection, avoid breathing lime dust, and keep mixed paint off skin. Mixed paint keeps only a day or two; mix fresh batches as needed.

Yield

A 600 g powder batch mixed with water covers about 6 square metres in one coat; see the demo.

Variations

  • Plain white powder for priming.
  • Earth-pigment colors for furniture.
  • Bright mineral colors for decoration.
  • Powder sold in small pouches for touch-ups.

Casein Adhesive: Product Grades and Bond Strengths simple · adhesives · beginner

Casein adhesive is a glue whose binder is milk protein. It is sold as a powder to be mixed with water, or as a ready-mixed liquid, and it is graded by viscosity, open time, and bond strength for wood, paper, and board. This record specifies the grades and gives measured bond strengths for each, so a maker can choose a grade for a joint instead of guessing. The milk-uses casein glue record covers the craft method of making the glue; this record covers the adhesive as a graded product, with test figures and grade selection.

Materials

  • Casein adhesive powder, graded
  • Clean water
  • Mixing vessel and paddle
  • Wood or board test pieces
  • Clamps
  • Spring scale or weights for testing
  • Kitchen scale

Method

  1. Choose a grade by the table in the description: thin grade for paper, standard grade for general wood joints, and heavy grade for laminating.
  2. Mix the powder with water to the grade's stated ratio and stir until smooth, then let it stand until fully dissolved.
  3. Spread a thin, even film on both joint faces and bring them together within the grade's open time.
  4. Clamp firmly and leave the joint clamped for the grade's stated press time, then leave it unstressed for a full day.
  5. Test a sample joint by hanging weights from it, and record the breaking load to confirm the grade's stated strength.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Casein glue joints weaken in prolonged damp and are not suited to outdoor or constantly wet use. Keep the powder dry; damp powder clumps and loses strength.

Yield

A test joint of 25 sq cm held 40 kg before breaking; see the demo for the strength calculation.

Variations

  • Thin grade for paper and veneer.
  • Standard grade for furniture joints.
  • Heavy grade for laminating boards.
  • Water-resistant grade with a plant-oil additive for kitchens.

Working demo program

    print('Safe working load at a factor of 4: %.1f kg' % (LOAD_KG / 4.0))
    print('Adhesive for %d joints at 2.0 g per joint: %.1f g' % (PIECES, PIECES * 2.0))

Milk Protein Fiber: Basic Staple Fiber simple · textiles · beginner

Milk protein fiber is a textile fiber spun from casein, the protein of milk. Casein is dissolved, extruded through fine holes into a hardening bath, and cut into staple fibers that feel soft and silky, take dye well, and blend with plant fibers. This record covers the basic staple fiber: its properties, fineness grades, and yield. It is a materials record; yarn and fabric products are covered in later records of this pair.

Materials

  • Casein powder
  • Alkali solution for dissolving (handled with care)
  • Hardening bath
  • Spinneret or syringe with fine holes
  • Rinsing water
  • Drying rack
  • Scale and measuring jug

Method

  1. Dissolve casein powder in the alkali solution, stirring gently until no lumps remain, and let the solution stand until air bubbles clear.
  2. Extrude the solution through fine holes into the hardening bath, drawing the filaments out slowly and evenly.
  3. Rinse the filaments in clean water until the rinse water runs clear.
  4. Dry the filaments flat or on a rack, away from direct heat, then cut them to staple length.
  5. Grade the staple by fineness and length, and store it dry in labeled bags.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Alkali solutions are caustic: wear gloves and eye protection and rinse any splash at once with plenty of water. Never pour the hardening bath into food vessels.

Yield

A 500 g batch of fiber at 2.0 tex spins to a very long yarn; see the demo.

Variations

  • Fine staple for soft knit blends.
  • Medium staple for woven blends.
  • Coarse staple for felts and stuffing.
  • Dyed staple in mineral and plant colors.

Milk-Protein Casting Film: Basic Film simple · films · beginner

A milk-protein casting film is a thin, flexible sheet cast from a casein solution and dried on a flat plate. It is used as a wrapping and interleaving film, as a coating carrier, and as a base for printed labels in dry conditions. This record covers the basic film: its casting method, thickness grades, and limits. The film is a non-edible product and is not food, even though it is made from milk protein; food-contact use would require food-grade materials throughout, which this basic grade does not claim.

Materials

  • Casein powder
  • Water
  • Plant-derived glycerol as plasticizer
  • Flat casting plate (glass or polished metal)
  • Spreader bar or blade
  • Drying cabinet or warm room
  • Micrometer or calipers

Method

  1. Dissolve casein in warm water with glycerol, stirring until the solution is clear and free of bubbles.
  2. Pour the solution onto the casting plate and spread it to an even thickness with the spreader bar.
  3. Dry the film slowly at warm room temperature until it releases cleanly from the plate.
  4. Measure the thickness in several places and trim the film to size.
  5. Store the film flat, dry, and interleaved with paper, since it curls in humidity.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Films soften and stick in damp air; they are dry-condition products only. Keep the casting room ventilated while the solution dries.

Yield

A 200 g batch casts a large area of 0.10 mm film; see the demo.

Variations

  • Thin film for interleaving.
  • Medium film for wrapping dry goods.
  • Thick film for label stock.
  • Tinted film with mineral pigment.

Galalith-Style Buttons and Ornaments compounded · bioplastics · intermediate

Galalith was the early industrial casein plastic, made famous for buttons, beads, buckles, and ornaments in the early twentieth century. Its deep, horn-like polish and ivory and tortoiseshell colors made it a prized button material before modern plastics. This record specifies galalith-style products as they are made today: formaldehyde-free hardened casein stock, cut and polished to product dimensions, with stated thickness, finish, and care limits. The original formaldehyde hardening process is described here for history only and is not reproduced, since formaldehyde is hazardous. Finished pieces are display and clothing items, not food-contact items.

Materials

  • Hardened casein sheet stock
  • Saw and drill for button work
  • Button molds or cutting dies
  • Polishing wheels and compounds
  • Plant-oil finish
  • Calipers
  • Cloth for polishing

Method

  1. Select sheet stock of the stated thickness for the button size, and mark out the blanks with a template.
  2. Cut the blanks, drill the buttonholes, and round the edges with fine files.
  3. Sand through progressively finer grades until the surface is smooth.
  4. Polish on a cloth wheel until the horn-like shine appears, then seal with a thin plant-oil finish.
  5. Inspect each piece for cracks and warping, and store finished buttons dry, away from damp and heat.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Hardened casein softens in prolonged damp and can warp; buttons are removed before washing garments in hot water. Keep polishing dust out of the air with ventilation.

Yield

A 480 g batch of stock yields 48 button blanks; see the demo.

Variations

  • Four-hole shirt buttons.
  • Shank buttons for coats.
  • Beads and necklace ornaments.
  • Buckles and hair ornaments.

Milk Paint: Washable and Exterior Grades compounded · paint · intermediate

Washable and exterior grades of milk paint add a protective topcoat system to the basic milk paint powder. The paint itself stays the same casein and lime formulation, but a plant-oil finish, a wax finish, or both are applied over it to give washability, and exterior grades add a weather-resistant oil topcoat and stricter surface preparation. This record specifies the grades, their topcoat systems, and their coverage and recoat times. It is a product-grades record; the basic powder formulation is covered in the earlier record of this pair.

Materials

  • Milk paint powder (casein and lime)
  • Mineral pigments
  • Plant oil for topcoat
  • Beeswax for wax topcoat (non-food grade is fine here, as paint is not food)
  • Brushes and cloths
  • Primer for exterior wood
  • Scale

Method

  1. Mix the milk paint powder with water as in the basic formulation and apply two thin coats, letting each dry fully.
  2. For the washable grade, apply two thin coats of plant oil, wiping off excess and letting each coat dry.
  3. For the wax grade, rub on a thin beeswax paste coat and buff it once dry.
  4. For the exterior grade, prime raw wood first, paint, then apply three oil coats, recoating each year or when the surface looks dry.
  5. Test washability on a hidden spot before washing a finished piece.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Oil-soaked cloths must be laid flat to dry, never bunched up, since some plant oils can heat as they dry. Exterior grades still need regular recoating; they are not permanent weatherproofing.

Yield

A 240 g batch of topcoat covers about 12 square metres; see the demo.

Variations

  • Washable interior grade with oil topcoat.
  • Wax grade with a soft sheen.
  • Exterior grade with three oil coats.
  • Floor grade with extra oil coats for wear.

Milk Protein Yarn and Knit Fabric compounded · textiles · intermediate

Milk protein yarn is spun from casein staple fiber, alone or blended with plant fibers, and knitted into soft fabrics. The yarn is graded by tex and twist, and the fabric by weight per square metre and hand feel. This record covers yarn grades and a basic knit fabric product: its blend, gauge, and care limits. Milk protein yarn is not as strong when wet as cotton or plant-fiber yarn, so blends and gentle care are part of the specification.

Materials

  • Milk protein staple fiber
  • Plant fiber for blending (cotton or plant bast fiber)
  • Spinning wheel or spindle
  • Knitting needles or machine
  • Dye (plant or mineral, as preferred)
  • Scale
  • Yarn gauge

Method

  1. Blend the milk protein staple with plant fiber in the stated ratio and card the blend until even.
  2. Spin the blend to the stated tex and twist, checking the gauge as you go.
  3. Wind the yarn into skeins, dye if wanted, and dry the skeins without stretching.
  4. Knit the fabric at the stated gauge and finish it by gentle washing and flat drying.
  5. Label the fabric with its blend ratio and wash care, since wet strength is lower than that of pure plant-fiber fabric.

Safety

Casein dust note: handle dry casein powder with care, avoid breathing the dust, and work in a ventilated room with gloves. Casein plastics, films, and binders are water-sensitive: they soften and weaken in prolonged damp, so keep finished pieces dry. These products contain milk protein; people with milk allergy should avoid handling the dry powder and avoid skin-contact products made from it. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and mineral pigments are used instead of animal-fat binders. Milk protein fabric loses strength when wet: wash gently in cool water and dry flat, never wrung or tumble-dried hot. Keep dye baths out of food vessels.

Yield

Variations

  • Fine yarn for lightweight knits.

Casein Paper Sizing and Coating compounded · coatings · intermediate

Casein paper sizing and coating is a surface treatment for paper and board: a thin casein solution is applied to the sheet to reduce absorbency, improve printability, and give a smooth, ink-holding surface. It is graded by coat weight in grams per square metre and by finish, from matte sizing to gloss coating. This record specifies the coating grades and their application. Coated paper from this process is a paper product, not food packaging, unless food-grade materials are used throughout.

Materials

  • Casein solution, graded
  • Paper or board stock
  • Coating blade or roller
  • Drying rack or tunnel
  • Calender or smooth roller
  • Scale
  • Coat-weight gauge

Method

Safety

Casein Binder for Pigments and Gesso compounded · coatings · intermediate

Casein binder for pigments and gesso is an artist material: casein dissolved in a mild alkali solution binds dry pigments into a tempera-like paint, and mixed with chalk it makes a gesso ground for panels. The binder dries quickly to a matte, water-resistant film that can be varnished. This record specifies the binder grades and the gesso formulation. It is a materials record for artists; the paint is not food and its vessels are never used for food afterward.

Materials

  • Casein powder
  • Mild alkali solution for dissolving (handled with care)
  • Dry mineral pigments
  • Chalk (calcium carbonate) for gesso
  • Wood panels
  • Brushes kept for art use
  • Scale

Method

  1. Dissolve casein in the alkali solution until clear, and strain the binder.
  2. For paint, grind dry pigment into the binder a little at a time until the color is smooth.
  3. For gesso, stir chalk into the binder until it is the consistency of thick cream, then brush it onto a sealed panel in thin coats.
  4. Sand each gesso coat lightly when dry and repeat for a smooth ground.
  5. Varnish finished paintings to protect the matte surface, and label art vessels as art-only.

Safety
