What Ancient DNA Is Rank 1 · simple

Ancient DNA is genetic material recovered from archaeological remains, such as bones, teeth, and preserved bodies, and studied to learn about past populations, their movements, and their relationships. It is published research data: a study reports its samples, its methods, and its results in a journal, and other researchers can check the work against the published data. This pair covers only that published ancient research, together with aggregated population results, and it never touches modern individual genomes or private test results. Ancient DNA is powerful for population history and weak for individual biography, and that asymmetry runs through every record in this pair.

Otzi the Iceman Rank 2 · simple

Otzi the Iceman is a naturally preserved body found in the Otztal Alps in 1991, dated to about 3300 BCE, and his genome was published in 2012. The published genome places him among early European farming populations, with ancestry related to the farming groups that spread from Anatolia, and later studies have refined that placement as methods improved. Otzi is a single ancient individual whose remains were preserved by ice, and his genome is cited here as published research data, not as a personal record: he lived more than five thousand years ago, and no living person's information is involved. His case also shows how a single genome is interpreted against population data, never alone.

Cheddar Man Rank 3 · simple

Cheddar Man is a Mesolithic skeleton from Cheddar Gorge in England, about ten thousand years old, whose genome was published in 2018. The published result placed him among western European hunter-gatherers, the population that lived in Britain before farming arrived, and later work has used his genome as a reference for that population. Cheddar Man is cited here by the published name for the skeleton, as de-identified archaeological research data, and the record draws no conclusion about any living person from it. His case is a reminder that a Mesolithic genome describes a population of its time, not a modern population, and that populations change through migration and mixture.

The Ancient One of Kennewick Rank 4 · simple

The Ancient One of Kennewick, also called Kennewick Man in the early literature, is a skeleton found near the Columbia River in 1996, about eight to nine thousand years old, whose genome was published in 2015. The published result showed continuity with later Native American populations of the region, a finding reported in the study itself. The remains were reburied in 2017 after a long legal and cultural process, and this record states that reburial factually and respectfully, without detail beyond the published record. The case is included because it is published research with a clear ethical lesson: ancient remains belong to a history that includes descendant communities, and research on them carries obligations that outlast publication.

Denisovans of Denisova Cave Rank 5 · simple

Denisovans are an ancient human group known first from a finger bone found in Denisova Cave in Siberia in 2008, and identified by DNA rather than by distinctive bones. The published sample identifier for that finger bone is Denisova 3, and its genome, published in 2010, revealed a group distinct from both Neanderthals and modern humans. Later work found Denisovan ancestry in present-day populations of Oceania and parts of Asia, reported as aggregate population ancestry, not as individual findings. Denisovans are known almost entirely through DNA, which makes them the clearest case in this pair of a population discovered by genetics and then traced through archaeology.

Neanderthal Genomes Rank 6 · simple

Neanderthal genomes were first published as a draft in 2010, from bones found in Europe and western Asia, and a high-coverage genome followed in 2014. The published results showed that Neanderthals were a distinct ancient population, and that present-day populations outside Africa carry a small share of Neanderthal-related ancestry as an aggregate population finding. Neanderthal research also showed the limits of early methods: contamination, short fragments, and chemical damage all had to be controlled before a genome could be trusted. The Neanderthal record in this pair is a population history, reported in aggregate terms, and it contains no individual modern genome and no finding about any living person.

Yamnaya and Steppe Ancestry Rank 7 · compounded

Yamnaya and steppe ancestry is the finding, published in 2015 by Haak and colleagues and by Allentoft and colleagues, that a large-scale migration from the Eurasian steppe in the third millennium BCE contributed substantially to later European populations. The studies compared genomes from Yamnaya burials with earlier and later European genomes, and reported a sharp ancestry change in central Europe around that time. The finding is about population movement at scale, not about any individual migrant, and it is debated in its details: the size, timing, and cultural correlates of the movement are all still discussed. This record reports the published finding and the debate together, because a debated finding reported as settled would mislead.

Early European Farmers Rank 8 · compounded

Early European farmers are the populations that carried farming from Anatolia into Europe beginning about nine thousand years ago, and their ancestry is traced through genomes from early farming sites across the continent. Published studies show a gradient: farming ancestry is strongest in the south and east of Europe and weaker in the far north and west, where hunter-gatherer ancestry persisted longer. The spread of farming involved both movement of people and adoption of farming by local groups, in proportions that varied by region, and ancient DNA is the evidence that separates those two processes. This record reports the gradient as an aggregate population pattern, with regional variation stated, not smoothed away.

Ancient Egypt Mummies Rank 9 · compounded

Ancient Egypt mummies were studied in a genome project published in 2017, which recovered genetic data from mummies spanning more than a thousand years of Egyptian history. The published results showed continuity across that span, with ancestry related to Near Eastern and Anatolian populations, and the study was notable because hot climates usually destroy ancient DNA, so recovery itself was a technical achievement. About ninety mummies yielded mitochondrial data in the study, reported here as an aggregate study size, not as individual findings. The Egyptian case shows both the reach and the limit of ancient DNA: it can trace a population across a millennium, but it cannot reconstruct any individual's life.

Viking Age Genomes Rank 10 · compounded

Viking Age genomes were published in 2020 by Margaryan and colleagues, from more than four hundred individuals across the Viking world, from Scandinavia to the North Atlantic and beyond. The published results showed that Viking Age Scandinavia was genetically diverse, with ancestry from across Europe and western Asia, and that the Viking phenomenon involved movement in many directions, not a single outward wave. The study is reported here as an aggregate population finding, with its large sample size stated because it is what makes the diversity claim possible. No individual Viking genome is reported here, and no finding is drawn about any living person from this study.

Ancient DNA and Language Spread Rank 11 · compounded

Ancient DNA and language spread is the question of whether a population movement carried a language with it, as proposed for the steppe migration and the Indo-European languages. Ancient DNA can show that a population moved, and linguistics can show that languages are related, but the two together do not prove that the movers spoke the reconstructed language: languages can spread without large migration, and migrations can occur without language replacement. The steppe case is the most discussed example, and it remains debated precisely because the genetic and linguistic evidence answer different questions. This record keeps those questions separate, and it labels any combined claim as interpretation, not as a finding of either field alone.

Contamination Control and Authentication Rank 12 · complex

Contamination control and authentication are the methods that make ancient DNA trustworthy: clean laboratories, protective clothing, blank controls, damage-pattern checks, and replication in a second laboratory. Ancient samples carry modern DNA from excavators, curators, and laboratory workers, and a result that does not control for that contamination is not a result. Authentication checks for the chemical damage pattern of old DNA, which modern contamination lacks, and a study that passes those checks can be trusted further than one that does not. This record is method, not finding, and it is included because a reader who cannot judge authentication cannot judge any ancient-DNA claim in this pair.

Sample IDs and Published Data Rank 13 · complex

Sample IDs and published data are how ancient-DNA research stays checkable: each sample is cited by the identifier given in its study, such as Denisova 3 for the Denisovan finger bone, and the published data carry that identifier so that later work can re-analyze the same sample. Identifiers are de-identified research labels, not personal records: they point to an archaeological sample, not to a living person, and they carry no modern personal information. This pair cites sample identifiers only as published, and where a study's identifier is not stated in the sources used here, the record says so rather than inventing one. The rule is simple: cite the published identifier, or cite nothing.

What Ancient DNA Cannot Prove Rank 14 · complex

What ancient DNA cannot prove is as important as what it can: it cannot prove an individual's biography, a family's pedigree, a language spoken by a specific person, or any finding about a living person. A single ancient genome cannot stand for a whole population, a shared ancestry share cannot name a migration's cause, and a genetic similarity cannot prove cultural identity. Ancient DNA is strongest at population scale, across many samples and many sites, and weakest at the individual scale, where archaeology, dating, and context must carry the interpretation. Any claim in this pair that goes beyond population history is labeled as interpretation, and any claim about a living person is absent, by rule.

Ethics: Consent, Descendant Communities, Repatriation Rank 15 · complex

Ethics for ancient DNA covers consent, descendant communities, and repatriation: remains were not donated by their owners, descendant communities may hold obligations toward them, and reburial may follow study, as in the Kennewick case. Published guidance now calls for consultation with descendant and local communities before sampling, for clear agreements about data use, and for respect toward remains as ancestors, not only as specimens. This record states those obligations as published guidance, not as this database's own policy invention, and it notes that older studies did not always meet today's standards, which is a reason to read them with their date in mind. Ethics is part of the evidence, not an appendix to it.

Future waves should add only newly published studies, cited by study and sample identifier, and say which study filled each gap.
