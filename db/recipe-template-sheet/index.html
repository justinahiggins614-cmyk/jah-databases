Basic Bread Loaf Template Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established The plain tin-loaf method and the baker's percentage ratios are standard, long-settled baking practice taught in general baking references.

A reusable sheet for a plain yeasted bread loaf: flour, water, yeast, and salt, mixed, risen, shaped, and baked in a tin.

A loaf of bread is the simplest yeasted bake, and the template exists so any cook can write the same loaf twice and get the same result. The sheet fixes the flour-to-water ratio, the yeast and salt amounts, the rise times, and the bake, so the only choices left are the flour and the shape. Without a template, a cook guesses the water, the dough is too stiff or too slack, the rise is mistimed, and the loaf comes out dense or pale. With the sheet, the loaf is repeatable: the same weights, the same steps, the same tin, and a record of what changed when the cook varies it.

History

Bread made from flour, water, yeast, and salt is one of the oldest prepared foods, baked in some form for thousands of years wherever grain was grown. The tin loaf, baked in a rectangular pan so it slices evenly for sandwiches, became a household standard with the spread of the enclosed oven and the baking tin. The template records that settled practice: a single rise, a shaped loaf, and a firm crust.

Method

Weigh the flour, water, yeast, and salt; never measure flour by volume alone.; Mix and knead until the dough is smooth and springs back when pressed.; Let the dough rise until doubled, then shape it into the tin.; Let it rise again until it crowns the tin, then bake until the crust is deep gold.; Cool the loaf on a rack before slicing so the crumb sets..

Template

Servings: 8; prep time: 20 minutes; cook time: 35 minutes.

Ingredients

  • plain flour: 500 g
  • water: 325 g
  • dried yeast: 7 g
  • salt: 10 g
  • sugar: 10 g

Steps

  1. Mix the flour, water, yeast, salt, and sugar into a rough dough.
  2. Knead on a clean surface until smooth and elastic.
  3. Cover and leave to rise until doubled in size.
  4. Shape the dough and place it seam-down in a greased loaf tin.
  5. Leave to rise again until the dough crowns the rim of the tin.
  6. Bake until deep gold, then turn out and cool on a rack.

Equipment

  • large mixing bowl
  • loaf tin
  • clean work surface
  • tea towel
  • cooling rack
  • oven

Worked example

Worked example, an invented example dish written fresh for this template: Simple White Sandwich Loaf. For 8 slices, mix 500 g plain flour with 325 g water, 7 g dried yeast, 10 g salt, and 10 g sugar. Knead for about ten minutes until smooth, cover, and let rise until doubled. Shape into a loaf tin, let rise again until the dough crowns the rim, and bake in a hot oven until the crust is deep gold and the base sounds hollow when tapped. Cool fully on a rack before slicing. The example is invented as a plain teaching loaf, not a famous named recipe.

Variations

Swap half the white flour for wholemeal flour and add a little more water; add seeds on top before baking; shape the same dough into rolls instead of a tin loaf and shorten the bake.

Storage

Cool fully, then keep in a bread bag or wrapped in a clean cloth at room temperature for a day or two; slice and freeze any loaf that will not be eaten promptly, and thaw slices as needed.

Scaling rules

Scale every ingredient by the serving ratio, including the yeast and the salt. Do not scale the rise time: a doubled batch rises in about the same time, while the bake lengthens a little. Split a large batch into two tins rather than one oversized loaf.

Science proof

Bread rises because yeast ferments sugars in the flour and releases gas, which the gluten network traps. The flour-to-water ratio decides the crumb: more water gives a softer, more open crumb, while too little water gives a tight, dry loaf. Salt slows the yeast slightly and strengthens the gluten, so it is weighed, not guessed. Baking sets the crumb and browns the crust; a larger loaf needs more time because heat travels inward from the surface. import hashlib
import json

def scale(ingredients, factor):
    return [[name, round(qty * factor, 2), unit] for name, qty, unit in ingredients]

title = 'Basic Bread Loaf Template'
base_servings = 8
target_servings = 16
ingredients = [['plain flour', 500.0, 'g'], ['water', 325.0, 'g'], ['dried yeast', 7.0, 'g'], ['salt', 10.0, 'g'], ['sugar', 10.0, 'g']]
factor = target_servings / base_servings
scaled_a = scale(ingredients, factor)
scaled_b = scale(ingredients, factor)
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(scaled_a, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(scaled_b, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Template: " + title)
print("Base servings: %d  Target servings: %d  Scale factor: %s" % (base_servings, target_servings, factor))
for name, qty, unit in scaled_a:
    print("  " + name + ": " + ("%g" % qty) + " " + unit)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:
    print("Deterministic: MATCH - both runs produced the same scaled quantities.")
else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")
print("Note: this demo scales the template's own ingredient quantities by the serving ratio; it is arithmetic on the template, not a tested kitchen result.")

Weeknight Soup Template Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: established The soften, simmer, season order and the vegetable-to-stock ratio are standard, long-settled soup practice in general cookbooks.

A reusable sheet for a quick blended or chunky soup: vegetables, stock, seasoning, and a finish, cooked in one pot.

A weeknight soup has to be fast, forgiving, and repeatable, and the template exists to hold those three qualities together. The sheet fixes the ratio of vegetables to stock, the order in which things go into the pot, and the point at which the soup is seasoned, so the cook is never guessing how much liquid a pot of vegetables needs. Without a template, a soup is either too thin and tastes of water or too thick and sticks to the pot. With the sheet, the same pot, the same weights, and the same simmer give the same soup, and the cook can swap the main vegetable without relearning the method.

History

Soup is one of the oldest cooked dishes, a pot of water, vegetables, and whatever else was at hand, simmered until tender. The weeknight version, cooked in under an hour from a small set of ingredients, became the standard home pattern when the stock cube and the blender made a smooth soup quick to finish. The template records that settled pattern: soften, simmer, season, and serve.

Method

Soften the onion and garlic in oil until sweet, without browning.; Add the main vegetables and the stock, and bring to a gentle simmer.; Simmer until the vegetables are fully tender.; Blend smooth or leave chunky, as the template states.; Season at the end and finish with herbs or a swirl of oil..

Template

Servings: 4; prep time: 10 minutes; cook time: 30 minutes.

Ingredients

  • tomatoes: 800 g
  • onion: 150 g
  • garlic: 12 g
  • vegetable stock: 750 ml
  • olive oil: 30 ml
  • fresh basil: 10 g

Steps

  1. Warm the oil and soften the onion and garlic.
  2. Add the tomatoes and the stock.
  3. Bring to a gentle simmer and cook until the vegetables are tender.
  4. Blend smooth or leave chunky.
  5. Season, add the basil, and serve hot.

Equipment

  • large pot
  • wooden spoon
  • blender or hand blender
  • chopping board
  • knife
  • ladle

Worked example

Worked example, a widely made standard dish described in the builder's own words: Tomato Basil Soup. For 4 bowls, soften 150 g onion and 12 g garlic in 30 ml olive oil, add 800 g chopped tomatoes and 750 ml stock, and simmer gently for about 25 minutes. Blend smooth, season, and stir in 10 g torn basil. Serve hot. Tomato soup with basil is a standard dish found in general cookbooks everywhere; this wording is fresh, not copied.

Variations

Use roasted vegetables instead of raw for a deeper flavour; swap the basil for parsley or thyme; stir in cream or a spoon of yoghurt at the end; leave the soup chunky and add cooked beans or grains.

Storage

Cool quickly, refrigerate promptly, and use within a few days; freeze in portions for longer keeping, and reheat until hot all the way through before serving.

Scaling rules

Scale the vegetables, stock, and oil by the serving ratio. Seasoning scales too, but taste and adjust at the end: salt is easier to add than to remove. A larger pot needs a little longer to reach a simmer, but the simmer time itself barely changes.

Green Salad and Vinaigrette Template Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established The three-to-one vinaigrette ratio and the dress-at-the-last-moment rule are standard, long-settled salad practice in general cookbooks.

A reusable sheet for a green salad with a simple vinaigrette: leaves, a crunchy vegetable, and a shaken oil-and-vinegar dressing.

A green salad is the most common cold dish in the kitchen, and the template exists because salads fail in predictable ways: too much dressing, soggy leaves, or a dressing that splits. The sheet fixes the oil-to-vinegar ratio, the amount of dressing per weight of leaves, and the order of dressing, so the salad is crisp and evenly coated every time. Without a template, the dressing is poured by eye and the leaves wilt under it. With the sheet, the dressing is shaken in a jar, measured, and tossed at the last moment.

History

Salads of raw leaves dressed with oil and vinegar are an old pattern, recorded in European cooking for centuries, and the three-to-one oil to vinegar vinaigrette is the classic ratio taught in general cookbooks. The modern green salad, with mixed leaves and a jar-shaken dressing, became a household standard in the twentieth century. The template records that settled ratio and the last-minute toss.

Method

Wash and dry the leaves thoroughly so the dressing clings.; Shake the oil, vinegar, mustard, and salt into a dressing.; Cut the crunchy vegetables and keep them separate until serving.; Toss the leaves with just enough dressing to coat them lightly.; Serve at once, before the leaves wilt..

Template

Servings: 4; prep time: 15 minutes; cook time: 0 minutes.

Ingredients

  • mixed salad leaves: 200 g
  • cucumber: 150 g
  • tomatoes: 200 g
  • olive oil: 60 ml
  • vinegar: 20 ml
  • mustard: 5 g
  • salt: 3 g

Steps

  1. Wash and dry the leaves and vegetables.
  2. Shake the oil, vinegar, mustard, and salt into a dressing.
  3. Combine the leaves, cucumber, and tomatoes in a wide bowl.
  4. Toss with just enough dressing to coat lightly.
  5. Serve at once.

Equipment

  • salad bowl
  • small jar with a lid
  • chopping board
  • knife
  • salad spinner or clean tea towel

Worked example

Worked example, an invented example dish written fresh for this template: Mixed Green Salad with Lemon Vinaigrette. For 4 servings, toss 200 g mixed leaves with 150 g sliced cucumber and 200 g halved tomatoes. Shake 60 ml olive oil with 20 ml vinegar, 5 g mustard, and 3 g salt, and toss the salad with just enough dressing to coat the leaves lightly. Serve at once. The example is invented as a plain teaching salad, not a famous named recipe.

Variations

Swap the vinegar for lemon juice; add toasted seeds or nuts; add cheese or cooked beans for a fuller salad; use a different leaf mix or add shredded carrot and radish.

Storage

Keep undressed leaves and vegetables refrigerated and dry, and keep the dressing in a sealed jar at room temperature for the day. A dressed salad does not keep: dress only what will be eaten at once.

Scaling rules

Scale the leaves, vegetables, and dressing by the serving ratio, and keep the oil-to-vinegar ratio fixed at three to one. For a very small salad, make the full dressing and keep the extra in a jar; halving a tiny amount of mustard is unreliable. No cooking time scales, because there is no cooking.

Roast Chicken Template Rank 4 · simple

Evidence: established Seasoning inside and out, roasting in a hot oven, resting before carving, and checking the thigh are standard, long-settled roast-chicken practice.

A reusable sheet for a whole roast chicken: a seasoned bird, roasted until the skin is crisp and the meat is cooked through.

A roast chicken is the centrepiece of many home meals, and the template exists because the two common failures are dry meat and pale, soft skin. The sheet fixes the seasoning, the oven heat, the resting time, and the check for doneness, so the same bird roasted the same way gives the same result. Without a template, the cook guesses the time and either cuts into an undercooked bird or leaves it in until it dries out.
