Evidence: established Hydrogen association is established textbook physical chemistry, explained by quantum mechanics since 1927.

Two hydrogen atoms meet, share electrons, and settle as an H2 molecule: the shortest sequence in chemistry.

Hydrogen association is the simplest atom sequence there is: two hydrogen atoms approach each other, their electron clouds overlap, the two electrons pair into a shared orbital, and the excess energy is carried away by a third body or a photon, leaving a bound H2 molecule. Every step is stable: the approach costs nothing, the pairing releases 436 kilojoules per mole, and the energy carried away leaves the molecule in its ground state. There is no step in which anything breaks or decays, so the whole sequence is stable. The sequence runs in reverse only if 436 kilojoules per mole is supplied from outside.

In a hydrogen discharge tube, hydrogen atoms made by the discharge drift through the gas and meet. Most meetings end with the two atoms flying apart again, because there is nothing to carry away the bond energy. When a third atom or the wall takes the energy, the pair sticks, and an H2 molecule leaves the scene. The sequence is short and every step is stable, so the product accumulates.

History

The association of hydrogen atoms was one of the first reactions explained by quantum mechanics, in the Heitler-London treatment of 1927. The need for a third body to carry away the bond energy was recognised in early studies of gas-phase recombination.

Method

Bring two hydrogen atoms together in the gas phase.; Let their electron clouds overlap and pair.; Carry away the released bond energy with a third body.; Confirm the H2 molecule by its spectrum..

Evidence: established Water formation by combustion is established textbook chemistry, demonstrated since the 1780s.

Hydrogen and oxygen gases combine into water, releasing heat: the classic combustion sequence.

Water formation runs in three movements: first the H-H and O=O bonds must break, which costs energy and needs a spark; then the freed atoms rearrange into O-H bonds, which releases far more energy than the breaking cost; then the heat escapes and the water settles as a liquid. The first movement is conditionally stable, because the mixture waits indefinitely until a spark supplies the activation energy, and the later movements are stable, because forming O-H bonds is downhill all the way. The whole sequence is therefore conditionally stable: it runs only when ignited, and the condition is the spark.

A balloon filled with a hydrogen-oxygen mixture hangs in a laboratory, doing nothing, for as long as it is left alone. A spark from a lighter sets it off with a pop, and the balloon is gone, replaced by a fine mist of water. The mixture was conditionally stable all along: stable until ignited, and then the sequence ran to completion in a fraction of a second.

History

Cavendish showed in the 1780s that burning hydrogen in air makes water, and the sequence of bond breaking and bond forming was worked out as chemical kinetics developed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. The reaction releases 572 kilojoules per two moles of water formed.

Method

Mix hydrogen and oxygen gases in the ratio two to one.; Supply a spark to break H-H and O=O bonds.; Let the freed atoms rearrange into O-H bonds.; Carry away the released heat and condense the water..

Evidence: established Salt formation is established textbook chemistry, demonstrated since the nineteenth century.

Sodium metal and chlorine gas combine into table salt: electron transfer followed by a crystal lattice.

The first movement is conditionally stable, because the electron transfer happens only when the two atoms meet with enough energy, and the later movements are stable, because forming the lattice is downhill all the way. The whole sequence is therefore conditionally stable: it runs on contact, and the condition is the meeting.

A pea-sized piece of sodium dropped into a flask of chlorine gas bursts into a yellow flame and coats the flask with white crystals. The sodium gives up its electron, the chlorine takes it, and the ions fall into a lattice, releasing heat and light. The white coating is table salt, made in seconds from two dangerous elements by a sequence that needed only the meeting to start.

History

The reaction of sodium with chlorine was demonstrated in the nineteenth century, and the Born-Haber cycle that accounts for its energy was worked out in 1919. The lattice energy of 787 kJ/mol is a standard tabulated value.

Method
