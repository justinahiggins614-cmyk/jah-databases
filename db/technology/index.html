JAH-TECH-000001 — Transistor

Overview. The transistor is a semiconductor switch and amplifier: a small voltage on one terminal controls a much larger current between two others. It replaced the vacuum tube and became the fundamental building block of all modern electronics -- every processor, memory chip, phone, and computer is an assembly of transistors.

History. Invented in December 1947 at Bell Telephone Laboratories by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley, who demonstrated the first point-contact transistor. The three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics. The junction transistor followed, and in 1959 Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng at Bell Labs invented the MOSFET, the type used in nearly all chips today.

How it works. In an n-channel MOSFET, a voltage applied to the gate terminal creates an electric field that opens a conducting channel between source and drain in the silicon. Below the threshold voltage the channel is closed (off, no current); above it the channel conducts, and the drain current grows roughly with the square of the gate overdrive voltage. The demo below models this square-law behavior and the resulting voltage gain, then shows the CMOS inverter transfer curve -- the basis of digital logic.

Key specifications. A single modern transistor switches in picoseconds and measures a few nanometers across; flagship chips pack tens of billions of them. The demo's model uses a transconductance parameter K = 2 mA/V^2 and threshold Vth = 1 V, representative of a small discrete MOSFET.

Impact. The transistor made the integrated circuit possible and therefore the entire digital age: radios, computers, the internet, smartphones, and AI hardware all rest on it.

import math
VDD, VTH, K = 5.0, 1.0, 2e-3   # supply, threshold, transconductance parameter
def ids(vgs, vds):
    """Square-law NMOS drain current (triode / saturation)."""
    if vgs <= VTH:
        return 0.0
    vdsat = vgs - VTH
    if vds >= vdsat:
        return K * vdsat ** 2
    return K * (2 * vdsat * vds - vds * vds)

print("Transistor demo: NMOS square-law model, K=%.4f A/V^2, Vth=%.1f V" % (K, VTH))
print("Gate voltage -> drain current (Vds = 5 V, saturation):")

JAH-TECH-000002 — Integrated Circuit

Overview. An integrated circuit (chip) is a complete electronic circuit -- transistors, resistors, capacitors, and their interconnections -- fabricated as one piece on a small slab of semiconductor. It collapsed room-sized electronics into millimeter-scale packages.

History. Jack Kilby of Texas Instruments built the first integrated circuit in 1958; Robert Noyce of Fairchild Semiconductor independently developed the planar process version in 1959 that made mass production practical. Kilby received the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physics. Intel's 4004 (1971) put a full CPU on one chip with 2,300 transistors.

How it works. Chips are made by photolithography: light projected through masks patterns microscopic layers of doped silicon, insulators, and metal wiring on a wafer, repeated dozens of times to build transistors and connect them. The demo below fits the historical transistor counts to Moore's observation -- counts doubling roughly every two years -- and projects the trend.

Key specifications. Landmark counts: Intel 4004 (1971) 2,300; Intel 8086 (1978) 29,000; Intel 80386 (1985) 275,000; Pentium (1993) 3.1 million; Pentium 4 (2000) 42 million; Core 2 Duo (2006) 291 million; Apple M1 (2020) 16 billion; Apple M2 (2022) 20 billion.

Impact. The IC made computing cheap, small, and reliable enough to put in everything from calculators to spacecraft, driving the digital revolution.

JAH-TECH-000003 — World Wide Web

Overview. The World Wide Web is the system of interlinked hypertext documents and resources accessed over the internet via URLs, the HTTP protocol, and HTML pages rendered by browsers. It turned the internet from a research network into the world's information platform.

History. Proposed by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN in 1989 and implemented in 1990; the first website (info.cern.ch) went up in 1991. Berners-Lee gave the core technologies away royalty-free, and CERN's 1993 declaration made the Web free for everyone, triggering explosive growth.

How it works. A browser resolves a URL's domain to an IP address, opens a connection, and sends an HTTP request; the server returns HTML, which the browser parses and renders, following hyperlinks to other pages. Search engines rank pages by analyzing the link graph -- the demo below runs the PageRank iteration on a small early-web link graph, the core idea behind web search ranking.

Key specifications. HTTP/1.0 (1996), HTTP/1.1 (1997), HTTP/2 (2015), HTTP/3 (2022); HTML standardized by the W3C; billions of indexed pages today versus a few dozen sites in 1992.

Impact. The Web reorganized commerce, media, science, and social life around instant global publishing and retrieval of information.

JAH-TECH-000004 — Relational Database

Overview. A relational database stores data as tables of rows and columns linked by keys, and queries it with a declarative language (SQL) based on relational algebra. It is the standard way the world keeps structured records -- banking, airlines, hospitals, and governments all run on it.

History. Edgar F. Codd of IBM published "A Relational Model of Data for Large Shared Data Banks" in 1970, giving the mathematical foundation. IBM's System R (1970s) proved it practical and produced SQL (originally SEQUEL, 1974); Oracle shipped the first commercial SQL database in 1979.

How it works. Data lives in relations (tables); each row is a tuple, each column an attribute. Queries use selection (filter rows), projection (pick columns), joins (combine tables on matching keys), and aggregation (counts, sums, averages). The demo below implements a miniature relational engine in pure Python -- tables, selection, an equi-join, and a group-by count -- over inventors and inventions.

Key specifications. SQL standardized as SQL-86 (1986) with major revisions since; ACID transactions (atomicity, consistency, isolation, durability) guarantee reliable updates; modern systems scale to trillions of rows.

Impact. Codd's model made data independent of the programs that use it, enabling the entire information economy built on queryable shared records.

JAH-TECH-000005 — Public-Key Cryptography

Overview. Public-key cryptography lets two parties communicate securely without sharing a secret in advance: each party has a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption. It underpins secure web browsing (TLS), email, digital signatures, and cryptocurrencies.

History. Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published the key-exchange concept in 1976 ("New Directions in Cryptography"). Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir, and Leonard Adleman described the RSA system in 1977 (published 1978). Classified GCHQ work by James Ellis and Clifford Cocks in the early 1970s anticipated both, and was declassified in 1997.

How it works. RSA rests on the difficulty of factoring large numbers: the public key (n = p*q, e) encrypts via c = m^e mod n, and only the holder of the private exponent d (with e*d = 1 mod (p-1)(q-1)) can recover m = c^d mod n. Diffie-Hellman lets two parties derive a shared secret over a public channel using modular exponentiation. The demo runs both with small textbook numbers.

Key specifications. Real deployments use 2048- to 4096-bit RSA keys (617-1233 decimal digits); the demo uses tiny primes (61, 53) purely to show the arithmetic, which is identical in structure to production use.

Impact. Public-key cryptography made e-commerce, secure messaging, and trusted software distribution possible on an open, untrusted network.

JAH-TECH-000006 — Graphics Processing Unit

Overview. A graphics processing unit (GPU) is a processor built for massive parallelism: thousands of simple cores executing the same operation on different data simultaneously. Designed for rendering images, the same architecture turned out to be ideal for scientific computing and training neural networks.

History. NVIDIA coined the term "GPU" with the GeForce 256 in 1999, billed as the first chip with hardware transform and lighting. Programmable shaders followed, and NVIDIA's CUDA platform (2007) opened GPUs to general-purpose computing (GPGPU), which later powered the deep-learning boom.

How it works. GPUs use a SIMT model: groups of threads run one instruction stream over wide vectors of data, hiding memory latency by swapping thread groups. Performance follows from core count times clock rate times operations per clock (often 2 via fused multiply-add). The demo applies Amdahl's law to show why parallelism wins, and computes throughput from core specs.

Key specifications. A flagship-class example: ~16,000+ shader cores at ~2 GHz with fused multiply-add delivers tens of teraflops (trillions of operations per second) in single precision; memory bandwidth reaches multiple terabytes per second on wide high-bandwidth memory buses.

Impact. GPUs made real-time 3D graphics, modern video games, large-scale simulation, and the training of today's AI models economically feasible.

JAH-TECH-000007 — Steam Engine

Overview. The steam engine converts heat into mechanical work by boiling water into steam and using its pressure to drive a piston or turbine. It was the first practical engine that freed industry from wind, water, and muscle, launching the Industrial Revolution.

History. Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine of 1712 pumped water from mines. James Watt's separate condenser -- patented in 1769 -- cut fuel waste dramatically and made steam power economical for factories, mills, and transport.

How it works. Heat boils water; the expanding steam pushes a piston (or spins turbine blades); the spent steam is condensed, and the cycle repeats. No heat engine can beat the Carnot limit set by its hot and cold temperatures: efficiency at most 1 - T_cold/T_hot. The demo computes Carnot limits for historical engines and the coal each needed per kilowatt-hour.

Key specifications. Newcomen engines ran near 0.5% thermal efficiency; Watt's improved engines a few percent; modern supercritical steam power stations exceed 40% thermal efficiency, approaching half the Carnot limit at their temperatures.

Impact. Steam power mechanized mining, textiles, railways, and shipping, reshaping labor, cities, and the global economy within a century.

JAH-TECH-000008 — Photovoltaic Cell

Overview. A photovoltaic (PV) cell converts sunlight directly into electricity with no moving parts: photons knock electrons loose in a semiconductor junction, and the junction's built-in field sweeps them into a current. Linked into panels and arrays, PV cells are now one of the cheapest sources of electricity on Earth.

History. Alexandre-Edmond Becquerel observed the photovoltaic effect in 1839. The first practical silicon solar cell was built at Bell Labs in 1954 by Daryl Chapin, Calvin Fuller, and Gerald Pearson, at about 6% efficiency.

How it works. Sunlight absorbed in silicon creates electron-hole pairs; the p-n junction separates them, producing current at a voltage set by the junction (around 0.6 V per cell). Power equals irradiance times area times efficiency. The demo computes panel output and traces a cell's current-voltage curve from the diode equation.

Key specifications. Commercial silicon panels now exceed 20-22% efficiency; laboratory cells top 26%, approaching the ~33.7% Shockley-Queisser limit for a single junction. A 1 m^2 panel in full sun (1000 W/m^2) yields roughly 200-230 W.

Impact. Solar PV went from powering satellites to powering grids, and now supplies a large and growing share of new electricity generation worldwide.

JAH-TECH-000045 — Haber-Bosch Process

Overview. The Haber-Bosch process synthesizes ammonia from nitrogen and hydrogen under heat, pressure, and catalysis: N2 + 3H2 <-> 2NH3. Ammonia becomes fertilizer, and fertilizer feeds roughly half of humanity.

History. Fritz Haber demonstrated the synthesis in 1909; Carl Bosch scaled it to industry at BASF's Oppau plant in 1913. Haber received the 1918 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

How it works. Breaking nitrogen's triple bond needs high temperature, but the reaction is exothermic, so heat destroys the equilibrium yield -- the classic compromise runs ~450-500 C at 150-250 atm over an iron catalyst, recycling unreacted gases. The demo applies van't Hoff's equation to show the equilibrium constant collapsing with temperature, which is why pressure and recycling are essential.

Key specifications. Modern plants use ~30 GJ per tonne of NH3 (~1-2% of world energy); roughly half the nitrogen atoms in human bodies passed through this process (Smil's estimate).

Impact. Haber-Bosch ended the guano-and-manure ceiling on food production, enabling the population growth of the 20th century.
