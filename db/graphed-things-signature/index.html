Minard's Map of Napoleon's Russian Campaign Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: design-study Design study: this Signature record designs how the Signature archive would present, animate, and teach Minard's map, and it presents no new historical claim.

Charles Joseph Minard's 1869 flow map of Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign, the most famous statistical graphic ever drawn: one picture that shows an army of 422,000 men marching to Moscow and about 10,000 staggering home, with the temperature falling as the line thins.

Minard's map is a single sheet that tells the whole disaster of 1812 without a paragraph of text. A thick beige band leaves the Niemen river in June 1812, 422,000 men wide, and narrows as it advances through Vilna, Vitebsk, Smolensk and on to Moscow, where only about 100,000 men arrive in September. Then the band turns black and comes back, narrowing at every river crossing, until about 10,000 survivors recross the Niemen in December. Below the retreat runs a second graphic, a line chart of the temperature falling day by day to thirty degrees below zero on the chart's scale, so the reader sees the cold arrive exactly as the black band thins. The width of the band is proportional to the number of men alive, which means the eye measures the loss directly: the map is a bar chart that moves across a map. Edward Tufte, the statistician who republished the map in his 1983 book The Visual Display of Quantitative Information, called it the best statistical graphic ever drawn, and it has been reprinted in nearly every book on data graphics since. What the map proved was not a battle but a logistics failure: no single defeat destroyed the Grande Armée, and the picture shows the army bleeding away at every stage, on the march in as well as on the retreat. Its data came from the published histories of the campaign, above all Thiers and Chambray, compiled by Minard into a table of army sizes at each named place and a table of temperatures. The map is also a warning about reading graphs: it is a compilation from secondary sources, not a muster roll, and its numbers are Minard's best estimates, rounded and dated to the places where the army was counted.

History

Charles Joseph Minard (1781-1870) was a French civil engineer who spent his career building canals, harbours and railways and his retirement drawing flow maps of trade, migration and war. He published the Russian campaign map in 1869, at the age of eighty-eight, as the Carte figurative des pertes successives en hommes de l'Armée Française dans la campagne de Russie 1812-1813, lithographed in Paris by Regnier and Dourdet. It was one of a series of figurative maps he issued in his last years, and it became his best known work only after Tufte republished it in 1983 and made it the centrepiece of modern data-graphics teaching.

Public record

The map is in the public domain and is reproduced in textbooks, museums and the statistical literature worldwide. The published figures behind it are the army sizes at each stage: 422,000 crossing the Niemen in June 1812, about 100,000 reaching Moscow in September, about 50,000 at the Berezina crossing in late November, and about 10,000 survivors in December. The temperature series runs from the retreat out of Moscow in October to the final crossing in December, falling to thirty degrees below zero on the chart's scale. The original lithograph and Minard's other figurative maps are held in French public collections and widely reproduced; no public endpoint or license applies because the work is a nineteenth-century printed map, not a database.

The design keeps the original numbers, adds nothing to the history, and teaches the reader to measure loss by width.

The place list runs Niemen, Vilna, Vitebsk, Smolensk, Moscow, then back through the Berezina to Vilna and the Niemen. All four army figures are Minard's published estimates, compiled from the histories of the campaign.

Science proof

The map works because width is a visual channel the eye reads without counting: a band half as wide means half as many men, and the reader feels the loss before doing the arithmetic. That is the science of the graphic - proportional encoding on a map, two variables (place and time) carried by one moving band, and a second variable (temperature) drawn on the same time axis below. Florence Nightingale's Rose Diagram of War Deaths Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: design-study Design study: this Signature record designs how the Signature archive would present, animate, and teach Nightingale's rose diagram, and it presents no new historical claim.

Florence Nightingale's 1858 rose diagram, a polar area chart that showed the British army in the Crimean War was dying of preventable disease, not of wounds: blue wedges for disease, red for wounds, black for other causes, month by month.

Florence Nightingale's rose diagram is the graph that changed a war and then changed public health. In the Crimean War the British army was losing far more men to disease than to Russian guns, and Nightingale, who had gone to the military hospital at Scutari in 1854, set out to prove it in a picture a politician could not ignore. Her diagram, published in 1858 as the Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East, is a polar area chart: twelve wedges, one for each month from April 1854 to March 1855, and in each wedge three coloured areas - blue for deaths from preventable diseases such as cholera, typhus and dysentery, red for deaths from wounds, black for all other causes. The area of each coloured patch is proportional to the number of deaths, so the blue floods the picture and the red is a thin rim. The numbers were stark: of roughly 18,000 deaths in the first year of the war, about 16,000 were from disease and only about 2,000 from wounds, and the death rate in the Scutari hospital in the worst months ran above 40 per cent of the men admitted. Nightingale's second diagram, covering April 1855 to March 1856, shows the blue shrinking after sanitary reforms - clean water, drainage, ventilation and laundry - cut the death rate to about 2 per cent. The diagram worked because it made a moral argument in geometry: the enemy was not the Russians but the filth of the army's own camps, and the remedy was sanitation, not more surgeons. Nightingale sent the diagram to Queen Victoria, to Parliament and to the press, and it helped drive the Royal Commission on the Health of the Army and the sanitary reforms that followed. She was elected the first woman member of the Royal Statistical Society in 1859, the year after the diagram appeared.

History

Nightingale (1820-1910) arrived at Scutari in November 1854 with thirty-eight nurses and found a hospital with no clean water, no drainage and men lying in their own filth. She kept meticulous records of admissions and deaths, and with the statistician William Farr she turned them into tables and then into the rose diagram, first published in her 1858 Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army and in a separate pamphlet the same year. The diagram is often called a coxcomb, after the shape of its wedges, and it is one of the earliest polar area charts used to drive public policy.

Public record

The diagram is in the public domain and is held in the collections of the Florence Nightingale Museum and the British Library, and reproduced in every history of statistics. The published figures are about 18,000 deaths in the first year of the war, about 16,000 of them from preventable disease and about 2,000 from wounds, with the hospital death rate above 40 per cent in the worst months and about 2 per cent after the sanitary reforms of 1855. Nightingale's own tables give the monthly deaths by cause, and the diagram is drawn directly from them. No public endpoint or license applies: the work is a nineteenth-century printed diagram, not a database.

The design keeps Nightingale's figures, adds nothing to the history, and teaches the reader to judge by area rather than by length.

John Snow's 1854 dot map of the Soho cholera outbreak: a black bar for every death, clustered around the Broad Street pump, the picture that proved cholera travelled in water and not in bad air.

John Snow's cholera map is the founding document of epidemiology, and it is a map with almost nothing on it: a street plan of Soho, a black bar for every cholera death, and a small mark for each water pump. The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram, the map of the stars: luminosity plotted against temperature, with most stars falling on the main sequence and the giants and dwarfs sitting apart, the graph that turned star catalogues into stellar evolution.

The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram is the map of the stars, and it is a scatter plot that became a theory. In 1911 the Danish astronomer Ejnar Hertzsprung plotted the brightness of stars against their colour, and in 1913 the American Henry Norris Russell plotted brightness against spectral class, and both found the same pattern: the stars do not scatter at random but fall into groups. Most stars lie on a diagonal band from hot bright blue stars at the top left to cool dim red stars at the bottom right, and that band is the main sequence, where stars spend most of their lives burning hydrogen. Above it sit the giants and supergiants, stars that are bright because they are enormous, and below it sit the white dwarfs, stars that are hot but tiny and therefore dim. The diagram's axes are luminosity, measured in units of the Sun's brightness, against temperature or spectral class, running from the hot O stars at about 30,000 degrees down to the cool M stars at about 3,000 degrees. A star's place on the diagram tells its life story: a star is born on the main sequence, leaves it when its hydrogen runs out, swells into a giant, and ends as a white dwarf, so the diagram is a map of stellar evolution as well as of the present sky. The diagram also gave astronomy its first distances: Russell used it to estimate how far away the stars were, and the main sequence became the standard candle by which the scale of the galaxy was measured. Modern versions plot tens of thousands of stars from the Hipparcos and Gaia satellites, and the pattern Hertzsprung and Russell found in a few dozen stars is still there, sharper than ever.

The Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, the graph of inequality: the share of income owned by the poorest plotted against their share of the population, and the single number that measures how far a society sits from perfect equality.

The Lorenz curve is the graph of who has what, and the Gini coefficient is the number that summarises it. Max Lorenz, an American economist, drew the curve in 1905: put the population on the horizontal axis, poorest first, and the share of total income on the vertical axis, and plot the cumulative share as you move through the population. In a perfectly equal society the curve is a straight diagonal, because the poorest ten per cent would hold ten per cent of the income and the poorest half would hold half. In any real society the curve sags below the diagonal, because the poor hold less than their share and the rich hold more, and the further it sags the more unequal the society. Corrado Gini, an Italian statistician, turned the sag into a number in 1912: the Gini coefficient is the area between the diagonal and the curve divided by the area under the diagonal, a number from zero, perfect equality, to one, where a single person holds everything. A Gini of about 0.25 marks a fairly equal society, the Scandinavian countries sit near 0.27 on income, the United States near 0.41, and South Africa above 0.60, among the highest in the world. The graph is used everywhere inequality is measured: income, wealth, land, and even the distribution of citations among scientists or of species among habitats. The World Bank publishes Gini coefficients for most countries, and the curve is the standard way to show what a single number hides - that two countries with the same Gini can have very different shapes, one with a poor bottom and one with a rich top. The curve's lesson is that inequality is a shape before it is a number, and the graph shows the shape.

The Phillips curve, the graph of the trade-off between unemployment and inflation: A. W. Phillips's 1958 plot of British wage inflation against unemployment from 1861 to 1957, and the argument that low unemployment brings rising wages.

The Phillips curve is the graph that gave governments a trade-off and then took it away. In 1958 the New Zealand-born economist A. W. Phillips, working at the London School of Economics, plotted the rate of change of money wages in Britain against the unemployment rate for the years 1861 to 1957, and he found a curve: when unemployment was low, wages rose fast, and when unemployment was high, wages rose slowly or fell. The relationship was tight enough to look like a law, and economists and governments read it as a menu - pick your unemployment rate and you pick your inflation rate, and a little more inflation buys a little less unemployment. The curve was redrawn for prices rather than wages, and Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow brought it to American policy in 1960 as the guide to managing the economy. Then the 1970s broke it: unemployment and inflation rose together, a combination the curve said could not happen, and Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that the trade-off was only short-run, because workers and firms learn to expect inflation and build it into wages, so the long-run curve is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment. The modern version, the expectations-augmented Phillips curve, keeps the short-run slope and adds the lesson that trying to hold unemployment below its natural rate only buys ever-rising inflation. The graph is still drawn in every macroeconomics course, now with the short-run curve, the long-run vertical line, and the expectations shift that moves the curve when people change what they expect. Its modern form is the expectations-augmented curve, and central banks still watch it: when unemployment falls below its natural rate, wage pressure builds, and the curve is the first place they look for the sign.

The Laffer curve, the graph of tax rates and tax revenue: Arthur Laffer's 1974 argument, drawn on a napkin, that revenue is zero at a zero rate and zero at a hundred per cent, and must peak somewhere in between.

The Laffer curve is the most famous napkin in economics. In 1974 the economist Arthur Laffer, dining with the journalist Jude Wanniski and the politicians Donald Rumsfeld and Dick Cheney, is said to have drawn a curve on a napkin showing tax revenue against the tax rate: at a rate of zero the government collects nothing, at a rate of one hundred per cent it also collects nothing, because nobody will work for nothing, and somewhere in between the revenue reaches a peak. The argument was that beyond the peak, cutting the tax rate raises revenue, because people work more, report more and hide less. The curve became the intellectual emblem of the supply-side tax cuts of the 1980s, above all the Reagan administration's 1981 tax cut, and it has been argued over ever since. The economics behind it is not disputed in its general form - nobody denies that a hundred per cent tax raises nothing - but the shape and the position of the peak are the whole argument, and they are not settled by the drawing. Modern estimates put the revenue-maximising rate for income tax well above the rates most countries charge, which means most economies sit on the rising part of the curve and a cut would lose revenue, while a few particular taxes - on capital gains, on cigarettes, on activities that can move abroad - may sit near or past their peaks. The curve's lesson is about the difference between a true statement and a useful one: the napkin proves that a peak exists, and it says nothing about where the peak is, which is the only question a government needs answered. Economists still argue over the peak's position, and the argument is the whole point of the graph.

The Moore's law plot is the graph that built the computer age, and it is a straight line on a logarithmic scale. In 1965 Gordon Moore, a co-founder of Fairchild Semiconductor and later of Intel, noticed that the number of components on a chip had doubled every year since 1959, and he predicted it would keep doubling, reaching 65,000 components by 1975. Ten years later, in 1975, he revised the doubling time to about two years, and the industry adopted the revised version as its roadmap: every two years, twice as many transistors, at roughly the same cost, which meant computers doubled in power and halved in price on a steady schedule. The plot that shows it is a log scale, where each step up is a doubling, and the data falls on a straight line from a few dozen transistors in the early 1960s to tens of billions today: the Intel 4004 of 1971 held about 2,300 transistors, the 8086 of 1978 about 29,000, the Pentium of 1993 about 3.1 million, and modern chips hold tens of billions. The Koch snowflake is the graph that broke the idea of length. In 1904 the Swedish mathematician Helge von Koch described a curve built by a single repeated rule: take a line segment, cut it into three equal parts, and replace the middle part with two sides of an equilateral triangle. The result, after infinite steps, is a curve with a paradox: its perimeter is infinite, because each step multiplies the length by four thirds, and four thirds to an infinite power is infinity, while the area it encloses is finite, because the triangles added at each step shrink fast enough to sum to a finite total. Koch's curve was one of the first fractals, though the word fractal came seventy years later from Benoit Mandelbrot, who saw that the same self-similarity describes coastlines, clouds, mountains and the branching of trees and blood vessels. The snowflake's fractal dimension is about 1.262, the logarithm of four over the logarithm of three, which says it is more than a line and less than a surface. The graph is drawn today by every fractal program in a few lines of code, and it is taught as the simplest example of a shape that is infinite in one measure and finite in another.
