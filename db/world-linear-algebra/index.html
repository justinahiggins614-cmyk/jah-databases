What Linear Algebra Is

ID: JAH-LNAL-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-linear-algebra#JAH-LNAL-000001 k=a489b0a9

Definition: Linear algebra is the mathematics of vectors and matrices: lists of numbers, and tables of numbers, with rules for adding them, multiplying them, and solving the equations they describe. It is the mathematics of many variables at once: a system of linear equations, a network, a picture, or a dataset is a matrix, and the tools of linear algebra — elimination, determinants, eigenvalues — are how it is solved. Linear algebra is the most used mathematics in computing.

History: Linear algebra began with the solving of simultaneous equations, in China 2,000 years ago, where the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art solved systems by elimination, and in Europe in the 17th century, when Leibniz and Cramer wrote the first determinants. In the 19th century, Cayley introduced matrices in 1858, and Grassmann and Peano introduced vector spaces. In the 20th century, linear algebra became the language of quantum mechanics, statistics, and computing: the first computers solved linear systems, and the first algorithms were matrix algorithms. Today linear algebra is everywhere: search engines, image processing, machine learning, and structural engineering are all linear algebra.

Key concepts: The key ideas are vector, matrix, linear equation, system of equations, linear transformation. A vector is a list of numbers, or a direction with a magnitude. Forces, velocities, and data points are vectors. A vector in three dimensions has three components. A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, with rows and columns. A photograph is a matrix of pixel values; a network is a matrix of connections. A linear equation is an equation where the unknowns appear to the first power only: 2x + 3y = 7 is linear, and x^2 + y = 7 is not. A system of equations is a set of equations to be solved together. A system of two equations in two unknowns has a solution where the lines cross. A linear transformation is a function that preserves addition and scaling: it maps lines to lines, and the origin to the origin. Every matrix is a linear transformation. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, write the problem as a system of equations, or as a matrix. Second, use elimination to solve it, or a factorisation. Third, check the solution: does it satisfy every equation?. Fourth, interpret the solution in the original problem. Fifth, if the system is large, use a computer: the algorithms are the same. Worked examples: The system 2x + y = 5 and x - y = 1 has the solution x = 2, y = 1: adding the equations gives 3x = 6, so x = 2, and then y = 1. Check: 2 times 2 + 1 = 5, and 2 - 1 = 1. A vector in the plane, (3, 4), has length sqrt(3^2 + 4^2) = 5. A matrix with rows (1, 2) and (3, 4) times the vector (1, 1) gives (3, 7): the first row gives 1 times 1 + 2 times 1 = 3, and the second gives 3 times 1 + 4 times 1 = 7. A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels is a matrix with a million entries.

Real applications: Computer graphics is linear algebra: a 3D scene is a set of matrices, and moving, rotating, and projecting it are matrix multiplications. Search engines are linear algebra: Google's PageRank is an eigenvector of the web's link matrix. Machine learning is linear algebra: a dataset is a matrix, and training a model is solving a linear algebra problem. Structural engineering is linear algebra: the forces in a truss are a system of linear equations, and the solution is the design.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is the difference between a vector and a matrix? A vector is a list of numbers; a matrix is a table. A matrix acts on a vector to give another vector: it is a transformation. Why is linear algebra so useful? Because so many problems are linear, or nearly linear: a small change in the input gives a proportional change in the output, and a matrix captures that. Is linear algebra hard? Its ideas are simple — vectors, matrices, solving equations — but its applications are deep, and its computations are large. Do I need a computer? For large systems, yes: a system with a million unknowns is solved by a computer, but the algorithm is the elimination you learn by hand.

Vectors

ID: JAH-LNAL-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-linear-algebra#JAH-LNAL-000002 k=756911c3

Definition: A vector is a quantity with magnitude and direction: a force, a velocity, or a displacement. In coordinates, a vector is a list of numbers: (3, 4) is a vector in the plane, and (1, 2, 3) is a vector in space. Vectors can be added, by adding their components, and scaled, by multiplying each component by a number. The length of a vector is the square root of the sum of the squares of its components.

History: Vectors were used implicitly for centuries: forces were drawn as arrows, and velocities as directed lines. In the 19th century, Hamilton invented quaternions in 1843, and Grassmann invented the vector in 1844, in his Ausdehnungslehre, which was ignored for decades. Gibbs and Heaviside, in the 1880s, created the vector analysis used today, with the dot product and the cross product. In the 20th century, vectors became the language of physics and engineering, and the vector space — the abstract setting for vectors — became the foundation of linear algebra. Today vectors are everywhere: in physics, in graphics, in data, and in machine learning.

Key concepts: The key ideas are vector, magnitude, direction, component, unit vector. A vector is a list of numbers, or a quantity with magnitude and direction. (3, 4) is a vector in the plane. The magnitude of a vector is its length: the square root of the sum of the squares of its components. The magnitude of (3, 4) is 5. The direction of a vector is where it points: the angle it makes with the axes. Two vectors with the same direction are parallel. A component is one of the numbers in a vector. The vector (3, 4) has components 3 and 4. A unit vector has magnitude 1. It is a pure direction: any vector can be written as its magnitude times a unit vector. A speed is a number; a velocity is a vector. Can vectors have more than three components? Yes: a vector can have any number of components. A data point with 100 features is a vector in 100 dimensions. What is a unit vector for? It is a pure direction: it separates the magnitude from the direction, and it is used to project one vector onto another. Is the zero vector a vector? Yes: it is the vector with all components zero. It has magnitude 0 and no direction, and it is the identity for addition.

Vector Spaces

ID: JAH-LNAL-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-linear-algebra#JAH-LNAL-000003 k=8c732ce4

Definition: A vector space is a set of vectors, with addition and scaling, that obeys ten axioms: the sum of two vectors is a vector, scaling a vector gives a vector, there is a zero vector, and so on. The plane is a vector space; so is the set of all polynomials, the set of all functions, and the set of all matrices of a given size. A vector space is the abstract setting in which linear algebra lives.

History: Vector spaces were implicit in the work of Grassmann in 1844, who defined the vector and its algebra, but his work was ignored. In the 1880s, Peano gave the first modern definition, with axioms, in his Calcolo geometrico of 1888. In the 1920s, Banach and Hilbert developed the infinite-dimensional vector spaces of analysis, and in the 1930s, the vector space became the foundation of linear algebra, through the work of Weyl and van der Waerden. Today the vector space is the central object of linear algebra, and it appears in every field that uses vectors: physics, engineering, statistics, and computer science.

Key concepts: The key ideas are vector space, subspace, span, linear independence, basis. A vector space is a set with addition and scaling that obeys the axioms: closure, associativity, a zero vector, inverses, and distributivity. A subspace is a subset of a vector space that is itself a vector space: it contains the zero vector and is closed under addition and scaling. The span of a set of vectors is all their linear combinations. The span of (1, 0) and (0, 1) is the whole plane. A set of vectors is linearly independent when no one of them is a combination of the others. (1, 0) and (0, 1) are independent; (1, 0) and (2, 0) are not. A basis is a linearly independent set that spans the space. Every vector in the space is a unique combination of the basis vectors. Second, find the zero vector, and check that inverses exist. Third, test independence: can any vector be written as a combination of the others?. Fourth, find a basis: a spanning independent set. Fifth, count the basis: its size is the dimension of the space. Worked examples: The plane is a vector space: the sum of two vectors in the plane is in the plane, and a scaled vector is in the plane. The set of all polynomials of degree at most 2 is a vector space of dimension 3, with basis 1, x, x^2: every such polynomial is a + bx + cx^2. The vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) form a basis of the plane: every vector (a, b) is a(1, 0) + b(0, 1). The vectors (1, 2) and (2, 4) are dependent, because (2, 4) = 2(1, 2): they span only a line, not the plane. The set of all 2-by-2 matrices is a vector space of dimension 4.

Real applications: Physics uses vector spaces: the states of a quantum system form a vector space, and the observables are operators on it. Engineering uses vector spaces: the signals in a system form a vector space, and the filters are linear transformations. Statistics uses vector spaces: the data form a vector space, and the regression is a projection onto a subspace. Computer graphics uses vector spaces: the colours, the positions, and the normals are all vectors in vector spaces.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is a vector space, in plain words? A set of things that can be added and scaled, like arrows: the sum of two arrows is an arrow, and a scaled arrow is an arrow. Why the axioms? Because they capture what addition and scaling must do: the axioms are the rules that make a set behave like arrows. What is the dimension? The number of vectors in a basis: the plane has dimension 2, space has dimension 3, and the polynomials of degree at most 2 have dimension 3. Can a vector space be infinite-dimensional? Yes: the set of all functions, or all polynomials, is infinite-dimensional, and analysis is the study of such spaces.

Matrices

ID: JAH-LNAL-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-linear-algebra#JAH-LNAL-000004 k=cbf2121e

Definition: A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, with rows and columns. Matrices can be added, by adding corresponding entries, and multiplied, by the row-by-column rule: the entry in row i and column j of the product is the dot product of row i of the first matrix and column j of the second. Matrix multiplication is the composition of linear transformations, and it is not commutative: AB is usually not BA.

History: Matrices were used implicitly for centuries, in the solving of simultaneous equations, but the word matrix was coined by Sylvester in 1850, and the theory was created by Cayley in 1858, in his Memoir on the Theory of Matrices, which defined addition, multiplication, and the inverse. In the 20th century, matrices became the language of quantum mechanics, statistics, and computing: Heisenberg's matrix mechanics of 1925 was the first quantum theory, and the first computers solved matrix equations. Today matrices are everywhere: in graphics, in search, in machine learning, and in engineering.

Key concepts: The key ideas are matrix, matrix multiplication, identity matrix, transpose, inverse matrix. A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. A 2-by-3 matrix has 2 rows and 3 columns, and 6 entries. Matrix multiplication is the row-by-column rule: the (i, j) entry of the product is the dot product of row i and column j. It is not commutative. The identity matrix has 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere. Multiplying by it changes nothing: AI = A. The transpose of a matrix swaps its rows and columns. The transpose of a 2-by-3 matrix is 3-by-2. The inverse of a matrix A is the matrix A^-1 with A A^-1 = I. Not every matrix has an inverse: only square matrices with non-zero determinant do. Matrix Multiplication

ID: JAH-LNAL-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-linear-algebra#JAH-LNAL-000005 k=83e92a11

Definition: Matrix multiplication is the rule for combining matrices: the (i, j) entry of the product is the dot product of row i of the first matrix and column j of the second. It is defined when the number of columns of the first equals the number of rows of the second, and the product has the rows of the first and the columns of the second. Matrix multiplication is the composition of linear transformations: applying one transformation and then another is multiplying their matrices.

History: Matrix multiplication was defined by Cayley in 1858, as the composition of linear transformations: the product of two matrices is the matrix of the composed transformation. In the 20th century, matrix multiplication became the central computation of linear algebra, and the search for faster algorithms began: Strassen's algorithm of 1969 multiplies n-by-n matrices in about n^2.81 steps, instead of n^3, and the current best is about n^2.37. Today matrix multiplication is the most computed operation in the world: it is the engine of graphics, machine learning, and scientific computing, and it is accelerated by specialised hardware.

Key concepts: The key ideas are matrix multiplication, composition, associativity, Strassen's algorithm, identity. The (i, j) entry of the product is the dot product of row i and column j. The product of an m-by-n and an n-by-p matrix is m-by-p. Composition is applying one transformation after another. The matrix of the composition is the product of the matrices. Matrix multiplication is associative: (AB)C = A(BC). It is not commutative: AB is usually not BA. Strassen's algorithm multiplies matrices in about n^2.81 steps, by trading multiplications for additions. It was the first to beat n^3. The identity matrix is the 1 of matrices: AI = IA = A. It is the matrix of the transformation that does nothing. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

Second, compute each entry as a dot product of a row and a column. Third, check the size of the result: rows of the first, columns of the second. Fourth, remember the order: AB is A followed by B, and it is not BA. Fifth, use the product to compose transformations: a rotation and a scaling are one matrix. Worked examples: A 2-by-3 matrix times a 3-by-2 matrix gives a 2-by-2 matrix. The product of [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and [[0, 1], [1, 0]] is [[2, 1], [4, 3]]: the (1, 1) entry is 1 times 0 + 2 times 1 = 2, and the (2, 2) is 3 times 1 + 4 times 0 = 3. Multiplying a matrix by the identity leaves it unchanged. A rotation by 90 degrees is the matrix [[0, -1], [1, 0]]: applied to (1, 0), it gives (0, 1), which is a 90-degree rotation. Composing two rotations gives another rotation, and the product of their matrices is the matrix of the composed rotation.

Real applications: Computer graphics is matrix multiplication: every object is moved, rotated, and scaled by multiplying matrices, and the scene is rendered by millions of multiplications. Machine learning is matrix multiplication: a layer of a neural network is a matrix multiplication, and training is a sequence of them. Search engines are matrix multiplication: the ranking is computed by multiplying the link matrix by a vector, again and again. Scientific computing is matrix multiplication: the simulation of a fluid, a structure, or a climate is a sequence of matrix multiplications.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why is the (i, j) entry a dot product? Because the product is the composition of transformations: the (i, j) entry is how much the j-th input contributes to the i-th output, and that is a dot product. Is matrix multiplication associative? Yes: (AB)C = A(BC). It is not commutative, but it is associative, and that is what makes it useful. How fast can matrices be multiplied? The naive method is n^3; Strassen's is about n^2.81, and the best known is about n^2.37. Whether n^2 is possible is an open problem. Why does the order matter? Because transformations do not commute: rotating and then scaling is different from scaling and then rotating, and the matrices reflect that.

Determinants

ID: JAH-LNAL-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-linear-algebra#JAH-LNAL-000006 k=793a1fc4

Definition: The determinant is a number assigned to a square matrix: it measures how the matrix scales volume, and whether it is invertible. A 2-by-2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] has determinant ad - bc. If the determinant is zero, the matrix is singular: it squashes space flat, and it has no inverse. If it is non-zero, the matrix is invertible. The determinant also appears in Cramer's rule, which solves a system by ratios of determinants.

History: Determinants are older than matrices: Leibniz wrote them in 1693, in a letter to l'Hôpital, and Cramer published his rule in 1750. In the 19th century, Cauchy and Jacobi developed the theory, and the determinant became the standard tool for solving systems. In the 20th century, the determinant was partly replaced by elimination, which is faster, but it remains the theoretical tool: it decides invertibility, it appears in the formula for the inverse, and it measures volume. Today the determinant is taught as the first invariant of a matrix, and it is used in geometry, in physics, and in the theory of equations.
