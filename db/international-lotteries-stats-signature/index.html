International Comparison: Matrices and Odds Method

Matrix: Methodology record — no single game matrix · Top-prize odds: 1 in not applicable (methodology record) · Rank: complex

This Signature-original study record presents International Comparison: Matrices and Odds Method as a teaching guide. The matrix figures quoted here are the public game's published figures, used as reference material for the mathematics; the worked analysis and the simulated demonstrations in this record are Signature-original material, not an authentic public record.

International comparison starts with the same method as the state comparison: record the matrix, the matrix date, and the combination count per line, and convert to a common basis before ranking. Across this pair, jackpot odds run from about 1 in 3.8 million for New Zealand Lotto to about 1 in 134.5 million for Powerball Australia. That range is driven entirely by pool sizes and second pools, not by country or by jackpot size.

Three adjustments are needed before any cross-country ranking. First, separate single-pool games, two-pool games, same-pool bonus games, and fixed-odds wheel games, because their odds are computed differently. Second, state whether the figure is per line, per play, or per dollar, since minimum lines and prices differ. Third, keep currency out of the odds ranking: a jackpot in euros, reais, or yen cannot be ranked against a jackpot in dollars without a conversion, and conversions move daily.

Jackpot Caps Across Countries: General Information

Matrix: Methodology record — no single game matrix · Top-prize odds: 1 in not applicable (methodology record) · Rank: complex

This Signature-original study record presents Jackpot Caps Across Countries: General Information as a teaching guide. The matrix figures quoted here are the public game's published figures, used as reference material for the mathematics; the worked analysis and the simulated demonstrations in this record are Signature-original material, not an authentic public record.

Jackpot caps take three forms. A money cap sets a maximum jackpot amount, as in German Lotto 6aus49, where the jackpot is capped at 50 million euros. A rollover cap sets a maximum number of rollovers, as in French Loto, where the jackpot rolls down to the next tier after 34 consecutive rollovers without a jackpot winner. A must-be-won rule sets a cap with a forced payout, as in New Zealand Powerball, where the jackpot becomes a Must Be Won draw at NZ$60 million.

Uncapped games take the opposite approach. California SuperLotto Plus has no rollover cap, so its jackpot keeps growing until won, and New York Lotto likewise has no cap. Uncapped games can therefore reach very large jackpots after long rollover runs, while capped games pay out sooner and more often at the cap boundary.

Currency and Payout Variation: General Information Only

Matrix: Methodology record — no single game matrix · Top-prize odds: 1 in not applicable (methodology record) · Rank: complex

This Signature-original study record presents Currency and Payout Variation: General Information Only as a teaching guide. The matrix figures quoted here are the public game's published figures, used as reference material for the mathematics; the worked analysis and the simulated demonstrations in this record are Signature-original material, not an authentic public record.

International prizes are paid in the currency of the country where the ticket was sold: euros in Germany, France, and Spain; reais in Brazil; yen in Japan; rand in South Africa; Australian dollars in Australia; New Zealand dollars in New Zealand. Headline jackpots in different currencies cannot be ranked without conversion, and a conversion done on one date will differ from a conversion done on another date. This record therefore ranks games by odds, not by converted jackpot size.

Payout form also varies by country. Some games pay lump sums only, some offer an annuity or a lump-sum choice, and some pay lifetime prizes with a cash option, as in the multi-state life-prize games. Tax treatment varies too: lottery prizes are tax-free in Germany and in New Zealand under local law, while in the United States federal withholding of 24 percent applies to prizes over $5,000 and state tax varies by state. This record is general information only, not tax advice.

Verification Guide: Official Sources by Country

Matrix: Methodology record — no single game matrix · Top-prize odds: 1 in not applicable (methodology record) · Rank: complex

This Signature-original study record presents Verification Guide: Official Sources by Country as a teaching guide. The matrix figures quoted here are the public game's published figures, used as reference material for the mathematics; the worked analysis and the simulated demonstrations in this record are Signature-original material, not an authentic public record.

The verification rule is the same in every country: the operator's own results archive is the primary source, and every other listing is a copy to be checked against it. Responsible Play and Common Fallacies

Matrix: Methodology record — no single game matrix · Top-prize odds: 1 in not applicable (methodology record) · Rank: complex

This Signature-original study record presents Responsible Play and Common Fallacies as a teaching guide. The matrix figures quoted here are the public game's published figures, used as reference material for the mathematics; the worked analysis and the simulated demonstrations in this record are Signature-original material, not an authentic public record.

The fallacies in international play are the same as in state play, with one addition: the currency fallacy, which treats a large-looking foreign jackpot as a large prize without converting currency, tax, and payout form. The other four are the gambler's fallacy (a number is due), the hot-number fallacy (a frequent number is more likely), the pattern fallacy (birthdays or shapes are special), and the system fallacy (a selection method beats the odds). None of them changes the combination count of any game in this pair.

A second international trap is the matrix-date trap: quoting an old matrix for a game that has changed, such as Oz Lotto before May 2022, New Jersey Pick-6 before April 2022, or New Zealand Powerball before September 2026. An odds figure without a matrix date is not a verified odds figure.

import random

TITLE = "Fallacy check"
random.seed(20261026)


def main():
    print(TITLE + " - labeled simulated draws")
    draws = [set(random.sample(range(1, 46), 6)) for _ in range(5000)]
    after_absence = 0
    hits_after_absence = 0
    for i in range(1, len(draws)):
        if 7 not in draws[i - 1]:
            after_absence += 1
            if 7 in draws[i]:
                hits_after_absence += 1
    print('Draws following a draw without 7: ' + str(after_absence))
    print('Of those, draws containing 7: ' + str(hits_after_absence))
    print('Rate x1000: ' + str(hits_after_absence * 1000 // after_absence))
    print('Fair-draw rate x1000 (6 / 45): ' + str(6 * 1000 // 45))
    print('Reading: absence did not make 7 more likely; the rate stays near the fair-draw rate.')
    print("SIMULATED DATA: these draws are generated for teaching only, not real draw history.")


main()
