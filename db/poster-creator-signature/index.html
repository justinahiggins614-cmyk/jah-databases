Feasibility: buildable-now One-message poster design is established practice today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a poster creator that builds a poster around one clear message, with nothing on the page that fights it. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

A poster that tries to say three things says nothing: the reader's eye has nowhere to land, the words compete with each other, and the one thing that mattered gets lost among the rest. A poster works only when one message is biggest, one message is first, and everything else serves it. A creator that asks for the one message first, makes it the biggest thing on the page, and refuses to let anything else grow as large, turns a crowded poster into a clear one: the reader sees the message from across the room, understands it in seconds, and remembers it afterward. The discipline is simple, and it is the difference between a poster and a noticeboard full of noise.

Worked example, Signature design: the same fair poster is built message-first, the message stays largest, and anything that competed with it was removed and named.

History

The one-message poster is as old as printed notices, from town criers' bills to wartime posters that said one thing in letters a street away. The creator applies that old discipline, before anything is drawn.

Proposed solution

Name the one message first, in the maker's own words; Size it largest, and keep everything else clearly smaller; Check the reading distance before the preview; Remove anything that competes, and name what was removed; Keep the refusal visible when a second message is added.

Dependencies

A way to name the one message; type sizes that keep it largest; a reading-distance check; a refusal for a second equal message.

Feasibility

Verification path

This database holds twenty-three full records about the poster creator, ordered from simple to compounded to complex, and a reader arriving without a map cannot tell which record to read first, which records belong together, or where the harder designs begin. The field guide index solves that by listing every record by number and by name, in order, with a one-line account of what each record covers and which rung of the ladder it sits on. Without the index, the simple records and the complex records blur together, and a reader who needs the basics may open a complex design first, while a reader who wants the hardest designs may never find them. The index is the map that keeps the ladder readable, and it is the record that turns twenty-three separate designs into one set a reader can walk through from start to finish.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order, with its Signature design noted; Give each record a one-line account of what its Signature version changes; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.

Dependencies

The twenty-three records themselves, listed in order; the ladder ranks simple, compounded, and complex; agreement that an index lists, and never replaces, the records it points to.
