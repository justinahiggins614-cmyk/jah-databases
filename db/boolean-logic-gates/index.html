What a Boolean value is Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Boolean values, truth tables, and the function count are established mathematics from Boole 1854 and Shannon 1937.

A Boolean value is either true or false, and every logic gate in this pair is a function from Boolean values to Boolean values.

Every gate, truth table, and circuit in the first half of this pair rests on one idea: a Boolean value has exactly two possibilities, true or false, written 1 or 0. A Boolean function takes one or more Boolean inputs and returns one Boolean output, and the function's entire behavior is captured by its truth table, the list of outputs for every combination of inputs. With one input there are four possible functions; with two inputs there are sixteen. The record states the idea, the notation, and the counting, so that every later record can be read as a function with a table, and it explains why two values are enough: any digital circuit, however large, is built from these two-valued functions wired together.

A function of one input has two rows: input 0 and input 1. The NOT function returns 1 for input 0 and 0 for input 1. A function of two inputs has four rows: 00, 01, 10, 11. The AND function returns 1 only for the row 11 and 0 for the other three. The XOR function returns 1 for 01 and 10 and 0 for 00 and 11. Each table is complete: there is no fifth row for two inputs, and no input combination is left unanswered.

History

George Boole published An Investigation of the Laws of Thought in 1854, turning logic into algebra with two values. Claude Shannon's 1937 master's thesis showed that Boolean algebra describes electrical switching circuits, which is why the same mathematics runs both logic and computers. The two-value convention, the truth table, and the function count all come from that line: Boole for the algebra, Shannon for the circuits.

Method

Write the two values as 1 for true and 0 for false, and use them consistently.; Define a Boolean function by listing its output for every combination of inputs.; Count the functions: with n inputs there are 2^n input combinations and 2^(2^n) functions.; Check a function by reading its truth table row by row, never by guessing.; Record the function with its name, its inputs, and its full table, so the table is the definition..

Dataset

The dataset is the counting table for Boolean functions: 1 input gives 2 combinations and 4 functions; 2 inputs give 4 combinations and 16 functions; 3 inputs give 8 combinations and 256 functions. The record also carries the full truth tables for NOT, AND, OR, and XOR as the reference tables for the pair.

Scorer

The scorer is a truth-table check: for each test case, state the output of a Boolean function for a given input combination and compare against the stored tables. The score is the share of ten cases answered correctly. A case passes when the stated output matches the table exactly.

The AND gate and its truth table Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: established The AND gate and its truth table are established logic from Boole 1854, and the gate is the standard conjunction in every digital logic text.

The AND gate returns 1 only when all its inputs are 1, and its truth table is the definition of logical conjunction.

The AND gate is the gate of togetherness: with two inputs A and B, the output is 1 only when A is 1 and B is 1, and 0 in the other three cases. Its truth table reads 00 gives 0, 01 gives 0, 10 gives 0, 11 gives 1. The gate is written A AND B, or A dot B, or AB in Boolean algebra, and it is the gate that builds the carry output of the half adder later in this pair. The record gives the table, the symbols, and the reading rule: AND is strict, it demands everything, and a single 0 anywhere in the inputs forces the output to 0. That strictness is what makes AND the gate for conditions that must all hold.

A security door needs two keys turned at once: the door opens only when key A is turned and key B is turned, which is an AND gate with the keys as inputs and the lock as output. If only key A is turned, the table row is 10 and the output is 0, so the door stays shut. If both are turned, the row is 11 and the output is 1, so the door opens. The same table governs the carry of a half adder: 1 AND 1 gives a carry of 1, and every other pair gives a carry of 0.

History

AND as a logical connective goes back to Aristotle's conjunction, and Boole made it algebraic in 1854. As a physical gate it arrived with relay and then transistor circuits in the twentieth century, and Shannon's 1937 thesis showed how AND gates wire into adders and larger circuits. The symbol set, the dot, the ampersand, and the D-shaped gate symbol, comes from the IEEE standard for logic symbols.

The OR gate and its truth table Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established The OR gate and its truth table are established logic from Boole 1854, and the inclusive reading is the standard disjunction in digital logic.

The OR gate returns 1 when at least one input is 1, and its truth table is the definition of logical disjunction.

The OR gate is the gate of either: with two inputs A and B, the output is 1 when A is 1, when B is 1, or when both are 1, and 0 only when both are 0. Its truth table reads 00 gives 0, 01 gives 1, 10 gives 1, 11 gives 1. The gate is written A OR B, or A plus B in Boolean algebra, and it is the gate that builds the carry output of the full adder later in this pair. The record gives the table, the symbols, and the reading rule: OR is generous, it accepts anything, and only a complete absence of 1s forces the output to 0. That generosity is what makes OR the gate for conditions where any one is enough.

A warning light must turn on when either sensor A or sensor B trips: the light is an OR gate with the sensors as inputs. If neither trips, the row is 00 and the light stays off. If sensor A trips alone, the row is 10 and the light turns on. If both trip, the row is 11 and the light turns on, because this OR is inclusive. The same table governs the carry of a full adder: the carry is 1 when either of its two partial carries is 1.

OR as a logical connective goes back to Aristotle's disjunction, and Boole made it algebraic in 1854. The inclusive reading used here, where both inputs 1 gives output 1, is the standard one in Boolean algebra; the exclusive reading is a different gate, XOR, covered in its own record. As a physical gate OR arrived with relay and transistor circuits, and Shannon's 1937 thesis placed it beside AND as a basic building block.

The NOT gate and its truth table Rank 4 · simple

Evidence: established The NOT gate and its truth table are established logic from Boole 1854, and the inverter is the standard negation in every digital logic text.

The NOT gate returns the opposite of its input, and it is the only gate in this pair with a single input.

The NOT gate is the gate of opposite: with one input A, the output is 1 when A is 0 and 0 when A is 1. Its truth table has two rows: 0 gives 1, and 1 gives 0. The gate is written NOT A, or A with a bar over it, or A prime, and it is the gate that inverts signals, builds NAND and NOR from AND and OR, and flips the select line of a multiplexer. The record gives the table, the symbols, and the reading rule: NOT swaps the two values, and applying it twice returns the original, so NOT NOT A is A. That involution is what makes NOT the gate for negation everywhere in logic and circuits.

A light must be on when a switch is off, an inverted signal: the light is driven through a NOT gate with the switch as input. Switch off, input 0, the gate outputs 1 and the light is on. Switch on, input 1, the gate outputs 0 and the light is off. Apply NOT twice, switch to light to a second gate, and the output follows the switch again, because NOT NOT A is A.

History

Negation as a logical operation goes back to Aristotle, and Boole made it algebraic in 1854 as complementation. As a physical gate NOT arrived with the transistor inverter, the simplest logic gate to build, and Shannon's 1937 thesis used it to complement signals in switching circuits. The bar notation and the triangle-with-circle gate symbol come from the standard logic-symbol conventions.

The XOR gate and its truth table Rank 5 · simple

Evidence: established The XOR gate and its truth table are established logic, and the addition-mod-2 reading is standard in every digital logic text.

The XOR gate returns 1 when its inputs differ, and its truth table is the definition of exclusive or.

The XOR gate is the gate of difference: with two inputs A and B, the output is 1 when exactly one input is 1, and 0 when both are the same. Its truth table reads 00 gives 0, 01 gives 1, 10 gives 1, 11 gives 0. The gate is written A XOR B, or A plus B with a circle, and it is the gate that builds the sum output of the half adder and the full adder later in this pair, because adding two bits without carry is exactly XOR. The record gives the table, the symbols, and the reading rule: XOR detects difference, and it is the only two-input gate in this pair whose output is 0 for the 11 row as well as the 00 row. That difference-detection is what makes XOR the gate for addition, parity, and comparison.

A half adder adds two bits: 0 plus 0 is 0 with carry 0, 0 plus 1 is 1 with carry 0, 1 plus 0 is 1 with carry 0, and 1 plus 1 is 0 with carry 1. The sum column is exactly the XOR table, 0, 1, 1, 0, and the carry column is exactly the AND table, 0, 0, 0, 1. The Jones polynomial Rank 18 · complex

Evidence: established The Jones polynomial is established knot theory from 1984, and the tabulated values are standard in the Knot Atlas.

The Jones polynomial assigns a Laurent polynomial in q to each knot, and it distinguishes mirror images that Alexander cannot.

The Jones polynomial, written V(q), assigns to each knot a polynomial in q with integer coefficients. The unknot has V(q) = 1. The right-handed trefoil has V(q) = q^-1 + q^-3 - q^-4. The left-handed trefoil has V(q) = q + q^3 - q^4, the mirror image with q replaced by q^-1. The figure-eight has V(q) = q^2 - q + 1 - q^-1 + q^-2, which is symmetric under q to q^-1, matching the fact that the figure-eight is amphichiral, the same as its mirror. The polynomial is an invariant: it does not change under Reidemeister moves, and its power to tell mirrors apart is what made it famous when Vaughan Jones discovered it in 1984. The record gives the values, the mirror rule, and the check that V(1) = 1 for every knot.

Compare the two trefoils. The right-handed trefoil has V(q) = q^-1 + q^-3 - q^-4, and at q = 1 it gives 1 + 1 - 1 = 1, passing the check. The left-handed trefoil has V(q) = q + q^3 - q^4, which is the same polynomial with q replaced by q^-1. The two polynomials differ, so the two trefoils are different knots, which Alexander could not prove. The figure-eight has V(q) = q^2 - q + 1 - q^-1 + q^-2, unchanged when q is replaced by q^-1, so the figure-eight equals its mirror.

History

Vaughan Jones discovered the polynomial in 1984 while studying von Neumann algebras, a connection nobody expected, and it earned him the Fields Medal in 1990. The polynomial revolutionized knot theory: it distinguished the two trefoils, led to the HOMFLY and Kauffman polynomials, and connected knots to statistical mechanics and quantum field theory.

A link is several closed loops together, like a knot with more than one component, and the linking number measures how they wind around each other. The classification of surfaces Rank 23 · complex

Evidence: established The classification of surfaces is established mathematics from the nineteenth century, the model classification theorem in topology.

Every closed surface is a sphere, a connected sum of tori, or a connected sum of projective planes, and the Euler characteristic tells which.

The classification theorem for surfaces says that every closed connected surface is one of three kinds: a sphere, a connected sum of g tori for some g, or a connected sum of k projective planes for some k. The connected sum glues two surfaces together by cutting a disk out of each and joining the boundaries. The orientable surfaces are the sphere and the torus sums, classified by genus, with chi = 2 - 2g. The non-orientable surfaces are the projective plane sums, classified by k, with chi = 2 - k: the projective plane itself has chi 1, and the Klein bottle, the connected sum of two projective planes, has chi 0. The record gives the theorem, the three kinds, the two formulas, and the examples: the sphere, the torus, the genus-2 surface, the projective plane, and the Klein bottle.

A reader is handed a surface with chi = 0 that is orientable: the classification says it is a torus, genus 1, because 2 - 2 x 1 = 0. Another reader is handed a surface with chi = 0 that is non-orientable: the classification says it is a Klein bottle, k = 2, because 2 - 2 = 0. The same Euler characteristic, two different surfaces, told apart by orientability. A third reader is handed a surface with chi = -2 that is orientable: it is a genus-2 surface, because 2 - 2 x 2 = -2.

History

The classification was completed in the nineteenth century by Möbius, Jordan, and von Dyck, and it remains the model classification theorem in topology: a complete list with no exceptions, proved by cutting a surface into a polygon and reading its edge identifications. The Klein bottle is named for Felix Klein, who described it in 1882.
