Ant Colony Optimisation for Robot Swarms

How ant trail-laying solves routing and search problems for robot teams.

Ant Colony Optimisation for Robot Swarms is a public record about How ant trail-laying solves routing and search problems for robot teams. Ant colony optimisation copies how ants find food: ants lay a scent trail, called pheromone, on their way home, and other ants follow the strongest scent, so the shortest path gets the most traffic and the strongest trail. Marco Dorigo introduced ant colony optimisation in 1992, and it is now a standard method for routing, scheduling and network design. In robot swarms, virtual pheromone is a number stored in a shared map or dropped as a digital marker, and robots follow the strongest marker instead of a real scent. The method balances exploring new paths against exploiting the best known path, because pheromone fades over time and weak trails disappear. Particle Swarm Optimisation for Robot Swarms

How flocks of simple searchers find the best spot by copying their neighbours.

Particle Swarm Optimisation for Robot Swarms is a public record about How flocks of simple searchers find the best spot by copying their neighbours. Particle swarm optimisation copies how birds search for food: each searcher remembers its own best spot and watches the swarm's best spot, then steers between the two. Kennedy and Eberhart introduced particle swarm optimisation in 1995, and it is used to tune robot controllers, plan paths and place sensors. Each particle is just a candidate answer with a position and a velocity, so the method needs no gradients and works on rough, jagged problems. The swarm balances wandering against copying: too much copying and the swarm freezes on the first good spot; too much wandering and it never settles. Artificial Bee Colony Search for Robot Swarms

How honeybee foraging finds good answers with employed, onlooker and scout bees.

Artificial Bee Colony Search for Robot Swarms is a public record about How honeybee foraging finds good answers with employed, onlooker and scout bees. The artificial bee colony algorithm copies honeybee foraging: employed bees work known food sources, onlooker bees choose among those sources by watching dances, and scout bees search for new sources. Karaboga introduced the artificial bee colony algorithm in 2005, and it is used for tuning, clustering and robot path planning. A food source that stops improving is abandoned, and its employed bee becomes a scout, so the swarm never gets stuck on a dried-up source. Robot swarms use the same split: some robots exploit the best known site while others keep exploring, which keeps the team both productive and curious. Stigmergy in Robot Swarms

Coordination through marks left in the environment instead of direct messages.

Stigmergy in Robot Swarms is a public record about Coordination through marks left in the environment instead of direct messages. Stigmergy is coordination through the environment: one agent changes the world, and another agent reacts to that change, so no direct message is ever sent. The term was coined by Pierre-Paul Grasse in 1959 to explain termite building, where a termite adds mud where other termites have already added mud, and a mound grows without any termite holding a plan. Robot swarms use digital stigmergy: robots leave markers in a shared map or drop physical tokens, and other robots steer toward the markers. Stigmergy is robust because it needs no radio links: if the markers survive, the coordination survives, even when robots come and go. Self-Assembly in Robot Swarms is a public record about Small robots joining together into larger shapes and machines. Self-assembly is when small parts join into a larger structure without outside help, and robot swarms do it by docking together into chains, bridges and rafts. Self-assembling swarms are useful where one big robot cannot go: a chain of small robots can cross a ditch, and a raft of robots can float across water. The hard part is deciding when to join and when to split, since a swarm that never splits is just one stiff machine. Self-Organisation in Robot Swarms is a public record about Order that appears from local rules with no central plan. Self-organisation is order that appears without a leader: each robot follows simple local rules, and the group as a whole shows patterns no single robot planned. Examples include synchronised flashing, where fireflies and robots blink together, and lane formation, where crowds sort themselves into walking lanes without signs. The key ingredients are local interaction, feedback and randomness: robots react to neighbours, good patterns get reinforced, and small random changes keep the swarm from freezing. Self-organisation is studied in physics, biology and robotics, because the same mathematics describes magnets, cells and robot swarms. Pheromone Communication in Robot Swarms

Scent-like signals that robots leave, follow and let fade.

Pheromone Communication in Robot Swarms is a public record about Scent-like signals that robots leave, follow and let fade. Pheromones are chemical scents that ants and bees use to mark trails, warn of danger and call for help, and robot swarms copy them with digital or physical markers. A robot pheromone has three properties: it is laid where something matters, it is followed in proportion to its strength, and it fades, so the swarm always tracks fresh information. Physical pheromone can be a glowing trail, a heat spot or a chemical puff, while digital pheromone is a number in a shared map. Pheromone systems are simple and scalable: a thousand robots can follow one trail without ever talking to each other directly. Firefly Synchronisation in Robot Swarms is a public record about Robots blinking in unison the way fireflies flash together. Fireflies in Southeast Asia flash in unison, thousands of them blinking at the same moment, and the mathematics of that synchrony also drives robot swarms. The model is simple: each firefly has an internal clock, and when it sees a neighbour flash, it nudges its own clock a little earlier, so the clocks drift together over time. Robot swarms use synchronised blinking to share timing without radio messages: a shared flash can mark the start of a task or the beat for a coordinated move. Synchronisation is also a warning sign: a swarm that flashes together is healthy, and one that cannot synchronise has lost contact. Fish Schooling Models for Robot Swarms

How fish schools stay together, dodge predators and save energy.

Fish Schooling Models for Robot Swarms is a public record about How fish schools stay together, dodge predators and save energy. Fish schools move as one body: each fish watches its neighbours and matches their speed and direction, which lets the school turn as a whole and confuse predators. Schooling saves energy because fish in the middle ride the water pushed by the fish ahead, and robot swarms copy this by drafting behind neighbours. The classic model uses three zones: a near zone for avoiding collisions, a middle zone for matching speed, and a far zone for staying with the group. Underwater robot teams use schooling rules to survey reefs and track fish without scattering the animals they study. Bird Flocking Models for Robot Swarms

How starling murmurations steer drone swarms.

Bird Flocking Models for Robot Swarms is a public record about How starling murmurations steer drone swarms. Starling murmurations are flocks of thousands of birds that swirl and fold in the sky, and each bird only watches about seven neighbours, yet the whole flock turns as one. The seven-neighbour rule was measured by researchers in Rome, and it explains how a flock can be both huge and fast to react: information spreads neighbour to neighbour in waves. Drone swarms copy the rule to fly in large formations that can dodge obstacles and reform, since each drone only needs to track a handful of neighbours. Murmurations also show the limits: a flock that turns too fast can split, and the two halves may take minutes to find each other again. The whole field in one record: many simple robots, one shared result.

Swarm Robotics Overview is a public record about The whole field in one record: many simple robots, one shared result. Swarm robotics studies large groups of robots that cooperate through local rules, without a central controller, copying ants, bees, birds and fish. The field promises systems that are robust, because losing one robot changes little; scalable, because adding robots needs no new plan; and flexible, because the same robots can do many jobs. Swarm robots are used in research labs, and the ideas are moving into warehouses, farms and disaster response, where many cheap robots beat one expensive one. The open problems are proving that a swarm will always work, keeping communication light, and moving rules from simulation to real hardware without surprises. Swarm Foraging by Robot Swarms is a public record about Collecting scattered items and bringing them home as a team. Foraging tests are used to compare swarm rules fairly, since the score is simple: items collected per hour. Swarm Aggregation by Robot Swarms

Gathering scattered robots into one cluster on command.

Swarm Aggregation by Robot Swarms is a public record about Gathering scattered robots into one cluster on command. Aggregation is the swarm task of gathering scattered robots into one cluster, and it is the first step for many jobs, from building a structure to charging together. The simplest rule is to move toward the average position of neighbours, which pulls the swarm into a ball, though it can be slow when the swarm starts far apart. Better rules use a beacon: one robot or a light marks the meeting point, and the others steer toward it, which gathers the swarm faster and more reliably. Aggregation must also avoid pile-ups: robots that arrive early must make room for latecomers, or the cluster jams. Swarm Dispersion by Robot Swarms

Spreading robots evenly across an area to watch or sense it.

Swarm Dispersion by Robot Swarms is a public record about Spreading robots evenly across an area to watch or sense it. Dispersion is the opposite of aggregation: scattered robots spread out evenly, so each one watches its own patch and the swarm covers the whole area. The rule is simple: move away from your nearest neighbour until every robot has roughly the same distance to its neighbours, which is the same mathematics as spreading electrons on a sphere. Dispersion is used for sensor coverage, where a swarm must watch a field, and for search, where a swarm must not waste two robots on the same patch. Good dispersion is measured by the largest uncovered gap: the smaller the gap, the better the spread. Swarms that keep working when individual robots fail.

Swarm Fault Tolerance by Robot Swarms is a public record about Swarms that keep working when individual robots fail. Swarm fault tolerance comes from numbers: in a swarm of a hundred robots, losing five changes the result only a little, while losing one robot from a team of two stops the mission. Swarms detect failures by watching neighbours: a robot that stops moving or stops signalling is routed around, and its work is picked up by others. The design rule is graceful degradation: the swarm should do slightly worse with each failure, never collapse suddenly, so operators can trust it in the field. Fault tolerance is tested by removing robots mid-run and measuring how much the swarm's score drops.
