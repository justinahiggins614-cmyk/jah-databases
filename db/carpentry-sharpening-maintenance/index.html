Chisel Sharpening Rank 1 · simple · sharpening · beginner

A sharp chisel cuts with light pressure and leaves a clean surface, while a dull chisel needs force, slips, and crushes wood fibers. Sharpening a chisel means flattening the back first, then honing the bevel: the back must be flat so the cutting edge is a true intersection of two planes. Hone the primary bevel at about 25 degrees, then add a microbevel about 5 degrees steeper for a durable 30 degree cutting edge. Work through the grits without skipping, and test the edge by paring end grain: a sharp chisel leaves a smooth, shiny surface with little effort.

Diagram shows a chisel bevel with primary angle 25 degrees and a microbevel 5 degrees steeper.

Specifications

Dimensions: Primary bevel 25 degrees; microbevel addition 5 degrees; total cutting angle 30 degrees

Materials: chisel (steel, n/a); whetstone (aluminum oxide, n/a); honing guide (steel, n/a)

Details

Flatten the back first: the edge is the intersection of two planes; Primary bevel: 25 degrees; Microbevel: 5 degrees steeper, total 30 degrees; Grit progression: 1000, then 4000, then 8000; Test: pare end grain cleanly with light pressure

Fasteners: none — skill guide

Tolerances: Bevel angle within 1 degree; back flat, no rounding

Load notes: No load; sharpening skill only.

Numbers check: Numbers check: angle sum 25.0 degrees + 5.0 degrees = 30.0 degrees

How to make it

  1. Flatten the chisel back on the coarsest stone until it is uniformly scratched.
  2. Set the honing guide for a 25 degree primary bevel and hone through the grits.
  3. Raise the angle by 5 degrees and hone a small microbevel on the finest stone.
  4. Remove the wire edge by stropping the back lightly.
  5. Test the edge on end grain and repeat the finest step if it drags.

Tools required

whetstones, honing guide, strop, combination square, marker pen

Safety gear

safety glasses, cut-resistant gloves, dust mask

Safety notes

Wear eye protection, keep fingers behind the cutting edge, and move the tool away from the body on the stone. Never test sharpness by running a thumb across the edge.

Plane Iron Sharpening Rank 2 · simple · sharpening · intermediate

A plane iron is sharpened like a chisel, but its edge must be straight and square across its width, or the plane will cut steps into the surface. Flatten the back, hone the bevel at about 30 degrees, and check the edge against a square after each grit: a skewed edge planes a skewed surface. A slight camber, a barely curved edge, suits smoothing planes because the corners do not dig in, but a jack plane iron can carry more camber for heavy removal. After honing, set the iron in the plane with a fine mouth opening and a thin shaving, then test on a board edge.

Materials: plane iron (steel, 1/8 in); whetstone (aluminum oxide, n/a)

Fasteners: none — skill guide

  1. Flatten the iron back on the stones until uniformly scratched.
  2. Hone the bevel at 30 degrees through the grits.
  3. Check the edge square after each grit and correct any skew.
  4. Add a slight camber for a smoothing plane if desired.
  5. Set the iron in the plane, adjust for a thin shaving, and test.

Hand Saw Sharpening Basics Rank 3 · simple · sharpening · advanced

Sharpening a hand saw means filing each tooth to a point with consistent size, set, and angle, and it is the most skill-demanding sharpening in this guide. Joint the teeth first by running a flat file over them so every tooth is the same height, then file each tooth with a triangular file sized to the tooth, keeping the file level and at the tooth's rake angle. Set, the sideways bend of alternate teeth, makes the kerf wider than the blade so the saw does not bind; too much set makes a rough, wandering cut. Beginners should practice on an old saw before filing a good one.

Diagram shows saw teeth being jointed and filed, with tooth height and set marked.

Specifications

Dimensions: Panel saw 26 in long; 8 teeth per inch; 208 teeth; file level, consistent rake

Materials: hand saw (steel, n/a); triangular file (steel, n/a)

Details

Joint teeth to equal height first; File size matched to tooth size; Keep the file level and the rake angle consistent; Set: alternate teeth bent sideways so the kerf clears the blade; Practice on an old saw before filing a good one

Fasteners: none — skill guide

Tolerances: Tooth height even within 0.005 in after jointing; set even both sides

Load notes: No load; sharpening skill only.

Numbers check: Numbers check: total count 26.0 x 8.0 = 208

How to make it

  1. Clamp the saw in a vise with the teeth just above the jaws.
  2. Joint the teeth with a flat file until every tooth shows a flat.
  3. File each tooth with the triangular file, keeping the angle consistent.
  4. Set the teeth evenly with a saw set.
  5. Test-cut scrap and correct any tooth that grabs or wanders.

Tools required

triangular files, flat file, saw set, saw vise, magnifier

Safety gear

safety glasses, cut-resistant gloves, dust mask

Safety notes

Wear eye protection, clamp the saw firmly, and file away from the body with the file handle secured.

Knife Honing Rank 4 · simple · sharpening · beginner

Honing a knife realigns the fine edge that folds over in use, and it is maintenance, not grinding: a honing steel or a fine stone straightens the edge without removing much metal. Hold the knife at its edge angle, about 15 degrees per side for a kitchen knife with a 5 degree microbevel addition for a 20 degree total, and draw the edge along the steel from heel to tip in smooth strokes, alternating sides. A knife that still drags after honing needs true sharpening on stones, because honing cannot restore an edge that is chipped or rounded. Test by slicing paper: a honed knife cuts cleanly without tearing.

Diagram shows a knife drawn along a honing steel at 15 degrees, heel to tip.

Specifications

Dimensions: Edge angle 15 degrees per side; microbevel addition 5 degrees; total 20 degrees

Materials: kitchen knife (steel, n/a); honing steel (steel, n/a)

Details

Honing realigns the edge; it removes little metal; Angle: 15 degrees per side, 5 degree microbevel addition, 20 degrees total; Alternate sides, heel to tip, smooth strokes; Honing cannot fix chips or a rounded edge; Test: clean paper slice without tearing

Fasteners: none — skill guide

Tolerances: Honing angle within 2 degrees of the edge angle

Load notes: No load; sharpening skill only.

Numbers check: Numbers check: angle sum 15.0 degrees + 5.0 degrees = 20.0 degrees

How to make it

  1. Hold the honing steel vertically with its tip planted on a board.
  2. Draw the knife down the steel at 15 degrees, heel to tip.
  3. Alternate sides with equal strokes.
  4. Test the edge on paper.
  5. If the knife still drags, sharpen it on stones instead of honing further.

Tools required

honing steel, fine whetstone (for true sharpening), cutting board

Safety gear

safety glasses, cut-resistant gloves, dust mask

Safety notes

Wear eye protection, keep the free hand clear of the blade path, and never hone toward the body.

Drill Bit Sharpening Rank 5 · simple · sharpening · intermediate

A drill bit is sharpened by grinding its two cutting lips to equal length and angle, with a standard point angle of 118 degrees for general work. Unequal lips make the bit drill oversize holes and wander, because the longer lip does all the cutting. Grind each lip against the wheel with the bit held at half the point angle to the wheel face, cool the bit in water often so the steel does not overheat and lose temper, and check both lips with a gauge. A correctly sharpened bit cuts with two even spirals of waste and needs only light feed pressure.

Diagram shows a drill point at 118 degrees with both cutting lips dimensioned.

Specifications

Dimensions: Point angle 118 degrees; bit diameter 1/4 in; cutting lip length about 0.146 in

Materials: twist drill bit (high-speed steel, 1/4 in diameter); bench grinder (aluminum oxide wheel, n/a)

Details

Point angle: 118 degrees for general work; Both lips equal length and angle; Cool in water often to protect temper; Test: two even waste spirals, light feed pressure; Overheated steel turns blue and loses hardness

Fasteners: none — skill guide

Tolerances: Lip lengths equal within 0.005 in; point angle within 2 degrees

Load notes: No load; sharpening skill only.

Numbers check: Numbers check: drill lip length for 0.250 in bit at 118.0 degrees point angle = 0.146 in

How to make it

  1. Dress the grinding wheel and set up eye protection and water.
  2. Grind one lip at half the point angle, cooling often.
  3. Grind the second lip to match the first in length and angle.
  4. Check both lips with a drill point gauge.
  5. Test-drill scrap and check the hole size and roundness.

Tools required

bench grinder, drill point gauge, water container, safety glasses

Safety gear

safety glasses, cut-resistant gloves, dust mask

Safety notes

Wear eye and face protection at the grinder, keep the tool rest close to the wheel, and never grind without the wheel guard in place. Cool the bit often; blue steel has lost its temper.

Router Bit Care Rank 6 · compounded · sharpening · intermediate

Router bits cut with carbide edges that stay sharp a long time but chip if abused, and care means cleaning, inspection, and correct handling rather than frequent grinding. Clean pitch and resin from bits with a resin remover and a brass brush, never a steel brush that can nick carbide, and inspect the edges under light: a chipped edge leaves a ridged cut and must be professionally sharpened or replaced. Store bits in their case or a rack where edges cannot knock together, and always check that the collet grips the shank fully, at least 3/4 inch of shank inside the collet, before switching the router on.

Tool Rust Removal Rank 7 · compounded · maintenance · beginner

Rust removal restores a tool without grinding away good steel: start with the gentlest method that works and step up only as needed. Light rust comes off with a Scotch-Brite pad and oil, moderate rust with fine wet-or-dry paper used with oil, and heavy rust with a vinegar soak followed by neutralizing, drying, and oiling, because vinegar left on steel invites new rust. Work through the grits 80, then 150, then 220, then 320 for a surface that can be protected, and always finish with oil or wax, because bare steel rusts again quickly in a damp shop.

Diagram shows a rusted tool surface before and after treatment through four grit stages.

Specifications

Dimensions: Grit progression 80, 150, 220, 320; finish with oil or wax; neutralize vinegar soaks

Materials: rusted tool (steel, n/a); wet-or-dry paper (silicon carbide, n/a); protective oil (camellia oil, n/a)

Details

Light rust: pad and oil; Moderate rust: fine paper with oil; Heavy rust: vinegar soak, then neutralize, dry, and oil; Grit progression: 80, 150, 220, 320, strictly ascending; Finish with oil or wax; bare steel re-rusts quickly

Fasteners: none — skill guide

Tolerances: Surface smooth and rust-free; no deep pitting ground away unnecessarily

Load notes: No load; maintenance skill only.

Numbers check: Numbers check: grit progression 80, 150, 220, 320, ratios 1.88, 1.47, 1.45, strictly ascending

How to make it

  1. Assess the rust and choose the gentlest effective method.
  2. Remove rust working through the grits from coarse to fine.
  3. If using vinegar, neutralize, rinse, and dry thoroughly.
  4. Oil or wax the clean surface immediately.
  5. Store the tool dry and recheck it after a week.

Tools required

Scotch-Brite pads, wet-or-dry paper, oil, rags, wire brush (brass)

Safety gear

safety glasses, cut-resistant gloves, dust mask

Safety notes

Wear eye protection and gloves, work in ventilation when using vinegar or solvents, and keep oily rags in a closed metal container.

Handle Repair and Replacement Rank 8 · compounded · maintenance · intermediate

A loose or cracked handle makes a tool unsafe, because the head can fly off in use, so handle work is safety work. Remove a broken handle by drilling out the old wood and driving the remainder out from the eye, fit the new handle by shaping it to the eye and seating it with the grain running along the handle length, and secure it with a wedge driven across the grain plus a metal wedge if the tool maker specifies one. A handle blank 1-1/2 inches square and 12 inches long suits many small striking tools; shape it with a spokeshave and rasp, sand it smooth, and finish it with oil so it does not dry and shrink.

Tool Storage Systems Rank 9 · compounded · maintenance · beginner

A tool storage system keeps tools findable, protected, and dry, and the best system is the one that puts each tool back in the same place every time. Wall panels and French cleats suit hand tools, drawers with dividers suit small tools and bits, and cutting edges need guards or racks so they do not knock together. Plan the wall area first: a panel 48 inches wide by 24 inches high holds a useful hand-tool set, and every tool gets an outline or label so a missing tool is obvious at a glance. Keep the storage dry, because tools stored against a damp wall rust even in a closed shop.

Saw Blade Cleaning Rank 11 · complex · maintenance · beginner

A saw blade caked with pitch and resin cuts hotter, slower, and rougher than a clean blade, and cleaning restores it without any grinding. Remove the blade from the saw first, soak it briefly in a pitch remover or a warm detergent solution, and scrub the teeth and gullets with a brass brush, never a steel brush that can dull carbide tips. Rinse, dry the blade fully so it does not rust, and lightly oil the plate before storing or refitting it. A 10 inch blade, 254 millimeters across, is the common table-saw size, and its teeth must be counted and inspected while it is out: a missing or chipped tooth means professional service.

Plane Tuning Rank 12 · complex · maintenance · intermediate

Tuning a plane means setting its parts so it cuts thin, even shavings: the iron sharp, the chipbreaker close to the edge, the mouth fine, and the sole flat. Set the chipbreaker about 1/32 inch behind the cutting edge for fine work, close the mouth so the shaving thickness nearly fills it, and check the sole with a straightedge, lapping it flat if it rocks. A mouth opening of 0.003 inch with a shaving of 0.002 inch leaves only 0.001 inch of clearance, which is the fine setting a smoothing plane wants. Adjust one thing at a time and test after each change, or the cause of any improvement is lost.

Square Calibration Checks Rank 13 · complex · maintenance · intermediate

A square is only useful while it is true, so calibration checks belong in regular maintenance: draw a line against the square, flip it, and draw again, and the lines must coincide. For a large square, the 3-4-5 check is stronger: a triangle with sides 12, 16, and 20 inches must have a true right angle, and its diagonal must measure exactly 20 inches. A combination square can be adjusted by tapping or filing its stock, but a framing square that is out of true is usually replaced or professionally trued, because bending it risks a worse error. Record the check date on the tool so the next check is not forgotten.

Oil and Wax Tool Protection Rank 14 · complex · maintenance · beginner

Oil and wax protect tool steel and wooden handles from moisture, and the rule is simple: clean the tool, apply a thin coat, and wipe it nearly dry, because a thick coat attracts dust and a missing coat invites rust. Camellia oil suits cutting edges, boiled linseed oil suits handles and bench tops, and paste wax suits cast-iron tables that must stay slick but rust-free. Apply to a surface 36 inches by 12 inches, for example a plane sole and sides, with a rag, let it sit briefly, then buff it dry. Reapply after any use in damp conditions and at least seasonally in a dry shop.

A workshop tool inventory lists every tool, its condition, and its place, and it turns lost tools and duplicate purchases into rare events. Walk the shop with a notebook, record each tool and its storage place, note anything damaged or dull, and give each storage slot a label that matches the list. Review the inventory twice a year, update it when a tool is bought or retired, and keep a copy away from the shop in case of fire or theft.

Seasonal Tool Care Rank 16 · complex · maintenance · beginner

Seasonal tool care is the twice-yearly pass that catches rust, dull edges, and dried handles before they become damage: clean every tool, sharpen what is dull, oil what is bare, and check handles and cords. Work through the grits 220, then 320, then 400, then 600 on surfaces that need refinishing, always ascending, and never jump from coarse to fine without the steps between, or deep scratches survive under a shiny finish. In damp seasons, add a wax coat to cast iron and check the shop for leaks, and in dry seasons, oil wooden handles so they do not shrink and loosen. Record the pass date so the next one is not missed.
