Morpheme

A morpheme is the smallest unit of language that carries meaning. The word unhappy contains two morphemes, un- and happy, and unhappiness contains three, un-, happy, and -ness; no smaller part of happy carries meaning on its own. Morphemes are abstract units: the same morpheme can appear in different shapes, as the plural morpheme does in cats, dogs, and horses. Root

Affix

An affix is a morpheme that attaches to a root or stem and cannot stand alone as a word. Prefixes attach before the root, as un- does in unhappy; suffixes attach after it, as -ness does in happiness; infixes sit inside it. Affixes change meaning, as un- reverses happy, or change the word's class, as -ness turns the adjective happy into the noun happiness. Prefix

A prefix is an affix placed before a root or stem. English un- in unhappy, re- in redo, and pre- in preview are prefixes: each changes the meaning of the word that follows it without changing the word's class. Some prefixes reverse meaning, some repeat an action, and some place an event in time, and a single word can carry more than one prefix. Infix

An infix is an affix placed inside a root or stem rather than before or after it. Infixes are rare in English but common elsewhere: Tagalog inserts -um- inside a verb root to mark the actor, and in some languages infixes mark tense or number. English has only playful infixes, as in abso-bloody-lutely, where an expletive is inserted inside a word for emphasis. Derivational Morphology

Derivational morphology is the word formation that builds new words from old ones. Adding -ness to happy gives happiness, a new noun; adding un- to happy gives unhappy, a new adjective. Inflectional Morphology

Inflectional morphology is the word formation that marks grammar without building a new word. English -s marks plural in cats, -ed marks past tense in walked, and -er marks comparison in taller. The word stays the same word: cats is still the noun cat, only marked as more than one, and the inflection must agree with the grammar of the sentence around it. Reduplication

Reduplication is the word formation that repeats a part of a word, or the whole word, to mark meaning. Indonesian repeats the whole word for plurality, as in buku-buku for books; Tagalog repeats the first syllable for aspect; English repeats with a vowel change in tick-tock and flip-flop. The repeated part is a copy, not a new morpheme with its own meaning. Allomorph

An allomorph is one variant shape of a single morpheme. The English plural morpheme appears as /s/ in cats, /z/ in dogs, and /ɪz/ in horses: three shapes, one morpheme, chosen by the sounds around them. Allomorphs show that morphemes are abstract units with several concrete forms, and the choice between forms is governed by rule, not by chance. Zero Morphology

Zero morphology is meaning marked by no change at all: the word stays the same while its grammar changes. English sheep is both singular and plural, and the past tense of cut is cut; the plural or the past is present in the grammar but has no spoken ending. Linguists write the missing ending as a zero morpheme, ∅, to keep the analysis regular. Suppletion

Suppletion is the use of an unrelated root for a grammatical form of a word. English go becomes went in the past tense, good becomes better, and person becomes people in the plural: the forms share no sounds, because they come from different historical roots that were joined into one paradigm. Suppletion is irregular by definition, and children must learn each suppletive form as a whole. Clitic

A clitic is a small word that leans on a neighbouring word for its pronunciation, though it is a separate word in the grammar. English 's in the dog's bone and n't in don't are clitics: they cannot stand alone, but they are not affixes either, because they attach to whole phrases rather than to single roots. Clitics sit between words and affixes, and every language has some. Morphological Typology

Morphological typology is the classification of languages by how they build words. Isolating languages use one morpheme per word, agglutinative languages stack many clear morphemes, fusional languages fuse several meanings into one ending, and polysynthetic languages pack a whole sentence into one word. Agglutination

Fusion

Latin -ō in amō marks first person, singular, present, indicative, and active all in a single vowel: the meanings are there but no piece carries just one. Morphophonology

Paradigm
