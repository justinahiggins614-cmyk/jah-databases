Formula 1 Rank 1 · simple · circuit racing · advanced

Formula 1 is the highest class of international single-seater racing, governed by the FIA, with a world championship that began in 1950 and a first championship race at Silverstone in England. Teams build their own cars to a strict technical rulebook, and each race weekend runs practice sessions, a qualifying session that sets the starting grid, and a grand prix run over a set number of laps. Points go to the top ten finishers, 25 for the winner, then 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, and 1, and the driver and team with the most points at the end of the season win the world championships. Pit stops for tyres, tyre compounds that wear at different rates, and drag reduction systems that help overtaking make strategy as important as raw speed.

History

The Formula One World Championship began in 1950, with the first championship race at Silverstone. It grew from pre-war grand prix racing into a global series run by the FIA.

Rules

Cars must fit a technical rulebook covering size, weight, power unit, and aerodynamics. Qualifying sets the grid; the race runs a set lap count, and the top ten score points from 25 down to 1.

Techniques

Drivers manage tyre wear, use drag reduction on straights, plan pit windows, and defend or attack through braking zones and corner exits.

Safety

Cars carry a halo cockpit protector, crash structures, fire-resistant clothing, and head restraints; circuits use barriers, run-off areas, and medical teams on standby.

Working demo program

POINTS = [25, 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 1]
print("Formula 1 points for the top ten finishers")
for place, pts in enumerate(POINTS, 1):
    print("Place %d: %d points" % (place, pts))
print("Winner to tenth gap: %d points" % (POINTS[0] - POINTS[-1]))
print("Top three total: %d points" % sum(POINTS[:3]))

NASCAR Rank 2 · simple · stock car racing · intermediate

NASCAR is American stock car racing, founded in 1948, in which cars that look like showroom models race door to door, mostly on oval tracks. The headline series runs long races of several hundred miles, with restarts that bunch the field back up, pit stops for fuel and tyres, and drafting, where a car following closely behind another gains speed from the slipstream. Races are often decided in the final laps, because a late caution flag can erase a big lead and set up a sprint to the finish. Drivers need car control in traffic, patience in long green-flag runs, and sharp judgement about when to pit and when to stay out.

History

NASCAR was founded in 1948 in the United States, growing out of stock car racing on dirt tracks and beaches into a national series on ovals and road courses.

Rules

Races run a set distance, cautions bunch the field for restarts, and pit stops handle fuel and tyres. Playoff-style rounds late in the season decide the champion.

Techniques

Drafting in the slipstream, managing tyre wear over long runs, and judging pit windows are core skills, along with close-quarters car control in traffic.

Safety

Cars use roll cages, head restraints, fire-resistant suits, and safer barriers; drivers wear full-face helmets and restraint systems.

Working demo program

MILES = 400
LAPS = 160
print("Example oval race")
print("Race distance: %d miles over %d laps" % (MILES, LAPS))
print("Miles per lap: %.1f" % (MILES / LAPS))
print("Fuel run example: 60 laps per tank, stops needed: %d" % (LAPS // 60))
print("Laps remaining after lap 100: %d" % (LAPS - 100))

IndyCar Rank 3 · simple · open-wheel racing · advanced

IndyCar is American open-wheel racing, headlined by the Indianapolis 500, a 500-mile race first run in 1911 at the Indianapolis Motor Speedway. The series races on ovals, road courses, and street circuits, so drivers must master high-speed oval running, where cars lap inches apart at over 200 miles per hour, and road-course precision with heavy braking and quick direction changes. Cars share a common chassis with team-set aerodynamics, and races feature pit stops for fuel and tyres, push-to-pass power boosts on road and street courses, and restarts that can change the order in seconds. The Indianapolis 500 remains the best-known single race in the series and one of the most famous races in the world.

History

The Indianapolis 500 was first run in 1911. IndyCar racing grew around it into a national series covering ovals, road courses, and street circuits.

Rules

Cars use a shared chassis with team setup choices. Road and street races offer push-to-pass boosts; all races use pit stops and restarts.

Techniques

Oval running needs steady speed in traffic and smooth lines; road courses need braking precision and quick changes of direction.

Safety

Cars use cockpit protection, crash structures, and head restraints, and ovals use safer barriers and large run-off areas.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_MILES = 500
LAPS = 200

print("Distance: %d miles over %d laps" % (DISTANCE_MILES, LAPS))
print("Miles per lap: %.1f" % (DISTANCE_MILES / LAPS))
AVG_MPH = 180
print("At %d mph average, race time is about %.2f hours" % (AVG_MPH, DISTANCE_MILES / AVG_MPH))

World Rally Championship Rank 4 · simple · rally · advanced

The World Rally Championship, founded in 1973, is rallying at world level: drivers and co-drivers race against the clock on closed public and forest roads, from snow and ice to gravel and asphalt. A rally is made of timed special stages linked by road sections, and the co-driver reads pace notes that describe every corner, crest, and hazard ahead, so the driver can commit to roads they have never raced at speed. Cars are production-based machines built to rally rules, with all-wheel drive, turbocharged engines, and heavy underbody protection. Winning takes speed, mechanical sympathy, and total trust between driver and co-driver, because one wrong note or one missed braking point can end a rally.

History

The World Rally Championship began in 1973 as the top international rally series, building on decades of long-distance road rallies.

Rules

Rallies run timed special stages plus road sections. Crews follow a schedule with service halts, and time penalties apply for lateness or rule breaches.

Techniques

Drivers read the road through pace notes, manage grip on loose surfaces, and balance speed with mechanical care over rough stages.

Safety

Cars carry roll cages, fire systems, and harnesses; crews wear helmets and fire-resistant clothing, and stages are closed to public traffic.

Working demo program

STAGES = [12.4, 18.2, 9.8, 21.5, 14.0]
print("Example rally day stage times (minutes)")
for i, t in enumerate(STAGES, 1):
    print("Stage %d: %.1f minutes" % (i, t))
print("Total stage time: %.1f minutes" % sum(STAGES))
print("Longest stage: %.1f minutes" % max(STAGES))

Motocross Rank 5 · simple · motorcycle racing · intermediate

Motocross is off-road motorcycle racing on closed dirt circuits full of jumps, whoops, banked berms, and rutted corners. Riders race in heats called motos, usually two per event, and the winner is decided by combined results, so consistency across both motos matters as much as a single fast start. Starts are critical: a good jump off the gate can put a rider into the first corner ahead of the pack, while a bad start means fighting through dust and traffic. Riders need strength, balance, and throttle control, because the bike moves underneath them constantly and the track changes every lap as ruts deepen and jumps wear.

History

Motocross grew from British off-road scrambling in the early twentieth century into a world championship sport with national series on every continent.

Rules

Riders compete in motos over a set time plus laps. Combined moto results decide the overall winner, and jump starts and course cutting are penalised.

Techniques

Starts, jump technique, berm riding, and throttle control on loose dirt are core skills, along with choosing lines as the track roughens.

Safety

Riders wear helmets, boots, body armour, and goggles; tracks are groomed, jumps are inspected, and medical teams cover every event.

Working demo program

LAPS = 16

print("Motos: %d, laps per moto: %d" % (MOTOS, LAPS))
print("Total race laps: %d" % (MOTOS * LAPS))
print("Overall points example: moto finishes 1st and 3rd")
print("Combined finishing positions: 1 + 3 = 4 (lowest total wins)")

Endurance Racing Rank 6 · compounded · circuit racing · advanced

Endurance racing tests cars and drivers over long distances, from six-hour races to the 24 Hours of Le Mans, first run in 1923. Teams of drivers share each car in stints, swapping at pit stops while mechanics change tyres, refuel, and repair damage, and the winning car is the one that covers the greatest distance in the time allowed. Different classes race together, so faster prototypes must pass slower grand touring cars safely, lap after lap, day and night. Reliability matters as much as speed: a car that spends an hour in the garage rarely wins, however fast it is on track. Night driving, changing weather, and driver fatigue make endurance racing a team contest of planning, discipline, and mechanical care.

History

The 24 Hours of Le Mans began in 1923 and became the flagship of endurance racing, with world and regional series following.

Rules

Races run a set time rather than a set distance. Driver stints are limited, classes race together, and the greatest distance covered wins.

Techniques

Stint planning, fuel saving, night driving, and safe passing through multi-class traffic are the defining skills.

Safety

Cars use crash structures, fire systems, and driver cooling; night running uses floodlit pits and strict headlight rules, with medical cover around the clock.

Working demo program

HOURS = 24
STINT_MIN = 55
print("Endurance stint planning")
print("Race length: %d hours" % HOURS)
print("Stint length: %d minutes" % STINT_MIN)
print("Stints in 24 hours: about %d" % (HOURS * 60 // STINT_MIN))
print("Three-driver rotation: each driver covers about %d hours" % (HOURS // 3))

Karting Rank 7 · compounded · grassroots racing · beginner

Karting is the grassroots of motorsport: small, open, four-wheeled karts racing on short circuits, and the starting point for most professional racing drivers. Karts have no suspension and no gearbox in the junior classes, so drivers learn pure car control, racing lines, braking points, and overtaking in a machine that responds instantly to every input. Races are short and close, with fields of twenty or more karts, and the low cost compared with car racing makes it the most accessible way into the sport. Many Formula 1 drivers, including multiple world champions, began in karting, and the discipline has its own world championships and national series.

History

Karting began in the late 1950s and spread worldwide as an affordable entry to motorsport, feeding drivers into every major car series.

Rules

Karts race in classes by age and engine. Heats and finals decide results, and contact, cutting, and jump starts are penalised.

Techniques

Racing lines, braking points, smooth steering, and slipstream overtaking are learned here before anywhere else.

Safety

Drivers wear helmets, suits, gloves, and rib protectors; karts carry bumpers and chain guards, and circuits use barriers and run-off.

print("Race time: %d seconds (%.1f minutes)" % (LAPS * LAP_SECONDS, LAPS * LAP_SECONDS / 60.0))
print("Field size example: 24 karts")

Drag Racing Rank 8 · compounded · acceleration racing · intermediate

Drag racing is acceleration in its purest form: two cars race side by side down a straight strip, usually a quarter mile, and the first across the finish line wins. Races are decided by reaction time at the start lights, called the Christmas tree, and by the car's acceleration and power delivery, with the fastest classes covering the quarter mile in under four seconds at speeds over 300 miles per hour. Because the strip is short, there is no room for error: a slow reaction, a missed gear, or a loss of traction ends the run. Classes range from street cars to purpose-built dragsters and funny cars, so the sport covers everything from weekend racers to professional teams.

History

Drag racing grew from informal street contests in the United States into an organised sport with national series and professional classes.

Rules

Cars race in pairs on a straight strip, usually a quarter mile. Reaction time counts, foul starts are penalised, and the first across the line wins.

Techniques

Launch control, throttle management for traction, and clean gear changes decide runs; staging position at the start line matters.

Safety

Cars use roll cages, fire systems, and parachutes at high speed; drivers wear fire-resistant suits and helmets, and strips have long shutdown areas.

Working demo program

print("Average speed: %.1f m/s" % (DISTANCE_M / TIME_S))
print("Average speed: %.1f km/h" % (DISTANCE_M / TIME_S * 3.6))

Touring Cars Rank 9 · compounded · circuit racing · intermediate

Touring car racing puts modified production cars on circuits in close, door-to-door competition, with fields of twenty or more cars and frequent contact. Cars are based on road models with roll cages, racing suspension, and tuned engines, and races often run in sprint format with two or three races per meeting, so a driver can recover from a bad first race. Success ballast, where winning cars carry extra weight, and reversed grids in some series keep the racing tight and unpredictable. Touring car racing is popular because fans recognise the cars on the road, and because the racing is famously hard but fair, with overtaking in almost every corner.

History

Touring car racing grew from production-car racing in the mid-twentieth century into national and world series with large, close fields.

Rules

Cars are production-based with safety and performance rules. Sprint races, success ballast, and reversed grids appear in many series.

Techniques

Close-quarters racing, defensive lines, and clean overtaking under braking are central, along with managing tyres over sprint distances.

Safety

Roll cages, fire systems, harnesses, and protective clothing are standard, and circuits use barriers and run-off.

Working demo program

print("Maximum points in a meeting: %d" % (RACES * POINTS[0]))

Superbike Racing Rank 10 · compounded · motorcycle racing · advanced

Superbike racing is production motorcycle racing at world level: bikes based on road-going superbikes, with 1000cc engines, racing on circuits in two or three races per weekend. Riders lean to extreme angles, brake later than seems possible, and race in packs where slipstreaming on straights and out-braking into corners decide positions. Electronics manage traction and wheelies, but the rider still controls throttle, braking, and body position, and small setup changes to suspension and gearing can transform a bike's behaviour. The racing is famously close, with lead changes several times per lap at the fastest circuits.

History

Superbike racing grew from production motorcycle racing into a world championship, with national series feeding it worldwide.

Rules

Bikes are production-based 1000cc machines with racing preparation rules. Weekends run multiple races, and points follow a standard scale.

Techniques

Body position, late braking, throttle control, and slipstream passing are central, along with setup feedback to the team.

Safety

Riders wear helmets, leathers with airbags, boots, and gloves; circuits use air fences, gravel traps, and medical cover.

Speedway Rank 11 · compounded · motorcycle racing · intermediate

Speedway is motorcycle racing on loose dirt ovals, with bikes that have no brakes and a single gear, sliding sideways through every corner. Races last four laps and feature four riders, with points of 3 for first, 2 for second, 1 for third, and 0 for last, and team meetings are built from a series of such heats. Riders control the slide with the throttle and their body weight, steering more with the rear wheel than the front, and races are short, violent, and spectacular. The simplicity of the bikes means rider skill dominates: throttle control, gate starts, and the ability to find grip on a changing dirt surface decide everything.

History

Speedway developed in the 1920s from dirt-track racing and became a major team sport in Britain, Poland, Australia, and beyond.

Rules

Four riders race four laps. Points are 3-2-1-0 per heat, and team meetings combine heats across the evening.

Techniques

Throttle-controlled sliding, gate starts, and finding grip on loose dirt are the core skills.

Safety

Riders wear helmets, leathers, boots, and gloves; tracks use air fences and the bikes carry no brakes by design, so starts and spacing are controlled.

Working demo program

HEATS = 15

print("Total points available: %d" % (HEATS * 6))
print("A rider winning 5 heats scores: %d points" % (5 * 3))

Drifting Rank 12 · complex · car control sport · intermediate

Drifting is a judged motorsport in which drivers deliberately slide their cars sideways through corners, linking slides into a continuous run, and are scored on line, angle, speed, and style. Competitions run in two formats: solo qualifying runs, where drivers chase a high score, and tandem battles, where a lead driver sets the line and a chase driver must mirror it as closely as possible. Cars are rear-wheel-drive machines with tuned engines, locked or limited-slip differentials, and handbrakes for initiating slides, and drivers control the slide with throttle, steering, and weight transfer. Judging rewards commitment: big angle, high speed, and a clean line close to the course markers score highest.

History

Drifting grew from Japanese mountain-road driving into an organised judged sport with national series and a world championship.

Rules

Solo qualifying sets the bracket; tandem battles pair a lead and a chase driver, judged on line, angle, speed, and proximity.

Techniques

Handbrake and clutch initiations, throttle steering, and weight transfer control the slide; tandem chasing demands mirroring the lead car.

Safety

Cars use roll cages, fire systems, and harnesses; courses use barriers and run-off, and judges and marshals control each run.

Working demo program

JUDGES = [28, 30, 27]

print("Judge scores: %s" % JUDGES)
print("Total: %d" % sum(JUDGES))
print("Average: %.1f" % (sum(JUDGES) / len(JUDGES)))
print("Maximum possible: %d" % (len(JUDGES) * 30))

Off-Road Racing Rank 13 · complex · rally raid · advanced

Off-road racing covers desert and cross-country events in which cars, trucks, and buggies race over hundreds of kilometres of sand, rock, and dried riverbeds, often with navigation by roadbook or GPS. The Dakar Rally, first run in 1979, is the best-known event: multi-day stages across deserts, with liaison sections between timed stages, and crews must manage fuel, tyres, navigation, and mechanical damage far from service. Vehicles range from lightly modified production cars to purpose-built prototypes and racing trucks, all built to survive jumps, rocks, and deep sand. Winning takes navigation skill, mechanical sympathy, and endurance, because a single navigation error or broken part can cost hours.

History

The Dakar Rally began in 1979 and became the flagship of cross-country rallying, with world championship series following.

Rules

Multi-day events combine timed stages and liaison sections. Navigation uses roadbooks, and time penalties apply for missed waypoints or lateness.

Techniques

Desert navigation, sand driving, tyre-pressure management, and mechanical care over long stages are the core skills.

Safety

Vehicles carry roll cages, fire systems, and tracking; crews wear helmets and protective clothing, and rescue helicopters cover stages.

Working demo program

Hillclimb Rank 14 · complex · time trial · intermediate

Hillclimb is a timed motorsport in which drivers race one at a time up a closed hill course, from short sprints of under a minute to long mountain courses of several kilometres, and the fastest single run wins. Courses mix tight hairpins, fast sweepers, and changing surfaces, and cars range from road cars to single-seaters and purpose-built prototypes with huge aerodynamic wings. Because each driver runs alone against the clock, there is no traffic and no drafting: the contest is purely driver, car, and hill. Hillclimb rewards commitment and precision, since one mistake costs the whole run, and the fastest cars produce some of the most spectacular speed and sound in motorsport.

History

Hillclimbing is one of the oldest motorsports, with events on public roads and purpose-built courses dating to the early twentieth century.

Rules

Drivers run alone against the clock, usually with practice runs and two or more timed runs; the fastest run wins.

Techniques

Course learning, braking precision, and commitment through fast sections decide runs; setup balances grip and power for the gradient.

Safety

Cars use roll cages and fire systems; courses use barriers, marshal posts, and medical cover, and spectators are kept behind lines.

Working demo program

Powerboat Racing Rank 15 · complex · water motorsport · advanced

Powerboat racing is motorsport on water: boats racing on closed circuits marked by buoys, in classes from small outboard boats to Formula 1 powerboats that reach speeds over 200 kilometres per hour. Formula 1 powerboats use tunnel hulls that ride on a cushion of air, with outboard engines of very high power, and races run over a set number of laps with a rolling start behind a pace boat. Handling a powerboat at speed means reading the water: wind, waves, and the wakes of other boats all change grip and trim, and drivers adjust engine trim and weight balance constantly. Crashes on water are violent, so boats use safety capsules, and rescue divers and helicopters stand by at every event.

History

Powerboat racing grew from early twentieth-century speedboat contests into world championships, with Formula 1 powerboats as the headline class.

Rules

Races run a set number of laps on buoy circuits, with rolling starts, and penalties for missed buoys or jump starts.

Techniques

Reading water conditions, trimming the engine, and choosing lines through wakes and chop are the core skills.

Safety

Boats use safety capsules and harnesses; drivers wear helmets and life jackets, and rescue divers and helicopters cover events.

Working demo program

print("Top speed example: 220 km/h")

Truck Racing Rank 16 · complex · circuit racing · intermediate

Truck racing puts racing trucks, weighing over five tonnes, on circuits in close competition, with the European championship as its headline series. Trucks use production-based cabs with racing chassis, powerful turbocharged diesel engines, and water-cooled brakes to handle the enormous weight, and races run in sprint format with several races per weekend. The spectacle is unique: huge vehicles racing door to door, braking from high speeds, and leaning through corners, yet the racing is close and disciplined, with strict rules on contact. Truck racing draws big crowds because the vehicles are familiar from the road and the racing is genuinely hard-fought.

History

Truck racing grew from road-truck competitions into a European championship with national series elsewhere.

Rules

Trucks meet weight, engine, and safety rules. Sprint races with several per weekend decide championships, and contact rules are strict.

Techniques

Braking management, weight transfer, and close-quarters discipline matter most, along with managing brake and tyre temperatures.

Safety

Trucks use roll cages, fire systems, and harnesses; circuits use barriers and run-off, and brake temperatures are monitored.

Working demo program
