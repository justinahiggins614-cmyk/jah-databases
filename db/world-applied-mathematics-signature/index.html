What Applied Mathematics Is

ID: JAH-APPMS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-applied-mathematics-signature#JAH-APPMS-000001 k=6143a49b

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a modelling ladder from a bridge to a bank, with the five-step method as the running tool.

Definition: Applied mathematics is mathematics used to solve real problems: modelling a physical system, predicting an outcome, or optimising a design. It takes the tools of pure mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, differential equations, statistics — and turns them toward physics, engineering, biology, economics, and medicine. The applied mathematician's question is not only whether a theorem is true, but whether it works, how accurate it is, and what it costs to compute.

History: Applied mathematics is as old as mathematics itself: the Egyptians and Babylonians used geometry to survey fields and astronomy to predict floods, and Archimedes used mathematics to design machines. Newton invented calculus to describe motion, and Euler applied it to ships, bridges, and the moon. In the 19th century, Fourier applied analysis to heat, Maxwell applied it to electricity, and statistics was applied to society. In the 20th century, the computer made applied mathematics a discipline of its own: numerical methods, optimisation, and simulation became central, and applied mathematics departments were founded at universities around the world. Today it is the mathematics behind engineering, climate models, medical imaging, and finance.

Key concepts: The key ideas are model, approximation, simulation, optimisation, numerical method. A model is a mathematical description of a real system: equations that capture what matters and ignore what does not. A model is judged by its predictions. A model of a bridge predicts its deflection; a model of the weather predicts tomorrow's rain. An approximation is a value close to the truth, with a known error. Applied mathematics is the art of good approximations. A simulation is a computation that imitates a real system, step by step. Weather forecasts are simulations of the atmosphere. Optimisation is finding the best choice: the cheapest design, the fastest route, or the strongest structure. A numerical method is an algorithm that computes an approximate answer. It is how computers do mathematics. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

Second, build a model: choose the variables and the equations. Third, solve the model, exactly if possible, numerically if not. Fourth, check the answer against reality: does it match measurements?. Fifth, refine the model: add what matters, drop what does not, and repeat. Worked examples: A bridge is modelled as a beam: the load is a force, the deflection is the solution of a differential equation, and the maximum deflection must stay below a limit. A weather forecast is a simulation: the atmosphere is divided into cells, the equations of fluid motion are solved in each cell, and the result is stepped forward in time. A hospital schedules its operating theatres by optimisation: the variables are the times and rooms, the constraints are the staff and equipment, and the objective is to treat the most patients. A bank prices an option by solving the Black-Scholes equation: the price is the solution of a partial differential equation.

Real applications: Engineering is applied mathematics: bridges, aeroplanes, and circuits are designed with differential equations, linear algebra, and optimisation. Medicine uses applied mathematics: medical imaging reconstructs pictures from scans with linear algebra, and drug doses are modelled with differential equations. Climate science is applied mathematics: climate models are simulations of the atmosphere and oceans, solved on supercomputers. Finance is applied mathematics: options are priced with stochastic calculus, and portfolios are optimised with linear algebra.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is the difference between pure and applied mathematics? Pure mathematics studies mathematics for its own sake; applied mathematics studies it for what it can do. The boundary is porous: today's pure theorem is tomorrow's applied tool. Is a model the truth? No: a model is a simplification, and it is judged by its predictions. A good model is wrong in ways that do not matter for the question asked. Why approximate? Because exact solutions are rare: most real problems have no closed form, so the answer must be computed numerically, with a known error. Do I need a computer? For most applied mathematics, yes: the equations are too large to solve by hand, and the computer is the laboratory of the applied mathematician.

Mathematical Modelling

ID: JAH-APPMS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-applied-mathematics-signature#JAH-APPMS-000002 k=5570edf7

Definition: A mathematical model is a set of equations, rules, or algorithms that describes a real system well enough to predict its behaviour. Building a model means choosing what to include and what to ignore: a model of a falling ball includes gravity and air resistance, but not the colour of the ball. A good model is simple enough to solve and accurate enough to trust, and it is tested against measurements. A model is a simplification: it keeps what matters for the question and drops the rest.

History: Mathematical modelling is ancient: the Babylonians modelled the moon's motion with arithmetic, and Ptolemy's model of the planets, from the 2nd century CE, predicted eclipses for a thousand years. Kepler's laws of 1609 and Newton's law of gravitation of 1687 replaced Ptolemy's model with a simpler and more accurate one. In the 19th century, Maxwell modelled electricity and magnetism, and in the 20th, the Lotka-Volterra equations modelled predator and prey. In the 1970s, the Club of Rome's Limits to Growth modelled the world economy, and today models are used for epidemics, climate, traffic, and finance, and the field has its own journals and societies.

Key concepts: The key ideas are model, variable, parameter, validation, sensitivity. A model is a mathematical description of a system. It is judged by its predictions, not by its realism. A variable is a quantity that changes: time, position, temperature, or population. A model names its variables. A parameter is a fixed quantity in a model, like the gravitational constant. It is measured, not predicted. Validation is testing a model against measurements. A model that fails validation must be revised. A weather model is validated by forecasting the past: if it predicts yesterday's weather from the day before, it is trusted. Sensitivity is how much the output changes when an input changes. A sensitive model needs accurate inputs. A chaotic system is extremely sensitive: a tiny change in the input grows into a large change in the output. Worked examples: A falling ball is modelled by the equation dv/dt = g - kv: gravity pulls down with acceleration g = 9.8 m/s^2, and air resistance slows it in proportion to its velocity, with constant k. Without air resistance, the ball falls 4.9 metres in one second, since (1/2)gt^2 = 4.9. With air resistance, the ball approaches a terminal velocity of g/k: for k = 0.5, that is 19.6 m/s. The model is validated by dropping a ball and timing it: if the measured times match the model, the model is trusted. A predator-prey model predicts that the two populations oscillate: when the prey are many, the predators grow, and when the predators are many, the prey shrink.

Real applications: Epidemiology models disease: the SIR model predicts how an epidemic spreads, and it guides public health decisions. Climate science models the atmosphere: general circulation models predict temperature and rainfall, and they are validated against the past. Engineering models structures: a bridge is modelled as a beam, and the model predicts whether it will stand. Economics models markets: supply and demand are modelled with equations, and the models predict prices and quantities.

Common questions and misconceptions: What makes a good model? One that answers the question asked, with the fewest assumptions: simple enough to solve, accurate enough to trust. A model that cannot be solved is not useful: the art is finding the simplest model that answers the question. Can a model be too simple? Yes: a model that ignores what matters gives wrong predictions. The art is knowing what matters. What is validation? Testing the model against measurements it was not built from. A model that fits only its own data is not validated. Are models ever finished? Rarely: a model is revised as new data arrives. The Newtonian model of the planets was revised by relativity, and climate models are revised every year.

Dimensional Analysis

ID: JAH-APPMS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-applied-mathematics-signature#JAH-APPMS-000003 k=253d5bcf

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a units ladder from a pendulum to a fireball, with Buckingham's pi theorem as the running tool.

Definition: Dimensional analysis is the study of units: every physical quantity has dimensions — length, mass, time — and an equation must balance them. It is a check on any calculation: if the dimensions do not match, the answer is wrong. It also predicts the form of a formula: the period of a pendulum must depend on length and gravity, not on mass, because only length and gravity give the dimension of time.

History: Dimensional analysis was used implicitly by Fourier in 1822, who noted that the dimensions of a quantity must be consistent, and it was formalised by Rayleigh in the 1890s and by Buckingham in 1914, whose pi theorem says that a physical law can be written in terms of dimensionless groups. The theorem was used by Taylor in 1950 to estimate the energy of the first atomic bomb from a photograph of the fireball, and it is now a standard tool of physics and engineering, taught as the first check on any calculation.

Key concepts: The key ideas are dimension, unit, dimensionless, Buckingham pi theorem, scaling. A dimension is a kind of quantity: length, mass, time, electric current. Every physical quantity has dimensions. A unit is a standard amount of a dimension: the metre for length, the second for time, the kilogram for mass. A dimensionless quantity has no dimensions: a ratio of two lengths, or an angle in radians. Pi is dimensionless. The pi theorem says that a physical law with n variables and k fundamental dimensions can be written with n - k dimensionless groups. Scaling is how a quantity changes with size: the volume of a cube grows as the cube of its side, and the surface as the square. Sixth, check the units of the final answer: they must be the units of the quantity wanted. Worked examples: The period of a pendulum depends on its length L and gravity g: length has dimension L, gravity has dimension L/T^2, and the period has dimension T. The only combination with dimension T is sqrt(L/g), so the period must be proportional to sqrt(L/g). For L = 1 metre and g = 9.8 m/s^2, sqrt(1/9.8) = 0.32 seconds, and the full formula gives 2pi times that, which is 2.01 seconds. The energy of an explosion can be estimated from the radius R of the fireball, the time t, and the air density rho: the only combination with the dimension of energy is rho R^5/t^2, and Taylor used it to estimate the atomic bomb's energy from a photograph.

Real applications: Physics uses dimensional analysis to check every equation: a formula with the wrong dimensions is wrong, however plausible it looks. Engineering scales with dimensions: a model aeroplane in a wind tunnel is scaled by dimensionless numbers, like the Reynolds number. Biology scales with dimensions: the metabolic rate of an animal grows as the 3/4 power of its mass, a scaling law. Fluid mechanics is organised by dimensionless numbers: the Reynolds number, the Mach number, and the Froude number decide the flow regime.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why must dimensions balance? Because an equation must hold in any system of units: if the left side is a length and the right side is a time, changing the unit of length would break the equation. What is a dimensionless number for? It captures the physics without the units: the Reynolds number tells whether a flow is laminar or turbulent, whatever the size of the system. Can dimensional analysis give the exact formula? No: it gives the form, up to a dimensionless constant. The pendulum's period is 2pi sqrt(L/g), and dimensional analysis gives sqrt(L/g) but not the 2pi. How did Taylor estimate the bomb's energy? From a photograph: he measured the fireball's radius over time, and dimensional analysis gave E proportional to rho R^5/t^2. The estimate was accurate.

Differential Equations in Applications

ID: JAH-APPMS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-applied-mathematics-signature#JAH-APPMS-000004 k=54d282fd

Definition: A differential equation in applications is a law of change: it says how a quantity changes, and its solution is the quantity's history. Newton's second law is a differential equation; so is the heat equation, the wave equation, and the equation of a population. Applied mathematics solves these equations — exactly when possible, numerically when not — and uses the solutions to predict, design, and control.

History: Differential equations were invented by Newton and Leibniz in the 1670s, to describe motion: the equation of a falling body is the first differential equation. In the 18th century, Euler, Bernoulli, and Lagrange solved the equations of mechanics, and in the 19th, Fourier solved the heat equation and Maxwell the equations of electromagnetism. In the 20th century, the computer made numerical solution routine: finite differences, finite elements, and spectral methods solve equations that no one can solve by hand. Today differential equations are solved for weather, aircraft, bridges, epidemics, and the climate.

Key concepts: The key ideas are differential equation, initial condition, ordinary differential equation, partial differential equation, numerical solution. A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives: dy/dt = ky says that y grows in proportion to itself. The initial condition is the value at the start. It picks one solution from the family of solutions. An ordinary differential equation has derivatives in one variable, usually time. The equation of a falling body is ordinary. A partial differential equation has derivatives in several variables, like time and space. The heat equation is partial. A numerical solution approximates the solution step by step, on a computer. It is how most real equations are solved. The weather forecast is a numerical solution: the atmosphere is divided into cells and the equations are stepped forward in time. Third, solve it exactly, if the equation allows, or numerically. Fourth, check the solution against measurements. Fifth, estimate the accuracy: how far could the numerical solution be from the truth?. Sixth, use the solution to predict, design, or control. Worked examples: A population growing at 2 percent per year obeys dy/dt = 0.02y, whose solution is y = y0 e^(0.02t): a population of 1,000 becomes 1,000 times e^0.2 = 1,221 after 10 years. A cooling cup of coffee obeys Newton's law of cooling: the temperature difference from the room decays exponentially, with a half-life of about 10 minutes. A spring obeys m d^2x/dt^2 = -kx, whose solution is a sine wave with frequency sqrt(k/m). The heat equation says that temperature spreads by diffusion: a hot spot in a rod spreads out, and its width grows as the square root of time.

Real applications: Physics is differential equations: motion, heat, waves, and fields are all described by them, and their solutions are the predictions of physics. Engineering designs with differential equations: the deflection of a beam, the vibration of a bridge, and the flow in a pipe are all solutions of differential equations. Biology models with differential equations: populations, epidemics, and the spread of a drug through the body are all differential equations. Finance prices with differential equations: the Black-Scholes equation is a partial differential equation, and its solution is the price of an option.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why are differential equations so common? Because the laws of nature are laws of change: they say how a quantity changes, and a differential equation is the mathematical form of a law of change. What is the difference between ordinary and partial? Ordinary equations have one independent variable, usually time; partial equations have several, like time and space, and they describe fields. When is a numerical solution needed? When the equation has no closed form, which is most of the time: the weather, a turbulent flow, and a complex structure all need numerical solution. What is a boundary condition? A condition at the edge of the domain: the temperature at the end of a rod, or the displacement at the end of a beam. It picks the solution, like an initial condition picks it in time.

Linear Algebra in Applications

Definition: Linear algebra in applications is the mathematics of many variables at once: a system of linear equations, a network, a picture, or a dataset is a matrix, and solving, transforming, and analysing matrices is how the problem is solved. Google's PageRank is an eigenvector; a photograph is a matrix; a regression is a least-squares problem. Linear algebra is the most used mathematics in computing.

History: Linear algebra began with the solving of simultaneous equations, in China 2,000 years ago and in Europe in the 17th century, when Leibniz and Cramer wrote the first determinants. In the 19th century, Cayley introduced matrices, and Grassmann and Peano introduced vector spaces. In the 20th century, linear algebra became the language of quantum mechanics, statistics, and computing: the first computers solved linear systems, and the first algorithms were matrix algorithms. Today linear algebra is everywhere: search engines, image processing, machine learning, and structural engineering are all linear algebra.

Key concepts: The key ideas are matrix, vector, eigenvector, least squares, singular value decomposition. A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers. A photograph is a matrix of pixel values; a network is a matrix of connections. A vector is a list of numbers, or a direction with a magnitude. Forces, velocities, and data points are vectors. An eigenvector of a matrix is a vector whose direction is unchanged by the matrix: the matrix only scales it. PageRank is an eigenvector. Least squares is the method of fitting a line to data: it minimises the sum of the squared errors, and it is solved by linear algebra. The singular value decomposition writes a matrix as a product of three matrices. It compresses images and finds the structure of data. Worked examples: A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels is a matrix with a million entries. Yes: a matrix with a million rows needs a supercomputer, and the algorithms must be efficient. Sparse matrices — mostly zeros — are solved with special methods.

Optimisation

ID: JAH-APPMS-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-applied-mathematics-signature#JAH-APPMS-000006 k=e9494d4f

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a best-choice ladder from a fence to a neural network, with the square field as the running example.

Definition: Optimisation is the mathematics of the best: given a goal and constraints, find the choice that maximises or minimises it. The goal is an objective function; the constraints are equations and inequalities. The methods range from calculus — set the derivative to zero — to linear programming, to the algorithms that train machine learning models. Optimisation is the mathematics of design, scheduling, and decision.

History: Optimisation is ancient: the isoperimetric problem — the shape enclosing the most area with a given perimeter — was known to the Greeks, who knew the answer is a circle. In the 17th century, Fermat and Newton used calculus to find maxima and minima, and in the 18th, Lagrange introduced multipliers for constrained problems. In the 1940s, Dantzig invented the simplex method for linear programming, to plan the US Air Force's logistics, and in the 1950s, Kuhn and Tucker generalised it. In the 20th century, optimisation became the mathematics of operations research, and in the 21st, it became the mathematics of machine learning: training a model is optimising a loss function.

Key concepts: The key ideas are objective function, constraint, linear programming, Lagrange multiplier, gradient descent. The objective function is the quantity to maximise or minimise: the profit, the cost, the error, or the time. A constraint is a condition the choice must satisfy: the budget, the capacity, or the laws of physics. Linear programming optimises a linear objective under linear constraints. It is solved by the simplex method. A Lagrange multiplier is a number that measures how much the optimum changes when a constraint is relaxed. It is the price of the constraint.
