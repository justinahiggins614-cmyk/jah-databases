What Applied Mathematics Is

Applied mathematics is mathematics used to solve real problems: modelling a physical system, predicting an outcome, or optimising a design. It takes the tools of pure mathematics — calculus, linear algebra, differential equations, statistics — and turns them toward physics, engineering, biology, economics, and medicine. The applied mathematician's question is not only whether a theorem is true, but whether it works, how accurate it is, and what it costs to compute.

A bridge is modelled as a beam: the load is a force, the deflection is the solution of a differential equation, and the maximum deflection must stay below a limit. A weather forecast is a simulation: the atmosphere is divided into cells, the equations of fluid motion are solved in each cell, and the result is stepped forward in time. A hospital schedules its operating theatres by optimisation: the variables are the times and rooms, the constraints are the staff and equipment, and the objective is to treat the most patients. A bank prices an option by solving the Black-Scholes equation: the price is the solution of a partial differential equation.

Mathematical Modelling

A mathematical model is a set of equations, rules, or algorithms that describes a real system well enough to predict its behaviour. Building a model means choosing what to include and what to ignore: a model of a falling ball includes gravity and air resistance, but not the colour of the ball. A good model is simple enough to solve and accurate enough to trust, and it is tested against measurements. A model is a simplification: it keeps what matters for the question and drops the rest.

A falling ball is modelled by the equation dv/dt = g - kv: gravity pulls down with acceleration g = 9.8 m/s^2, and air resistance slows it in proportion to its velocity, with constant k. Without air resistance, the ball falls 4.9 metres in one second, since (1/2)gt^2 = 4.9. With air resistance, the ball approaches a terminal velocity of g/k: for k = 0.5, that is 19.6 m/s. The model is validated by dropping a ball and timing it: if the measured times match the model, the model is trusted. A predator-prey model predicts that the two populations oscillate: when the prey are many, the predators grow, and when the predators are many, the prey shrink.

Dimensional Analysis

Dimensional analysis is the study of units: every physical quantity has dimensions — length, mass, time — and an equation must balance them. It is a check on any calculation: if the dimensions do not match, the answer is wrong. It also predicts the form of a formula: the period of a pendulum must depend on length and gravity, not on mass, because only length and gravity give the dimension of time.

The period of a pendulum depends on its length L and gravity g: length has dimension L, gravity has dimension L/T^2, and the period has dimension T. The only combination with dimension T is sqrt(L/g), so the period must be proportional to sqrt(L/g). For L = 1 metre and g = 9.8 m/s^2, sqrt(1/9.8) = 0.32 seconds, and the full formula gives 2pi times that, which is 2.01 seconds. The energy of an explosion can be estimated from the radius R of the fireball, the time t, and the air density rho: the only combination with the dimension of energy is rho R^5/t^2, and Taylor used it to estimate the atomic bomb's energy from a photograph.

Differential Equations in Applications

A differential equation in applications is a law of change: it says how a quantity changes, and its solution is the quantity's history. Newton's second law is a differential equation; so is the heat equation, the wave equation, and the equation of a population. Applied mathematics solves these equations — exactly when possible, numerically when not — and uses the solutions to predict, design, and control.

A population growing at 2 percent per year obeys dy/dt = 0.02y, whose solution is y = y0 e^(0.02t): a population of 1,000 becomes 1,000 times e^0.2 = 1,221 after 10 years. A cooling cup of coffee obeys Newton's law of cooling: the temperature difference from the room decays exponentially, with a half-life of about 10 minutes. A spring obeys m d^2x/dt^2 = -kx, whose solution is a sine wave with frequency sqrt(k/m). The heat equation says that temperature spreads by diffusion: a hot spot in a rod spreads out, and its width grows as the square root of time.

Linear Algebra in Applications

Linear algebra in applications is the mathematics of many variables at once: a system of linear equations, a network, a picture, or a dataset is a matrix, and solving, transforming, and analysing matrices is how the problem is solved. Google's PageRank is an eigenvector; a photograph is a matrix; a regression is a least-squares problem. Linear algebra is the most used mathematics in computing.

A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels is a matrix with a million entries. Compressing it with the singular value decomposition keeps the largest 50 singular values and discards the rest, reducing a million numbers to 100,000 — a factor of 10 — with little visible loss. A system of two equations, 2x + y = 5 and x - y = 1, has the solution x = 2, y = 1: check 2 times 2 + 1 = 5, and 2 - 1 = 1. Google's PageRank is the eigenvector of the web's link matrix: the page with the largest entry is the most important. A least-squares fit to the points (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 5) is the line y = 2x + 1, with zero error, because the points are exactly on the line.

Optimisation

Optimisation is the mathematics of the best: given a goal and constraints, find the choice that maximises or minimises it. The goal is an objective function; the constraints are equations and inequalities. The methods range from calculus — set the derivative to zero — to linear programming, to the algorithms that train machine learning models. Optimisation is the mathematics of design, scheduling, and decision.

A farmer has 100 metres of fence to enclose a rectangular field: the area is x(100 - 2x)/2, where x is the length, and the derivative is zero when x = 25, so the field is 25 by 25 metres, a square, with area 625 square metres. A delivery company minimises its route: the shortest route visiting 10 stops is a travelling salesman problem, and the optimum is found by search. A linear programme maximises profit subject to the constraints of labour and materials: the simplex method moves from corner to corner of the feasible region, and the optimum is at a corner. Training a neural network minimises a loss function by gradient descent: the parameters are adjusted downhill, step by step, until the loss stops falling.

Calculus in Applications

Calculus in applications is the mathematics of change: the derivative measures a rate, the integral measures an accumulation, and together they solve the problems of motion, growth, and optimisation. The velocity is the derivative of position; the distance is the integral of velocity. Calculus is the language in which the laws of nature are written, and the tool with which they are solved.

A ball is thrown upward with velocity 20 m/s: its height is h = 20t - 4.9t^2, its velocity is dh/dt = 20 - 9.8t, and it reaches its highest point when the velocity is zero, at t = 2.04 seconds, with height 20.4 metres. The distance travelled by a car whose velocity is v(t) = 10 + 2t m/s over 10 seconds is the integral of v from 0 to 10, which is 100 + 100 = 200 metres. The area under the curve y = x^2 from 0 to 1 is the integral, which is 1/3. The maximum of the function f(x) = x(10 - x) is at x = 5, where the derivative 10 - 2x is zero, and the maximum value is 25.

Fourier Analysis

Fourier analysis decomposes a signal into sines and cosines: any periodic function can be written as a sum of waves, and the Fourier transform tells which waves, and how much of each. It is the mathematics of sound, images, and communication: a musical note is a sum of harmonics, a photograph is a sum of frequencies, and a radio signal is a sum of waves. The fast Fourier transform, invented in 1965, computes the transform in n log n steps, and it is one of the most used algorithms in the world.

A square wave of frequency 1 Hz is the sum of sines at 1, 3, 5, 7, ... Hz, with amplitudes 1, 1/3, 1/5, 1/7, ... : the first three terms already give a recognisable square wave. A musical note at 440 Hz, concert A, has harmonics at 880, 1,320, and 1,760 Hz, and the harmonics decide whether it sounds like a violin or a flute. A photograph is compressed by keeping the low frequencies and discarding the high ones: a JPEG keeps about 10 percent of the coefficients and the picture still looks right. The fast Fourier transform of a million samples takes about 20 million operations, since n log2 n with n = 1,000,000 is about 20,000,000, while the direct transform would take a trillion.

Numerical Methods

Numerical methods are algorithms that compute approximate answers to mathematical problems: solving equations, integrating functions, and solving differential equations, all on a computer. They are needed because most real problems have no exact solution, and because the computer is the only way to handle the size of real problems. A numerical method is judged by its accuracy, its speed, and its stability: whether small errors grow or shrink.

Newton's method finds the square root of 2: starting from 1.5, the next guess is (1.5 + 2/1.5)/2 = 1.4167, and the next is (1.4167 + 2/1.4167)/2 = 1.4142, which is correct to 4 decimal places after two steps. Simpson's rule integrates x^2 from 0 to 1 with two intervals: (1/6)(0 + 4 times 0.25 + 1) = 1/3, which is exact, because Simpson's rule is exact for polynomials up to degree 3. Euler's method solves dy/dt = y with step 0.1: after 10 steps, y is 2.5937, while the exact answer e is 2.7183, an error of 0.12, which shrinks as the step shrinks.

Statistics and Data Analysis

Statistics is the mathematics of data: collecting it, summarising it, and drawing conclusions from it, with a measure of uncertainty. Data analysis is the practice: cleaning the data, plotting it, fitting models, and testing hypotheses. The tools are the mean and the standard deviation, the correlation and the regression, the confidence interval and the p-value. Statistics is how science turns measurements into knowledge.

The heights of 5 people are 170, 175, 180, 185, and 190 cm: the mean is 180 cm, and the standard deviation is about 7.9 cm. The correlation between height and weight in a sample is typically about 0.5: taller people tend to be heavier, but not always. A regression of weight on height predicts that each extra centimetre of height adds about 0.8 kg of weight. A survey of 1,000 people finds that 52 percent support a proposal: the 95 percent confidence interval is about 49 to 55 percent, because the standard error is sqrt(0.52 times 0.48/1000), which is about 1.6 percent.

Applied Mathematics in Physics

Applied mathematics in physics is the mathematics that describes the physical world: the differential equations of motion, the linear algebra of quantum mechanics, the Fourier analysis of waves, and the statistics of measurement. Physics is the oldest customer of applied mathematics, and the two have grown together: Newton invented calculus for physics, and Einstein's relativity needed the tensor calculus that mathematicians had just built.

A pendulum of length 1 metre has a period of 2pi sqrt(1/9.8) = 2.01 seconds. A wave on a string obeys the wave equation, and its speed is sqrt(tension/density): a string with tension 100 N and density 0.01 kg/m has a wave speed of sqrt(100/0.01) = 100 m/s. The energy levels of a hydrogen atom are -13.6/n^2 eV: the first level is -13.6 eV, the second is -3.4 eV, and the difference, 10.2 eV, is the energy of the photon emitted when the atom falls from the second level to the first. The heat in a rod spreads by the heat equation: a hot spot of width 1 cm spreads to a width of about sqrt(2 times the diffusivity times the time), which for steel is about 1 cm in 10 seconds.

Applied Mathematics in Engineering

Applied mathematics in engineering is the mathematics that designs and builds: the differential equations of structures and fluids, the linear algebra of circuits and control, the optimisation of designs, and the statistics of quality. An engineer uses mathematics to predict whether a design will work, before it is built, and to make it better, cheaper, and safer. Engineering is applied mathematics with a deadline.

A steel beam 10 metres long, carrying a load of 1,000 N at its centre, deflects by an amount given by the formula PL^3/(48EI): with P = 1,000 N, L = 10 m, E = 200 GPa, and I = 0.0001 m^4, the deflection is 1,000 times 1,000/(48 times 200 x 10^9 times 0.0001) = 0.00104 m, about 1 mm. A control system keeps a room at 20 degrees: the thermostat measures the temperature, compares it with the target, and switches the heater on or off — that is feedback. A signal with noise is filtered by keeping the low frequencies and discarding the high ones: the Fourier transform separates the signal from the noise. The reliability of a system with two independent components, each with reliability 0.99, is 1 - (1 - 0.99)^2 = 0.9999, if either component suffices.

Applied Mathematics in Biology

Applied mathematics in biology is the mathematics of living things: the differential equations of populations, the combinatorics of genomes, the statistics of trials, and the networks of proteins. Biology is the newest customer of applied mathematics, and the fastest growing: the genome is a string of three billion letters, and reading it is a mathematical problem. Mathematics turns biological data into understanding.

A population of bacteria doubles every 20 minutes: after 2 hours, 6 doublings, it is 2^6 = 64 times as large. The SIR model predicts that an epidemic peaks when the number of susceptible people falls to the point where each infected person infects fewer than one other: the peak is when S/N = 1/R0, where R0 is the basic reproduction number. For R0 = 3, the peak is when one third of the population is still susceptible. The human genome has about 3 billion base pairs: comparing two genomes means aligning two strings of 3 billion letters, and the alignment is a problem in combinatorics. A phylogenetic tree of 100 species has 99 internal branches: the tree is built from the differences between the genomes.

Applied Mathematics in Economics

Applied mathematics in economics is the mathematics of choice and markets: the optimisation of consumers and firms, the equilibrium of supply and demand, the statistics of data, and the stochastic calculus of finance. Economics is a mathematical science: its theories are equations, its predictions are computations, and its data are analysed statistically. The mathematics of economics is the mathematics of decisions under scarcity.

A market has demand Q = 100 - 2P and supply Q = 20 + 2P: the equilibrium is where they are equal, so 100 - 2P = 20 + 2P, giving P = 20 and Q = 60. A consumer with a budget of 100, facing prices 10 and 5, maximises utility by spending where the marginal utility per pound is equal for both goods. A prisoner's dilemma has a Nash equilibrium where both players defect, even though both would be better off cooperating: the equilibrium is not the best outcome. A share price follows a random walk: its logarithm changes by a random amount each day, with a mean and a standard deviation, and the Black-Scholes formula prices an option from that random walk.

Applied Mathematics in Medicine

Applied mathematics in medicine is the mathematics of the body: the differential equations of a drug's concentration, the statistics of a clinical trial, the linear algebra of a scan, and the models of an epidemic. Medicine is becoming a mathematical science: the data are large, the decisions are quantitative, and the mathematics is what turns data into treatment. The applied mathematician in medicine works with doctors, not instead of them.

A drug is given by injection, and its concentration in the blood decays exponentially: if the half-life is 4 hours, then after 8 hours the concentration is a quarter of the initial value. A clinical trial with 1,000 patients, 500 on the drug and 500 on a placebo, finds that 100 on the drug recover and 80 on the placebo recover: the difference is 4 percent, and the standard error is about 2.8 percent, so the 95 percent confidence interval for the difference is about -1.5 to 9.5 percent, which includes zero — the trial does not prove the drug works. A CT scan measures the absorption of X-rays along many lines, and the image is reconstructed by solving a large linear system: the Radon transform is inverted. The SIR model predicts that an epidemic with R0 = 3 will infect about 94 percent of the population if nothing is done.

Control Theory

Control theory is the mathematics of making a system behave as wanted: a thermostat keeps a room at a set temperature, an autopilot keeps an aeroplane on course, and a robot arm follows a path. The central idea is feedback: measure the output, compare it with the target, and adjust the input to correct the error. Control theory is the mathematics of feedback, stability, and design.

A thermostat keeps a room at 20 degrees: it measures the temperature, and if it is below 20, it switches the heater on, and if it is above, it switches it off. A PID controller for a robot arm adjusts the motor in proportion to the error, the accumulated error, and the rate of change of the error: the three terms together give a fast, accurate, and stable response. A cruise control keeps a car at 70 mph: it measures the speed, and if the car slows on a hill, it opens the throttle. The Kalman filter estimates the position of a satellite from noisy GPS measurements: it combines the measurements with a model of the motion, and the estimate is better than either alone.

Fluid Dynamics

Fluid dynamics is the mathematics of flowing liquids and gases: the Navier-Stokes equations describe the velocity, pressure, and temperature of a fluid, and their solutions predict the lift of a wing, the drag of a car, and the weather. The equations are nonlinear and hard to solve, so fluid dynamics is mostly numerical: the flow is computed on a grid, step by step, on a supercomputer. It is one of the most important and most difficult parts of applied mathematics.

The Reynolds number of a flow is the density times the velocity times the length, divided by the viscosity: for water flowing at 1 m/s in a pipe of diameter 0.1 m, with density 1,000 kg/m^3 and viscosity 0.001 Pa s, the Reynolds number is 1,000 times 1 times 0.1/0.001 = 100,000, which is turbulent, since the transition is at about 2,300. Bernoulli's principle says that the pressure plus half the density times the velocity squared is constant along a streamline: a wing's top surface has a faster flow, so a lower pressure, and the difference is the lift. A car at 30 m/s has a drag force of about half the density times the velocity squared times the drag coefficient times the area: for air density 1.2 kg/m^3, a drag coefficient of 0.3, and an area of 2 m^2, the drag is 0.5 times 1.2 times 900 times 0.3 times 2 = 324 N.

Wave Equations

A wave is a disturbance that travels: a sound wave travels through air, a light wave through space, and a water wave across the sea. The wave equation is a partial differential equation that describes how a wave moves: the second derivative in time equals the wave speed squared times the second derivative in space. Its solutions are sines, cosines, and their sums, and they describe every wave in nature.

A guitar string of length 0.65 m, fixed at both ends, vibrates at frequencies that are multiples of the fundamental: the fundamental is v/(2L), where v is the wave speed. For a wave speed of 400 m/s, the fundamental is 400/(2 times 0.65) = 308 Hz, and the harmonics are 616, 924, and 1,232 Hz. The speed of sound in air is about 343 m/s: a note of 440 Hz has a wavelength of 343/440 = 0.78 m. An earthquake wave travels at about 5,000 m/s through rock: a wave from 100 km away arrives after 20 seconds. Two waves in phase add: two sounds of the same pitch and amplitude sound twice as loud, and two waves out of phase cancel, which is how noise-cancelling headphones work.

Chaos Theory

Chaos theory is the study of systems that are deterministic but unpredictable: a small change in the starting point grows exponentially, so that the future cannot be forecast far ahead. The weather is chaotic: a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil can, in principle, change the weather in Texas. Chaotic systems are described by simple equations, but their behaviour is complex, and they are studied with geometry: the strange attractor.

The Lorenz equations have a parameter, the Rayleigh number: at low values the system settles to a fixed point, and at high values it is chaotic, tracing a butterfly-shaped attractor. The logistic map x -> 3.9 x (1 - x), starting from 0.5, gives 0.975, then 0.095, then 0.335, then 0.869, and the sequence never repeats: it is chaotic. Two weather forecasts starting from temperatures that differ by 0.01 degrees will agree for a few days, then diverge completely: the prediction horizon of the weather is about 10 days. The Lyapunov exponent of the Lorenz system is about 0.9: differences grow by a factor of e^0.9, about 2.5, per unit time.

Game Theory

Game theory is the mathematics of strategic choice: each player's best move depends on what the others do, and the theory predicts the outcome. A game has players, strategies, and payoffs, and the Nash equilibrium is the set of strategies where no player can do better by changing alone. Game theory is used in economics, biology, politics, and computer science, wherever rational agents interact.

In the prisoner's dilemma, two prisoners can cooperate (stay silent) or defect (confess): if both cooperate, each gets 1 year; if one defects, the defector goes free and the other gets 3 years; if both defect, each gets 2 years. Each player's best response is to defect, whatever the other does, so the Nash equilibrium is mutual defection, with 2 years each, even though mutual cooperation, with 1 year each, would be better for both. In a game of rock-paper-scissors, the Nash equilibrium is to play each move with probability 1/3: any other strategy can be exploited. In an auction, the Nash equilibrium of a first-price auction is to bid below your value: the bid is a balance between winning and paying too much.

Operations Research

Operations research is the mathematics of running things well: scheduling, routing, inventory, and queuing, solved with optimisation, statistics, and simulation. It was born in the Second World War, when mathematicians planned radar, convoys, and bombing, and it is now the mathematics of logistics, airlines, hospitals, and factories. Operations research turns a practical problem into a mathematical model, solves it, and implements the answer.

A factory makes two products, A and B: A gives a profit of 3 per unit and uses 1 hour of labour, B gives 2 per unit and uses 2 hours, and 100 hours are available. The linear programme maximises 3A + 2B subject to A + 2B ≤ 100: the optimum is at a corner, and it is A = 100, B = 0, with profit 300, or A = 0, B = 50, with profit 100 — so the optimum is to make only A, with profit 300. A queue with arrivals at 10 per hour and service at 12 per hour has a utilisation of 10/12 = 0.83, and the average number in the system is 0.83/(1 - 0.83) = 5. A delivery route visiting 10 stops has 9!/2 = 181,440 possible routes, and the shortest is found by search or by heuristics.

Applied Mathematics in Computing

Applied mathematics in computing is the mathematics that makes computers work: the algorithms that sort and search, the linear algebra of graphics and machine learning, the Fourier analysis of signals, the statistics of data, and the cryptography that secures it. Computing is applied mathematics made physical: every program is mathematics, and the limits of computing are the theorems of mathematics. The applied mathematician in computing designs the algorithms and analyses their speed.

Sorting a million numbers by merge sort takes about 20 million comparisons: n log2 n with n = 1,000,000 is about 20,000,000. A photograph is compressed by the discrete cosine transform: a JPEG keeps about 10 percent of the coefficients. A neural network with a million parameters is trained by gradient descent: the loss is minimised step by step, and the training takes millions of steps. RSA encryption with a 2,048-bit key takes a few milliseconds: the security rests on the hardness of factoring a 2,048-bit number, which would take longer than the age of the universe with known algorithms.

A History of Applied Mathematics

Applied mathematics has no single founder: it is the use of mathematics to solve real problems, and it is as old as mathematics itself. The Egyptians surveyed fields, the Greeks computed the size of the Earth, Newton invented calculus for physics, and Fourier invented his analysis for heat. The subject took its modern form in the 20th century, when the computer made numerical methods, optimisation, and simulation central, and it is now the mathematics behind engineering, science, medicine, and finance.

The timeline: Egyptian surveying, about 2000 BCE; Archimedes, 3rd century BCE; Newton and Leibniz, 1670s; Euler, 18th century; Fourier, 1822; Maxwell, 1865; the first computers, 1940s; the fast Fourier transform, 1965; public-key cryptography, 1976. The span from Egyptian surveying to the first computer is about 4,000 years. The subject's tools are old, but the subject itself is new: it was the computer that made numerical methods, optimisation, and simulation central, and it is the computer that keeps the subject growing.

Applied Mathematics in the Modern World

Applied mathematics in the modern world is everywhere: the weather forecast, the search engine, the medical scan, the financial market, and the climate model are all applied mathematics. The modern world runs on mathematics that is computed, not just proved: the equations are solved on supercomputers, the data are analysed by statistics, and the decisions are made by optimisation. Applied mathematics is the invisible infrastructure of modern life.

A weather forecast is computed on a supercomputer: the atmosphere is divided into cells of about 10 km, the equations are solved in each cell, and the forecast is stepped forward in 10-minute steps. A search engine answers a query in a fraction of a second: it looks up the words in an index, ranks the pages by PageRank, and returns the top ten. A medical scan takes a few minutes: the CT scanner measures the absorption of X-rays, and the computer reconstructs the image by solving a large linear system. A climate model runs for months on a supercomputer: it simulates the atmosphere and oceans for a century, and its output is the predicted climate.
