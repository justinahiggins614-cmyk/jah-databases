Natural Language Processing

Natural language processing is the teaching of computers to handle human language: to read, understand, and produce text and speech. It covers tokenization, parsing, translation, and question answering, and it is built from statistics, linguistics, and machine learning. Natural language processing is the field behind search engines, voice assistants, and machine translation. Tokenization

Tokenization is the splitting of text into pieces, called tokens, that a system can work with: words, punctuation, or subwords. The sentence The cat sat. becomes the tokens The, cat, sat, and a full stop, and every later step, from tagging to translation, works on tokens rather than on raw text. Tokenization looks easy in English and is hard in languages without spaces. Part-of-Speech Tagging

Part-of-speech tagging is the labelling of each word with its class: noun, verb, adjective, and the rest. In The cat sat, cat is a noun and sat is a verb, and the tagger must decide from the context, because sat can also be a noun in other sentences. Tagging is the first analysis a system does, and parsing, translation, and search all build on it. Parsing

Parsing is the finding of the structure of a sentence: which words group into phrases, which phrase is the subject, and which word depends on which. A parser takes The cat sat on the mat and returns a tree, and the tree lets later steps, from translation to question answering, work with meaning rather than with a flat string of words. Machine Translation

Machine translation is the translating of text by computer: from one language to another, without a human in the loop. Early systems used rules and dictionaries, later systems used statistics from parallel texts, and current systems use neural networks that read a whole sentence and write the translation. Machine translation is used for web pages, documents, and messages, and its quality is now high for common language pairs. Word Embeddings

Word embeddings are the representation of words as numbers that capture meaning: each word becomes a list of numbers, a vector, and words with similar meanings have similar vectors. The vector for king is close to queen, and the difference between king and man is close to the difference between queen and woman. Embeddings let systems compare meanings with arithmetic. Information Retrieval

Bias in Language Technology

Bias in language technology is the unfairness built into language systems: when a system works worse for some speakers, or repeats the stereotypes in its training data. A speech recogniser that fails on some accents, or a translation that turns a doctor into he and a nurse into she, is biased, and the bias harms the people it touches. Finding and fixing bias is now a central duty of the field.
