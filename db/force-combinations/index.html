Gravity and Normal Force on a Flat Surface Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Established textbook physics: Newton's laws and the normal force are settled, directly measurable, and taught everywhere.

A 10 kilogram block resting on a flat horizontal surface, held by gravity pulling down and the normal force of the surface pushing up.

A block sits on a table and does not move, which is only possible because two forces cancel exactly. Gravity pulls the block down with a force equal to its mass times the acceleration of gravity, about 98.1 newtons for a 10 kilogram block, and the table pushes back up with a normal force of the same size. If the normal force were smaller the block would sink into the table, and if it were larger the block would lift off. The combination is the simplest possible force balance, and it is the baseline against which every more complicated combination in this database is judged: two forces, equal, opposite, and acting on the same body.

History

The idea that a surface pushes back as hard as it is pushed goes back to Newton's third law, published in the Principia in 1687, and the normal force has been the textbook first example of equilibrium ever since. Generations of students have met it as the first free-body diagram: weight down, normal force up, net force zero.

Net force is the vector sum of gravity, 98.1 newtons straight down, and the normal force, 98.1 newtons straight up. The sum is zero, so the acceleration is zero and the block stays at rest. Equilibrium holds for any mass on a rigid level surface, because the normal force adjusts itself to match the weight up to the strength of the surface material.

Worked example

A 10 kilogram steel block rests on a laboratory bench. Its weight is 10 times 9.81, or 98.1 newtons downward, and the bench supplies a normal force of 98.1 newtons upward. The vector sum is zero, the block does not accelerate, and it stays where it was put. If a second identical block is stacked on top, the weight doubles to 196.2 newtons and the normal force doubles with it, so the balance holds at the new value.

Gravity and Friction on an Incline Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: established Established textbook physics: inclined-plane equilibrium and the static friction limit are settled and routinely measured.

A 10 kilogram block on a slope of 20 degrees, held by gravity, the normal force, and static friction acting up the slope.

A block on a slope wants to slide down, and three forces decide whether it does. Gravity pulls straight down, the slope pushes back with a normal force perpendicular to its surface, and static friction grips along the surface and opposes the slide. On a 20 degree slope a 10 kilogram block needs 33.55 newtons of friction to stay put, while the most friction the surface can supply is the coefficient of static friction, 0.4 here, times the normal force of 92.10 newtons, or 36.84 newtons. The need is smaller than the limit, so the block holds. Raise the slope past 21.8 degrees and the need passes the limit, and the block slides.

History

The inclined plane is one of the classical simple machines, studied since antiquity and formalised by Stevin and Galileo, and the friction limit was put on a quantitative footing by Amontons and Coulomb in the eighteenth century. The angle at which a block first slides, whose tangent is the coefficient of static friction, is still the standard laboratory way to measure that coefficient.

Gravity and Tension in a Simple Pendulum Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established Established textbook physics: pendulum dynamics and centripetal force are settled, measured, and taught everywhere.

A 10 kilogram bob on a 1 metre string, swinging through the bottom of its arc, where tension exceeds weight to supply the centripetal force.

A pendulum bob at the bottom of its swing is not in static equilibrium, and that is the point of this combination. Gravity pulls down with 98.1 newtons, the string pulls up with a tension of 117.72 newtons, and the difference of 19.62 newtons is not an error: it is exactly the centripetal force the curved motion requires, mass times speed squared over length, for a speed of 1.4 metres per second on a 1 metre string. The combination holds when the actual net force equals the net force the motion requires, and here it does, to the newton.

History

Galileo timed pendulum swings with his pulse and found the period nearly independent of amplitude for small swings; Huygens built the first pendulum clock in 1656 and worked out the circular-motion force. The pendulum became the standard demonstration that a net force can be present and correct, because it is the force that bends the path.
