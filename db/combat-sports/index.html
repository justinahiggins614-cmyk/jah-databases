Judo Rank 1 · simple · grappling · beginner

Judo is a grappling art founded in Japan in 1882 by Jigoro Kano, who built it from older jujutsu schools by keeping the throws and holds that could be practised safely at full effort and removing techniques meant to injure. Contestants wear a heavy jacket and trousers called a judogi and grip the sleeves and lapel to unbalance, throw, pin, strangle, or arm-lock an opponent. A clean throw onto the back scores ippon and ends the match at once, while lesser throws, pins, and penalties score smaller amounts. Judo became an Olympic sport for men in 1964 and for women in 1992, and its practice pairs hard randori sparring with careful breakfalls, so that students learn to fall safely before they ever learn to throw hard.

History

Kano founded the Kodokan in Tokyo in 1882 and judo spread worldwide through schools, universities, and police and military training before reaching the Olympic Games.

Rules

A match is normally four minutes for senior players, with golden score overtime if the scores are tied. Ippon ends the match; waza-ari scores count toward victory, and two waza-ari combine to ippon.

Techniques

Core families are hand throws, hip throws, foot sweeps, sacrifice throws, pins, strangles, and elbow locks. Every technique begins with kuzushi, breaking the opponent's balance.

Safety

Breakfalls are taught first. Strangles and armlocks are practised with tapping, and referees stop a match the moment a hold becomes dangerous.

Taekwondo Rank 2 · simple · striking · beginner

Taekwondo is a Korean striking art known for fast, high kicks, and its name is commonly explained as the way of the foot and fist. Modern taekwondo grew in Korea in the mid-twentieth century from older Korean martial traditions and other influences, and it was organised under the World Taekwondo federation, which standardised the sport form. In sport competition, athletes wear protective trunk and head gear and score points for kicks and punches to the trunk protector, with extra points for spinning and head kicks, while an electronic scoring system records clean contact. Taekwondo became a full Olympic sport at Sydney 2000. Its traditional side keeps patterns called poomsae, breaking, and self-defence drills alongside sparring.

History

Taekwondo took shape in Korea after the Second World War as several kwan schools merged, and it spread worldwide through military, school, and Olympic programmes.

Rules

Sport matches run three rounds of two minutes. Kicks to the trunk score two points, spinning kicks and head kicks score more, and punches to the trunk score one point.

Techniques

Signature kicks include the roundhouse, side kick, back kick, axe kick, and spinning hook kick, all chambered from a light bouncing stance.

Competitions

The Olympic Games, the World Taekwondo Championships, and the World Taekwondo Grand Prix are the sport's leading events.

Karate Rank 3 · simple · striking · beginner

Karate is an empty-hand art that developed on Okinawa, where island traditions of unarmed fighting met Chinese boxing influences, and it later spread through Japan and then the world. Its practice rests on three pillars: kihon, the basic punches, kicks, and blocks; kata, solo forms that string techniques into set patterns; and kumite, sparring with a partner. Sport karate awards points for controlled strikes to scoring areas, and it appeared at the Tokyo 2020 Olympic Games. Traditional karate-do treats the art as character training as much as fighting, with etiquette, breathing, and restraint taught beside technique.

History

Karate developed on Okinawa and was carried to mainland Japan in the early twentieth century, where styles such as Shotokan, Goju-ryu, Shito-ryu, and Wado-ryu took shape.

Rules

In sport kumite, controlled punches and kicks to the head and body score points, with higher scores for kicks and for throws followed by a scoring technique.

Techniques

Basics include the straight punch, front kick, roundhouse kick, side kick, and a family of rising, middle, and knife-hand blocks.

Training

Kata trains memory, posture, and breathing; kihon drills repeat basics in lines; kumite applies them against a moving partner.

Aikido Rank 4 · simple · grappling · beginner

Aikido is a Japanese art founded by Morihei Ueshiba in the early twentieth century, built on the idea of blending with an attack rather than meeting force with force. Its techniques are throws and joint locks that redirect an attacker's energy, and practice is usually cooperative: one partner attacks with a grab or strike and the other blends, turns, and throws or pins. Aikido has no regular competitive matches, which sets it apart from most of the other arts in this database, and its grading centres on technique, ukemi falling skill, and understanding of principles. Weapons practice with the wooden sword and staff appears in many schools as a way of teaching distance and timing.

History

Ueshiba developed aikido from older jujutsu and sword traditions, and it spread after the Second World War through his students, including to France and the United States.

Techniques

Core techniques include irimi-nage, the entering throw; shiho-nage, the four-direction throw; kote-gaeshi, the wrist turn; and ikkyo, the first arm control and pin.

Training

Partners alternate as attacker and defender, attacks are given sincerely, and the defender blends rather than clashing.

Safety

Ukemi, the art of falling and rolling safely, is taught from the first lesson, and joint locks are applied gradually with tapping.

Muay Thai Rank 5 · simple · striking · beginner

Muay Thai is Thailand's striking art, often called the art of eight limbs because it uses fists, elbows, knees, and shins together. Fighters train on pads and heavy bags, practise clinch work in which they grip an opponent's neck and land knees and elbows, and condition their shins by repeated pad and bag work rather than by striking hard objects. A traditional bout opens with the wai kru ram muay, a ritual dance honouring teachers, and is fought to music played live at ringside. Professional bouts in Thailand traditionally run five rounds of three minutes, and the sport is practised worldwide both as a fighting sport and as a fitness discipline.

History

Muay Thai grew from older Thai battlefield and festival fighting traditions and became an organised ring sport with stadiums in Bangkok at its centre.

Rules

Bouts are usually five rounds of three minutes for professionals. Scoring rewards effective kicks, knees, elbows, and clinch control; knockouts end a bout at once.

Techniques

The eight limbs are the two fists, two elbows, two knees, and two shins, used with kicks, teeps, clinch knees, and elbow cuts.

Training

Training pairs pad rounds, bag rounds, clinch rounds, skipping, and running, with sparring kept controlled outside competition.

Capoeira Rank 6 · compounded · striking · intermediate

Capoeira is a Brazilian art that blends fighting, dance, acrobatics, and music, and it was developed by enslaved Africans in Brazil, who disguised combat training within dance and music. Players form a circle called a roda, musicians play the berimbau and other instruments and sing, and two players enter the circle and exchange kicks, sweeps, dodges, and acrobatic movements in a flowing game. There is no single contest format: some rodas are playful and musical, others are fast and combative, and the quality of a game is judged by flow, cleverness, and musicality as much as by contact. In 2014 UNESCO inscribed the capoeira circle on its list of intangible cultural heritage.

History

Capoeira developed among enslaved Africans in colonial Brazil, was banned at times, and later became an open national art with Angola and Regional styles.

Techniques

The ginga, a swaying base step, links kicks such as the queixada and armada, dodges called esquivas, sweeps, and acrobatic moves like the au cartwheel.

Training

Classes pair movement drills with music practice, so students learn to play instruments and sing as well as to move.

Competitions

Games are played in the roda rather than in scored tournaments, though festivals and batizados mark student progress.

Sambo Rank 7 · compounded · grappling · intermediate

Sambo is a grappling art developed in the Soviet Union in the 1920s and 1930s, drawing on judo, folk wrestling styles, and other combat systems, and its name is a shortened form of a Russian phrase meaning self-defence without weapons. Sport sambo resembles judo and wrestling: athletes wear a jacket, shorts, and wrestling shoes and score for throws, pins, and submissions, with leg locks allowed, which sets it apart from judo. Combat sambo adds strikes and is used in military and police training as well as in mixed martial arts preparation. Sambo has its own world championships and a world federation, and it is widely practised across Eastern Europe and Central Asia.

History

Sambo was systematised in the Soviet Union by Vasili Oshchepkov and Viktor Spiridonov, among others, and spread through state sport and military programmes.

Rules

Sport sambo bouts run five minutes for senior men. Throws score up to four points, pins score, and submissions end the bout.

Techniques

Throws come from jacket grips similar to judo, plus leg attacks from wrestling and a wide family of leg locks.

Safety

Leg locks are applied gradually in training, tapping ends every submission, and throws are practised with breakfalls.

Krav Maga Rank 8 · compounded · striking · intermediate

Krav Maga is a self-defence system developed in Israel by Imi Lichtenfeld, who combined boxing, wrestling, judo, and street-fighting experience into a practical method for soldiers and then for civilians. Its teaching stresses awareness and avoidance first, then simple, aggressive responses to common attacks such as grabs, chokes, and weapon threats, trained under stress so that responses survive surprise. Krav Maga has no forms, no traditional uniform, and no regular sport competition, and its civilian classes are usually taught in ordinary training clothes. Because it is taught as self-defence, its techniques are practised on pads and with cooperative partners, and weapon defences are introduced only after empty-hand fundamentals are solid.

History

Lichtenfeld developed krav maga in the 1940s for the Israeli military, and civilian schools spread it worldwide from the 1980s onward.

Techniques

Core tools are simultaneous defence and attack, groin and eye-level strikes as defences of last resort, escapes from grabs and chokes, and defences against stick and knife threats.

Training

Drills use pads, stress exercises, and scenario practice, with the aim of reacting quickly under surprise and fatigue.

Safety

Training partners cooperate, protective gear is worn for contact drills, and weapon defences are practised slowly with training weapons.

Sumo Rank 9 · compounded · grappling · intermediate

Sumo is Japan's national wrestling tradition, in which two wrestlers meet in a clay ring called a dohyo and try to force each other out of the ring or to touch the ground with any part of the body other than the soles of the feet. Its rituals are ancient: wrestlers purify the ring with salt, stamp their feet, and perform the shiko leg-stomping exercise, and bouts are usually decided in seconds by a push, a throw, or a sidestep. Professional sumo is organised by the Japan Sumo Association, with six tournaments a year in Japan, and wrestlers live and train in stables called heya under a strict hierarchy. Amateur sumo is practised worldwide with weight classes, which professional sumo does not use.

History

Sumo's roots reach back more than a thousand years in Japan, from ritual contests at shrines and the imperial court to the professional sport of the Edo period.

Rules

A bout is won by forcing the opponent out of the ring or making any part of their body other than the soles of the feet touch the ground.

Techniques

Winning methods include pushes, thrusts, belt throws, trips, and sidesteps at the initial charge.

Competitions

Professional sumo holds six grand tournaments a year, and the highest rank is yokozuna, awarded only to wrestlers of exceptional strength and dignity.

Kendo Rank 10 · compounded · weapon art · intermediate

Kendo is the Japanese way of the sword, practised with a bamboo sword called a shinai and protective armour called bogu, descended from samurai sword training. Practitioners score points in matches by striking the head, wrist, torso, or throat with the correct part of the shinai while shouting the target and showing strong posture, and a match is normally won by the first to score two points. Training centres on footwork, cutting practice against a partner's shinai, and formal drills called kata performed with wooden swords. Kendo is taught as character training: respect, correct posture, and loud, committed spirit are graded alongside technique.

History

Kendo developed from samurai sword schools in the Edo period and was organised into its modern sport form in the twentieth century, spreading worldwide after the Second World War.

Rules

A match is won by two points. Valid strikes are to the head, wrist, torso, or throat, made with the correct part of the shinai, good posture, and a shout naming the target.

Techniques

Core cuts are the head cut, wrist cut, torso cut, and thrust, all driven by footwork that advances and retreats without crossing the feet.

Equipment

The shinai is a split bamboo sword, and the bogu armour protects the head, wrists, torso, and groin.

Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Rank 11 · complex · grappling · advanced

Brazilian jiu-jitsu is a ground grappling art developed in Brazil from judo groundwork, in which a smaller practitioner uses leverage, position, and joint locks and strangles to control and submit a larger opponent. Most of its practice is groundwork: passing the guard, escaping bad positions, taking the back, and finishing with chokes and joint locks, and students spar live from the first months, which is why its techniques are tested constantly. In sport competition, points are awarded for takedowns, guard passes, and dominant positions, and a submission ends the match at once. Brazilian jiu-jitsu grew worldwide from the 1990s, carried by mixed martial arts and by schools that made live sparring the centre of training.

History

Brazilian jiu-jitsu grew from the judo taught in Brazil by Mitsuyo Maeda and was developed by the Gracie family and their students into a ground-focused art.

Rules

Sport matches award points for takedowns, guard passes, knee-on-belly, mount, and back control, and submissions end the match immediately.

Techniques

Core positions are the guard, side control, mount, and back control, with finishes including the rear-naked choke, armbar, and triangle choke.

Safety

Tapping ends every submission instantly, and live sparring is graded by intensity so beginners roll lightly.

Mixed Martial Arts Rank 12 · complex · striking · advanced

Mixed martial arts is a combat sport in which athletes combine striking, wrestling, and grappling under a unified set of rules, and it grew from early no-holds-barred contests into a regulated sport in the 1990s and 2000s. Bouts are fought in a caged enclosure or a ring, usually over three rounds of five minutes, or five rounds for championship bouts, and they are won by knockout, submission, or judges' decision. The Unified Rules ban strikes to the back of the head, eye gouging, groin strikes, and several other dangerous actions, and referees, judges, and ringside doctors oversee every bout. Fighters train across disciplines, typically pairing a striking art with wrestling and ground grappling, and weight classes run from strawweight to heavyweight.

History

Modern mixed martial arts grew from Brazilian vale tudo contests and early American events in 1993, then adopted unified rules and weight classes as it became a regulated sport.

Rules

Most bouts run three rounds of five minutes, with five rounds for title bouts. Knockout, submission, and judges' decision decide bouts, and the Unified Rules ban eye gouging, groin strikes, and strikes to the back of the head.

Techniques

Fighters combine a striking base, wrestling takedowns and defence, and ground grappling, and they must defend in every phase.

Safety

Bouts are overseen by a referee, judges, and ringside doctors, and fighters pass medical checks and weigh-ins before competing.

Savate Rank 13 · complex · striking · advanced

Savate is a French striking art that pairs punches with kicks delivered in shoes, and it developed in France in the nineteenth century from street fighting and sailing traditions. Its kicks are distinctive: fighters kick with the shoe, using the toe and sole, and they combine boxing punches with kicks to the legs, body, and head in a flowing, upright style. Savate is practised as a sport with weight classes and graded levels, and it also keeps an older cane-fighting companion art called canne de combat. The art is known for elegant footwork, long-range kicking, and precise rather than heavy striking.

History

Savate was formalised in nineteenth-century France, notably by Charles Lecour, who combined English boxing with French kicking methods.

Rules

Sport savate bouts award points for clean punches and kicks to legal targets, with kicks to the legs, body, and head scored by precision and effect.

Techniques

Signature kicks include the fouette roundhouse, the chasse side kick, and the revers turning kick, all delivered with the shoe.

Equipment

Savate is practised in shoes, which are part of the technique, and competitors wear gloves, groin protection, and a mouthguard.

Kung Fu Rank 14 · complex · striking · advanced

Kung fu is a broad name for the Chinese martial arts, covering hundreds of styles that share training in stances, forms, strikes, kicks, and weapons, and it is tied to Chinese philosophy, medicine, and performance traditions. Its best-known traditions include Shaolin temple arts, the internal styles of tai chi, bagua, and xingyi, and southern styles such as Wing Chun, and modern wushu turned many of its forms into a performance and competition sport. Training typically pairs basic stances and footwork with forms that string techniques together, partner drills, and weapons such as the staff, broadsword, and straight sword. Kung fu is practised worldwide both as a fighting art and as a health and cultural discipline.

History

Chinese martial arts developed over centuries in temples, villages, armies, and opera troupes, and they spread worldwide in the twentieth century through migration and film.

Techniques

Training covers stances such as horse stance and bow stance, hand strikes, kicks, throws, and weapons including the staff and swords.

Training

A session typically pairs stance work, basics, a form, and partner drills, with conditioning built into stance holding.

Competitions

Modern wushu competitions judge forms for difficulty and execution, while traditional schools test through forms and partner work.

Pencak Silat Rank 15 · complex · striking · advanced

Pencak silat is the martial art of the Malay archipelago, practised across Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, and Brunei, and it blends strikes, joint locks, throws, sweeps, and weapons with dance-like movement and music. Its practice pairs seni, the artistic and flowing forms, with bela diri, self-defence, and olahraga, the sport form, in which athletes score for strikes, takedowns, and sweeps in timed rounds. Silat styles vary widely by island and school, and many keep weapons such as the kris dagger, the staff, and the sickle as part of advanced training. Silat was recognised by UNESCO in 2019 as part of Indonesia's intangible cultural heritage, and its sport form appears at the Southeast Asian Games.

History

Silat developed across the islands of Southeast Asia over centuries, carried by villages, courts, and trading ports, and it was organised into national federations in the twentieth century.

Rules

Sport silat matches run timed rounds, and athletes score for punches, kicks, takedowns, and sweeps, with penalties for illegal techniques.

Techniques

Movement flows through low stances, evasive steps, sweeping kicks, joint locks, and throws, often chained into continuous combinations.

Competitions

The Southeast Asian Games and the world pencak silat championships are the sport's leading events.

Escrima and Kali Rank 16 · complex · weapon art · advanced

Escrima and kali are the stick and blade arts of the Philippines, taught first with rattan sticks and later with knives and empty hands, and they are also known as arnis. Their core idea is that stick, blade, and empty hand share the same angles of attack, so a student who learns twelve basic striking angles with a stick can transfer them to a knife and then to the hand. Training pairs solo angle drills and sinawali weaving patterns with partner flow drills, in which partners exchange strikes continuously, and with disarms and locks. Arnis was declared the national martial art and sport of the Philippines in 2009, and its sport form scores for clean stick strikes in timed matches.

History

Filipino stick and blade arts developed across the Philippine islands over centuries and were systematised in the twentieth century by many family schools.

Techniques

Twelve basic angles of attack organise stick, blade, and empty-hand work, and sinawali weaving patterns train both hands together.

Training

Solo angle drills, partner flow drills, disarm practice, and footwork triangles form the core training methods.

Safety

Training uses rattan sticks and padded sticks for sparring, blades are practised with training versions, and protective gear is worn for contact.
