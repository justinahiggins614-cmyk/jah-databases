Terror: Definition Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Established: this facet is documented in the published emotion-science literature (Ekman, Plutchik, Russell, and the appraisal tradition), and the record reports what that literature states.

Terror is the intense form of fear: an overwhelming dread that rises when a grave threat is felt as present and beyond coping, and that floods the body with alarm.

What exactly is terror, and where does it sit among the fear emotions? The question matters because terror is easily confused with its neighbours: it resembles fear, but it is stronger and more overwhelming; it resembles anxiety, but it answers a present threat rather than an uncertain future; it resembles horror, but it needs no revulsion. The literature treats terror as the intense form of fear on Plutchik's wheel, an emotion that follows the appraisal of a grave, imminent threat beyond the person's coping, and that carries the body into freezing, flight, or struggle. This record sets out the working definition used across the pair, so that every later record, triggers, expression, measurement, can be read against the same sense of the word.

History

The word terror comes from the Latin terror, dread, from terrere, to frighten. It entered English through Old French in the late fourteenth century, first meaning great fear or dread, and it kept that sense into the modern period. Burke's Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful (1757) gave terror its most discussed philosophical account: terror, Burke argued, is the ruling principle of the sublime, and whatever excites ideas of pain and danger is a source of the sublime. Darwin took the term into the science of expression in 1872, describing terror's signs, and Plutchik placed terror as the intense form of fear on his wheel of 1980.

This facet in detail

A walker on a mountain path hears a rockfall above and sees the stones coming down toward the path, and the feeling reported is terror: the threat is grave, it is present, and it is beyond coping in the moment, so the body freezes for a heartbeat and then runs. The walker does not deliberate; the appraisal is made at once, and the body answers with alarm. The same shape appears in a person who wakes to a stranger in the room, a sailor who sees a wave rising over the bow, and a child who hears a sudden crash in the dark: a grave threat, felt as present, and a body flooded with alarm.

How it is described

What the record shows

The literature reports that terror is reliably described as an intense, unpleasant, high-arousal emotion, distinct from fear by its intensity and by the sense of being overwhelmed, and distinct from anxiety by its present threat rather than an uncertain future. The Geneva Emotion Wheel lists fear as one of its twenty emotion families, with terror named as the intense form. Studies of terror report that it is the fear emotion most often described with the words overwhelming and flooding, and that its reported bodily signs are the strongest of the fear family.

Cautions and limits

The record's cautions are plain. Terror has no single laboratory measure, and most of its evidence is self-report and observation: people describe what they felt, and observers describe what they saw, and neither is a measurement of the feeling itself. The line between fear and terror is drawn by intensity, and the literature does not agree on where the line falls: some writers treat terror as fear at its highest, others as a separate state. The record therefore presents terror as the intense form of fear, following Plutchik, and it states the disagreement beside the definition. Every claim is stated as reported, with its source.

History

This facet in detail

The sentence shows the word doing its philosophical work: terror is the response to pain and danger, and the sublime is the delight taken in that response at a safe distance. A reader meeting the sentence today can see the older sense, great fear, and the modern sense, the intense form of fear, standing together, which is why the history of the term matters for the definition.

Terror: Triggers Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established Established: this facet is documented in the published emotion-science literature (Ekman, Plutchik, Russell, and the appraisal tradition), and the record reports what that literature states.

The situations people report as setting off terror: sudden grave threat, the dark, the unknown, pursuit, disaster, and threat to loved ones, described as reported situations only.

What sets terror off? The literature groups triggers by what is threatened rather than by where it happens: the body, the loved ones, and the familiar world are the three broad kinds, and each kind has its own shape. How is terror measured, and what do the measures actually measure? The literature measures terror by self-report: people are asked how much fear they feel, or how often, or of what, and their answers are scored. History

The measurement of the fear emotions begins with the Fear Survey Schedule, published by Wolpe and Lang in 1964, which lists feared objects and situations and asks respondents to rate their fear of each.
