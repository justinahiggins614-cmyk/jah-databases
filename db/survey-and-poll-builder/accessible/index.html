The Survey and Poll Builder Database

Accessible version: plain text, no scripts, no sign-in, free forever. The survey and poll builder helps a person ask a plain question and gather plain answers, in words, with each question written out, the choices listed, and the results shown fairly. Every survey shown is a clearly labeled illustrative sample of a generic kind of question, never a real survey of real people.

  1. A Favorite Season Question

    Record 1 of 24. simple. Feasibility: buildable-now. The design uses only web pages, forms, and counters that exist today; only the design work stands in the way.

    The survey and poll builder helps a person ask a plain question and gather plain answers, in words, with each question written out, the choices listed, and the results shown fairly. Every survey shown is a clearly labeled illustrative sample of a generic kind of question, never a real survey of real people. A favorite season question gives a person a plain way to ask what season people love, with the question written out, the four seasons listed as choices, and the results counted fairly. The builder must show the question in plain words, so every person who answers understands it the same way, and the count must be honest, so the winning season is the one most people picked. Without a plain question, people answer different questions, and a count of different questions is a poor way to learn anything. Worked example: in a website built this way, a favorite season question runs as a small, complete feature a visitor can try in one sitting, with the fair-play and moderation rules of this database applied throughout.

    History: Asking a plain question is an old habit: a show of hands in a hall, a slip of paper in a box, a count taken at the end of the day. The website form grows out of that habit, adding a plain question, listed choices, and a fair count of the answers. Asking a favorite season is an old habit, from a show of hands in a hall to a slip of paper in a box.

    Proposed solution: Name the survey and what a visitor does in it; Write the rules in plain sentences a new visitor can follow; Give every survey the same fair start: the same clues, the same time, the same scoring; Let a visitor try one survey without signing in; Check the survey works the same way on a phone and on a computer.

    Dependencies: What must exist first: a list of generic kinds of questions, a plain question with listed choices for each kind, a fair count of answers, and a review step before publishing. Nothing else is needed to open the first survey.

    Verification path: A visitor opens one survey, follows the rules in plain sentences, and finishes it without help. The demo program prints the same total as the math proof, and a second visitor repeating the steps gets the same result.

    Science proof: total = count x each. In plain words, this equation turns counted quantities into one checkable number: with 4 items at 2 each, the arithmetic gives 8 surveys, and the demo prints that same total, so the claim can be checked by arithmetic alone.

  12. A Streak of Questions Answered Each Week

    Record 12 of 24. compounded. Feasibility: buildable-now. The design uses only web pages, forms, and counters that exist today; only the design work stands in the way.

    The survey and poll builder helps a person ask a plain question and gather plain answers, in words, with each question written out, the choices listed, and the results shown fairly. Every survey shown is a clearly labeled illustrative sample of a generic kind of question, never a real survey of real people. A streak gives a person a reason to come back next week: one question answered each week in a row builds a chain, and breaking the chain costs something the person has earned. The streak must be forgiving, because a person who misses one week through no fault of their own should not lose a month of answers. Without a streak, every question is a first question, and the site gives a loyal person nothing that a stranger does not also get. Worked example: in a website built this way, a streak of questions answered each week runs as a small, complete feature a visitor can try in one sitting, with the fair-play and moderation rules of this database applied throughout.

The website form grows out of that habit, adding a plain question, listed choices, and a fair count of the answers. Streaks grew out of daily puzzles, where returning each day became part of the game itself.

Without an index, the simple records that teach the design are buried beside the complex records that assume it, and a visitor who wants one idea must read the whole database to find it. The index solves this by listing every record in ladder order, with its place in the ladder, so a reader can start at the beginning and climb, or jump straight to the record they need. Worked example: a reader who wants the simplest starting point opens record 1, A Favorite Season Question, and a reader who wants the hardest problem opens record 23, A Yearly Showcase of Questions.

    History: Asking a plain question is an old habit: a show of hands in a hall, a slip of paper in a box, a count taken at the end of the day. The website form grows out of that habit, adding a plain question, listed choices, and a fair count of the answers. Field guides and indexes are as old as books themselves: a table of contents that tells a reader where everything lives. This index follows that habit, listing the twenty-three content records of this database in the order a reader should meet them.

    Proposed solution: Add a tournament bracket with rounds, divisions, and a season; Add a creator studio so visitors can build a new survey; Add a review step so every new survey is checked before it is published; Add the survey in many languages, with the same rules in each; Add an offline pack so the survey works with no connection.

    Dependencies: What must exist first: a list of generic kinds of questions, a plain question with listed choices for each kind, a fair count of answers, and a review step before publishing. A tournament system, a creator studio, a review step, translations, and an offline pack are also needed before the complex surveys open.

    Verification path: A tournament runs a full season, a creator publishes a new survey through the review step, and the offline pack is tried with no connection. The demo program prints the same total as the math proof, and every step is checked by a second reader.

    Science proof: total = count x each. In plain words, this equation turns counted quantities into one checkable number: with 23 items at 1 each, the arithmetic gives 23 records, and the demo prints that same total, so the claim can be checked by arithmetic alone.

    Math proof: Worked arithmetic: 23 x 1 = 23. The demo sets count to 23 and each to 1, multiplies them, and prints Total: 23, so a fresh run of the demo verifies this proof exactly.
