Chapter 1: Vision

Here, under Vision, the book asks what a temple on the hill as the ancient centre of the city requires in practice, and it answers by describing what this design is for, who it serves, and what a visitor should understand within the first hour of arriving. The description stays concrete throughout, naming sizes, materials, and routines, because a design book that stays vague cannot be rebuilt from it. Where a number is given, it is given as a design target for a study model, not as a claim about a finished building, and the chapter keeps that distinction visible. In the public reading, this chapter is presented as a shared design study, open to any reader, with its claims checked against the chapter data itself.

Three counts govern this chapter: 14 gathering places, 36 seats per gathering place, and 6 linked systems. They were chosen to keep the design human in scale: large enough to welcome a crowd, small enough that a steward can still know the names of the people in one unit, and simple enough that a newcomer can learn the pattern in a single walk. The chapter returns to these counts whenever it introduces a new element, asking whether the element fits the scale, serves the unit, and joins the linked systems without breaking them. Any future edition of this design should begin by re-justifying these three counts, not by copying them, because a count copied without reason is how a good design slowly becomes an unworkable one.

Nothing in this chapter is built from a material the book has not named, and the named materials are bronze fittings, clay tiles, and dressed stone. That discipline matters: a design book that waves at unspecified riches cannot be costed, cannot be repaired, and cannot be honestly compared with any other design. The chapter therefore lists, for each material, where it is used, how much of it a study model needs, and what would replace it if it were unavailable, so that the design survives contact with a real workshop. Any golden surface described here is a clear golden appearance target, distinct from bulk gold, and the distinction is repeated wherever the surface is mentioned, so that no reader mistakes a look for a material claim.

Under Vision, the working systems are market stall order, craft workshops, and spring and cistern supply, and the chapter treats them as living routines, not as diagrams. Routines need people, and so the chapter names who does what, when the work changes hands, and how a tired worker is relieved without the design stalling. It also names the quiet work, the cleaning, the mending, and the counting, because a city that hides its maintenance work will soon find that nobody is doing it. The systems are presented as design proposals to be tested in a study model, with their limits stated, and the reader is invited to improve them rather than to admire them.

Neighbours are the authors of this chapter as much as its builders are, because a temple on the hill as the ancient centre of the city only works when neighbours practise it together. The chapter therefore describes shared work, shared meals, shared celebrations, and shared repair, and it treats each as a skill that can be learned, practised, and handed on. It also makes room for disagreement, since neighbours will differ, and it describes places and routines where differences can be talked through without the design itself becoming a weapon in the argument. A design that cannot hold disagreement gently will not hold a city for long, and this chapter takes that warning seriously in every routine it proposes.

Two readings run side by side in this chapter, and the reader should keep them apart. The faith reading asks what the design means and what it hopes for, while the engineering reading asks what the design is made of, how it works, and what it costs to keep working, and each reading is judged by its own standards. Nothing in the engineering reading claims a miracle of materials or a finished full-scale city, and nothing in the faith reading is presented as a laboratory result, and the chapter refuses to blur that boundary for effect. Full-scale construction is NOT-ESTABLISHED, transparent gold is an appearance target distinct from bulk gold, and the 2,220 km cube figure is ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY wherever it appears.

Read together with the other chapters, Vision completes one more piece of the pattern of The Temple on the Hill: Jerusalem's Ancient Centre. The pattern is not a picture to be admired from outside, but a set of rooms, routes, routines, and gatherings that people can enter, use, and change, and this chapter has described one of those pieces in enough detail to be built as a study model. The next chapter takes up a different piece, but the counts, materials, and systems introduced here will reappear there, because a city is one design, not sixteen unrelated designs sharing a cover. Chapter 2: History

This chapter, History, takes up how earlier forms of this design arose, what was kept, what was changed, and what the change teaches a present-day builder. In The Temple on the Hill: Jerusalem's Ancient Centre, that question is answered through a temple on the hill as the ancient centre of the city, and the answer is worked out in built form rather than left as a slogan. The chapter therefore begins with the people who will use the design, not with the design in the abstract, because a design that nobody can use has failed its first test. In the public reading, this chapter is presented as a shared design study, open to any reader, with its claims checked against the chapter data itself.

The chapter works with 14 gathering places, 36 seats per gathering place, and 6 linked systems. Those three numbers are not ornaments: the gathering places set the scale of the design, the seats per gathering place set the size of a single well-run unit, and the linked systems set how many parts must work together before the design can be called complete. A builder who changes one number must recheck the other two, because a larger gathering needs wider routes, more water, and more resting places, and the chapter shows that chain of consequences in each example it gives. Together the three numbers give 504 seat-places across the design when every gathering place is full, and 56 as the simple sum of the three headline counts, figures the reader can verify with the demo that accompanies this book.

The materials of this chapter are bronze fittings, clay tiles, and dressed stone. Bronze fittings carries the main load of the work, clay tiles gives the design its working surfaces and places to sit and gather, and dressed stone marks thresholds and edges so that a visitor can read the design by touch and by sight. Each material is chosen for a reason the chapter states: durability where people tread, warmth where people linger, and clarity where people must find their way without asking. Where the chapter mentions transparent gold, it means an appearance target, a clear golden look for a surface, distinct from bulk gold as a material, and the chapter never treats the look as a claim that bulk gold could serve the same structural purpose.

The systems that carry this chapter are market stall order, craft workshops, and spring and cistern supply. Market stall order does the daily work of the design, craft workshops keeps the design intelligible to a stranger, and spring and cistern supply keeps it running when the first system is under strain. The chapter describes each system as a routine with named roles, named times, and named checks, because a system described only as an idea cannot be staffed, timed, or tested. It also describes what happens when a system fails, since a design that only works on a perfect day is not a design for a real city, and the failure plan is part of the design, not an afterthought.

For the people who use this design, History should feel calm, legible, and kind. A child should be able to find water, shade, and a safe adult without crossing a busy route; an older visitor should be able to sit, rest, and rejoin the gathering without feeling left behind; and a stranger should be able to ask one question and receive one clear answer. The chapter measures itself against those three visitors, not against a grand view from above, because a city is judged by its smallest daily kindnesses before it is judged by its largest occasions. Where the design would fail one of those visitors, the chapter says so plainly and proposes a change, since hiding a failure would only move it to the day the design is actually used.

Faith interpretation and engineering design are kept distinct throughout this book, and this chapter is no exception. Statements about meaning, hope, and the life of faith belong to the faith reading of the design, while statements about sizes, materials, routes, and routines belong to the engineering reading, and the chapter does not let one kind of statement borrow authority from the other. Full-scale construction of the city described here is NOT-ESTABLISHED: this book is a design study, a set of proposals to be tested in models and in small-scale trials, not a claim that a full-scale city has been built or could be built as described. The 2,220 km cube figure is ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY wherever it appears, a scale figure used to discuss proportion, not a measured build plan, and no part of this chapter depends on treating it as a construction target.

To rebuild History from this chapter alone, a builder should begin with the layout, then the materials, then the routines, checking each against the counts given above. The rebuild guide at the end of the book gathers those checks into a single ordered list, and this chapter contributes its own checks to that list, so that nothing important is left to memory. The chapter ends by naming its weakest point honestly, because a design book that hides its weaknesses forces the next builder to discover them by failure, while a book that names them invites the next builder to improve them. That invitation is the purpose of the whole book: not to close discussion, but to give discussion a concrete design to work on, test, and surpass.

A design proposal can be beautiful, coherent, and still unbuilt, and the chapter keeps the line between a proposal and a finished work visible in every paragraph, so that no reader mistakes an idea for an achievement. The engineering reading is limited to what the chapter actually demonstrates: a layout that can be drawn, counts that can be checked, routines that can be rehearsed, and a model that can be built at study scale. Full-scale construction remains NOT-ESTABLISHED, and the 2,220 km cube figure remains ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY, a figure for discussing scale, never a dimension to be ordered or a site to be cleared.

The measure of Rebuild Guide is whether a stranger, a neighbour, and a steward would all recognise the design as serving them. If the stranger is lost, the neighbour is burdened, or the steward is exhausted, then the design has failed in that chapter, whatever its beauty from a distance, and the chapter says which of those three is most at risk here. Future work should therefore begin with the most at-risk user, redesigning for that person first, and only then restoring the larger pattern, because a city that serves its most vulnerable visitor well will usually serve everyone else well too. This chapter hands that work to the reader, with the counts, materials, and systems above as the starting point, and with the rebuild guide as the record of what was tried.

Chapter 16: Looking Forward

Here, under Looking Forward, the book asks what a temple on the hill as the ancient centre of the city requires in practice, and it answers by describing what this design makes possible next, what it deliberately leaves unfinished, and what a later builder should test first. The description stays concrete throughout, naming sizes, materials, and routines, because a design book that stays vague cannot be rebuilt from it. Where a number is given, it is given as a design target for a study model, not as a claim about a finished building, and the chapter keeps that distinction visible. In the public reading, this chapter is presented as a shared design study, open to any reader, with its claims checked against the chapter data itself.

Three counts govern this chapter: 14 gathering places, 36 seats per gathering place, and 6 linked systems. They were chosen to keep the design human in scale: large enough to welcome a crowd, small enough that a steward can still know the names of the people in one unit, and simple enough that a newcomer can learn the pattern in a single walk. The chapter returns to these counts whenever it introduces a new element, asking whether the element fits the scale, serves the unit, and joins the linked systems without breaking them. Any future edition of this design should begin by re-justifying these three counts, not by copying them, because a count copied without reason is how a good design slowly becomes an unworkable one.

Nothing in this chapter is built from a material the book has not named, and the named materials are bronze fittings, clay tiles, and dressed stone. That discipline matters: a design book that waves at unspecified riches cannot be costed, cannot be repaired, and cannot be honestly compared with any other design. The chapter therefore lists, for each material, where it is used, how much of it a study model needs, and what would replace it if it were unavailable, so that the design survives contact with a real workshop. Any golden surface described here is a clear golden appearance target, distinct from bulk gold, and the distinction is repeated wherever the surface is mentioned, so that no reader mistakes a look for a material claim.

Under Looking Forward, the working systems are market stall order, craft workshops, and spring and cistern supply, and the chapter treats them as living routines, not as diagrams. Routines need people, and so the chapter names who does what, when the work changes hands, and how a tired worker is relieved without the design stalling. It also names the quiet work, the cleaning, the mending, and the counting, because a city that hides its maintenance work will soon find that nobody is doing it. The systems are presented as design proposals to be tested in a study model, with their limits stated, and the reader is invited to improve them rather than to admire them.

Neighbours are the authors of this chapter as much as its builders are, because a temple on the hill as the ancient centre of the city only works when neighbours practise it together. The chapter therefore describes shared work, shared meals, shared celebrations, and shared repair, and it treats each as a skill that can be learned, practised, and handed on. It also makes room for disagreement, since neighbours will differ, and it describes places and routines where differences can be talked through without the design itself becoming a weapon in the argument. A design that cannot hold disagreement gently will not hold a city for long, and this chapter takes that warning seriously in every routine it proposes.

Two readings run side by side in this chapter, and the reader should keep them apart. The faith reading asks what the design means and what it hopes for, while the engineering reading asks what the design is made of, how it works, and what it costs to keep working, and each reading is judged by its own standards. Nothing in the engineering reading claims a miracle of materials or a finished full-scale city, and nothing in the faith reading is presented as a laboratory result, and the chapter refuses to blur that boundary for effect. Full-scale construction is NOT-ESTABLISHED, transparent gold is an appearance target distinct from bulk gold, and the 2,220 km cube figure is ILLUSTRATIVE ONLY wherever it appears.

Read together with the other chapters, Looking Forward completes one more piece of the pattern of The Temple on the Hill: Jerusalem's Ancient Centre. The pattern is not a picture to be admired from outside, but a set of rooms, routes, routines, and gatherings that people can enter, use, and change, and this chapter has described one of those pieces in enough detail to be built as a study model. The next chapter takes up a different piece, but the counts, materials, and systems introduced here will reappear there, because a city is one design, not sixteen unrelated designs sharing a cover. That continuity is deliberate, and the reader who checks it, chapter against chapter, is doing exactly the work this book was written to support.
