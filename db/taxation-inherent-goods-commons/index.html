JAH-TIGC-000001 — The Public Trust Doctrine: Commons Held for Everyone

The public trust doctrine is a legal principle, rooted in Roman law and carried into English and then American law, holding that certain resources, classically navigable waters, shorelines, and the lands beneath them, are held by the government in trust for the public and cannot simply be given away or sold off for private benefit. The doctrine's core idea is that some things belong to the people as a whole, so the state's role is stewardship rather than ownership in the ordinary sense: it may regulate use, grant limited permissions, and charge fees, but it may not destroy the public's underlying interest. The doctrine has been applied differently across jurisdictions, and its scope is debated, but its existence as a legal tradition is well documented and it frames many modern arguments about who may charge for access to shared resources.

How it works. The working method of the doctrine is a trust test: first, identify whether the resource falls within the trust, such as a navigable waterway or a public shoreline; second, ask whether the government's action serves a public purpose compatible with the trust; third, check whether the action alienates the resource or merely regulates its use; fourth, look for a remedy, which in trust cases can include invalidating a grant that violated the trust. Courts applying the doctrine have struck down grants of trust land and have required governments to consider public access and environmental protection when managing trust resources.

Use and limits. Use this record as the legal starting point for any argument about charging for air, water, sunlight, or fish: the doctrine shows that law already distinguishes between things held in trust and things privately owned. Do not use the doctrine as proof that all taxation of natural resources is unlawful, because the doctrine itself permits regulation and fees consistent with the trust. JAH-TIGC-000002 — Severance Taxes on Minerals, Oil, and Gas

A severance tax is a tax imposed when a natural resource is severed, that is, extracted, from the ground, and it is one of the oldest ways governments have charged for resources they did not create. States and countries with oil, gas, coal, or mineral production levy severance taxes on the value or volume extracted, and the revenue often goes to general funds, to the communities near extraction, or to permanent funds for future generations. The justification offered for such taxes is that the resource is a one-time endowment: once extracted and sold, it is gone, so the public that owned it in place should receive a share of its value. Severance taxes are therefore a direct answer to the question of whether a government may charge for something it did not make, and in practice governments routinely do.

How it works. The working method of a severance tax is a rate applied to a base: the base may be the market value of the extracted resource or its volume, the rate may be flat or graduated, and the collection point is usually the producer at the wellhead or mine mouth. Some systems allow deductions for production costs, others tax gross value, and the choice shapes both revenue and incentives. Because resource prices swing, severance revenue swings with them, which is why some jurisdictions save windfall years into permanent funds rather than spending every year's receipts.

Use and limits. Use this record to test any claim that taxing uncreated resources is unprecedented: severance taxes are widespread, long-standing, and accepted in resource states. Do not use this record to claim that severance taxes are fair in every design, because rates, bases, and revenue uses are contested and vary widely. JAH-TIGC-000003 — Fishing Licenses and Catch Rights

Fishing licenses are permissions to take fish from waters that are, in most legal systems, public resources, and they illustrate how governments charge for access to a living resource they did not create. A license typically grants the holder the right to fish in specified waters, with specified gear, during specified seasons, up to specified limits, and the fee funds management, enforcement, and conservation. Commercial fisheries add further layers, including quotas that cap the total catch and individual shares that divide the quota among fishers. The system rests on a simple problem: fish reproduce and move, no one owns a wild fish until it is caught, and without rules the shared stock collapses, so access must be managed even though the fish themselves are not government property.

How it works. The working method of fisheries management has four parts: science sets a sustainable catch level, rules divide that catch among users, licenses and quotas enforce the division, and enforcement deters cheating. Quota systems add a market element, letting shares be traded so that efficient fishers buy shares from others, while license systems keep access open but limited by seasons and gear. Both systems charge the fisher, not for the fish as property, but for managed access to a shared stock and for the management that keeps the stock alive.

Use and limits. Use this record to see the strongest practical answer to the note's argument: governments do charge for fish, through licenses and quotas, and the charge is defended as payment for management of a shared stock. Do not use this record to claim that any particular license fee is just, because fee levels and quota allocations are contested in every fishery. JAH-TIGC-000005 — Solar Access Laws and the Right to Sunlight

Sunlight is the note's most striking example, because no government creates the sun, yet sunlight is already the subject of law: solar access laws protect a property owner's right to receive sunlight for solar panels against shading by neighbors' buildings and trees, and some jurisdictions treat solar access as a protected interest that can be recorded as an easement. Governments also charge indirectly for sunlight's benefits, through property taxes that rise when solar installations raise property values, and through utility rules that set what solar owners are paid for power they feed to the grid. The legal picture is therefore not that sunlight is untaxed or unowned, but that the law governs the uses of sunlight, shading, collection, and sale, rather than sunlight itself.

How it works. The working method of solar access law is easement and zoning: a solar easement is a recorded agreement that a neighbor will not block sunlight to a defined collector area, and zoning rules may limit building heights or require setbacks near solar installations. Net metering rules then govern the commercial side, setting the credit a solar owner receives for exported power, which is a price set by regulators rather than by the sun. Together these rules create a market in sunlight's products, electricity and property value, without any claim that the sun itself is owned.

Use and limits. Use this record to refine the note's argument: the law does not tax sunlight as such, but it does govern and charge for the valuable uses of sunlight. Do not claim that solar access is unprotected, because easements and zoning already protect it in many places. JAH-TIGC-000012 — Grazing Fees on Public Lands

Grazing fees are charges for letting livestock graze on public lands, mainly in the American west, where the federal government owns large areas of rangeland. Ranchers pay a fee per animal per month, and the fee is set by a formula that considers forage value, beef prices, and production costs, with the revenue supporting range management, fencing, water, and restoration. The fee is famously lower than the price of grazing on private land, which has made it a long-running controversy: critics call the difference a subsidy to ranchers, while ranchers argue that public land is poorer forage, that they maintain improvements, and that the fee formula already accounts for those facts. The controversy itself is instructive, because it shows that charging for an uncreated resource does not settle the question of the right price.

How it works. The working method of grazing management is a permit system: the land agency sets a carrying capacity for each allotment, issues permits for a number of animals and a season, monitors range condition, and adjusts permits when the range degrades. Fees are only one part of the system; the permit, the season, and the capacity limit do most of the conservation work, and the fee mainly recovers part of the management cost. When range condition worsens, the agency's remedy is fewer animals or a shorter season, not simply a higher fee.

Use and limits. Use this record as a small, concrete case of charging for grass the government did not grow: the charge exists, it is contested, and its level is the real argument. Do not use grazing fees as proof that public land grazing is either a giveaway or a fair deal, because the answer depends on the comparison with private land and on range conditions that vary by allotment. JAH-TIGC-000013 — Timber Sales on Public Lands

Timber sales on public lands are auctions or contracts under which private firms pay to harvest trees from national forests and other public forests, with the price set by bidding or by appraisal and the revenue returning to the treasury or to local counties. The system is defended as a way to supply wood while funding forest management, and it is criticized when sale prices fall below the cost of building the roads and administering the sales, which amounts to a subsidy for logging. The debate turns on accounting: a sale that covers its costs and leaves a healthy forest is a reasonable use of a public resource, while a sale that loses money and degrades the forest is not, and both kinds have occurred.

How it works. The working method of a timber sale is planning first: foresters survey a stand, set harvest limits that protect water, wildlife, and regeneration, mark the trees or areas to be cut, and then offer the sale. After harvest, the purchaser must meet reforestation and road conditions, and the agency monitors regrowth. The sale price reflects species, volume, access, and market conditions, which is why similar forests can produce very different revenues in different years.

Use and limits. Use this record to see another routine charge for an uncreated resource, trees that grew without government planting, where the argument is about price and stewardship rather than about whether charging is possible. Do not use timber sales as proof that public forests should be logged or preserved in general, because each sale's merits depend on its stand, its price, and its conditions. JAH-TIGC-000014 — Beach Access and Shoreline Ownership

Shoreline ownership varies by state, but the pattern everywhere is a public interest in the shore: in many states the public owns the wet sand below the high tide line and holds a right of access, while the dry sand above it may be private, and in a few states the public's right extends across the dry sand to the vegetation line. The result is a patchwork in which a beachgoer may lawfully walk the water's edge everywhere, may lawfully use the dry sand in some states, and may be trespassing in others, and the patchwork generates constant disputes between homeowners and the public. Governments charge for beach access indirectly, through parking fees, park entrance fees, and the cost of maintaining access points, and directly where they operate public beaches.

How it works. The working method of shoreline law is boundary drawing: surveyors and courts fix the high tide line, the vegetation line, or another marker, and access rules follow the boundary. Where the boundary moves with erosion and storms, the law moves with it, which is why shoreline disputes recur after major storms. Some states have strengthened public access by statute, guaranteeing perpendicular access points and lateral passage, while others leave access to negotiation with private owners.

Use and limits. Use this record to see how the note's water example plays out at the shore: the public's right exists, but its extent depends on the state and on a moving natural boundary. Do not assume that all beaches are public or all private, because the answer is state by state and tide by tide. JAH-TIGC-000004 — Water Rights: Riparian Rules and Prior Appropriation

Water law in the United States splits into two traditions that answer the same question, who may use water they did not create, in opposite ways. Riparian doctrine, dominant in the wetter east, ties water use to ownership of land beside the water: a riverside owner may use the water reasonably, without unreasonably harming downstream owners. Prior appropriation, dominant in the drier west, follows first in time, first in right: the first user to divert water for a beneficial use holds the senior right, even if their land does not touch the stream. Both systems charge for water in practice, through permits, fees, and infrastructure costs, and both treat water as a shared resource that private use must respect.

How it works. The working method of each system is allocation under scarcity. Riparian systems ask whether a use is reasonable against other riverside owners, and courts balance competing reasonable uses when water runs short. Appropriation systems record diversion dates and quantities, and in a shortage senior rights are satisfied before junior rights receive anything, which is why western water rights are bought, sold, and litigated as valuable property. Modern states blend the systems, adding permits, groundwater rules, and environmental flow protections that neither tradition originally contained.

Use and limits. Use this record to show that water, the note's first example, is already governed by elaborate charging and allocation systems in every state. Do not use either tradition as proof that water is free, because both traditions exist precisely to manage competing claims to a resource no one created. JAH-TIGC-000006 — Spectrum Auctions: Charging for the Airwaves

The radio spectrum, the range of frequencies used for broadcasting, mobile phones, and wireless data, is a resource no government created, yet governments worldwide charge billions for its use through spectrum auctions and license fees. The legal basis is that spectrum is a scarce shared resource: two transmitters on the same frequency in the same place interfere, so someone must allocate frequencies, and allocation by auction both assigns the frequencies and collects revenue for the public. Auction winners receive licenses for defined frequencies, areas, and periods, and they pay for that exclusive use, which is why mobile networks cost what they do and why spectrum auctions are major fiscal events.

How it works. The working method of a spectrum auction is design under scarcity: regulators define license blocks by frequency and geography, set auction rules that discourage collusion and encourage efficient assignment, run the auction, and collect payment over the license term. Unlicensed spectrum also exists, for uses like Wi-Fi, where the rule is not payment but compliance with technical limits that keep interference low. The combination shows two ways to manage a shared invisible resource: charge for exclusive use, or allow free use under technical rules.

Use and limits. Use this record as the clearest large-scale example of charging for an uncreated resource: the airwaves are not made by any government, and governments charge for them openly. Do not use spectrum auctions as proof that every commons should be auctioned, because unlicensed spectrum shows that free shared use is also a workable model. JAH-TIGC-000007 — Airspace Rights and the Sky Above Land

Airspace law answers the note's air example with a compromise: landowners own the airspace above their land only so far as they can reasonably use it, while the navigable airspace above that is a public highway for aviation, managed by the government. The old maxim that ownership extended to the heavens gave way when aviation made that rule unworkable, and modern law draws a line between the low airspace a landowner actually uses, where trespass and nuisance rules still apply, and the higher airspace where aircraft may pass without permission. Governments charge for the use of that public airspace indirectly, through aviation taxes, airport fees, and air traffic services, and they regulate drones in the low airspace where landowner and public interests meet.

How it works. The working method of airspace law is layered: property law governs the low layer, aviation law governs the navigable layer, and drone rules now govern the contested middle layer where hobby and commercial drones fly. A drone over a backyard can be both a lawful flight in navigable airspace and a nuisance or privacy invasion, depending on height, purpose, and local rules, which is why drone regulation is still evolving. The layers show that air, like water, is governed by dividing it into zones with different rules rather than by declaring it wholly free or wholly owned.

Use and limits. Use this record to answer the note's air claim precisely: untreated air itself is not taxed, but the uses of airspace are governed, zoned, and charged. Do not claim that landowners own the sky, because aviation law settled that question against unlimited upward ownership decades ago. JAH-TIGC-000008 — The Argument: Tax Only What Government Establishes

The note's argument deserves a fair statement before it is judged: it claims that a government may legitimately tax only the markets and systems it establishes, because taxation is a charge for a service or a system provided, and a government that did not create air, water, sunlight, or fish provides no service in their creation. On this view, charging for an inherent good is a racket, because the charge is enforced by systems placed around the good, fences, licenses, and meters, rather than by any contribution to the good itself. The argument's strength is its clarity: it forces any tax on a natural resource to justify itself, and it exposes cases where fees function as rent extraction rather than as payment for management. Its weakness, as the other records in this database show, is that governments routinely do charge for natural resources, and the charges are usually defended as payment for management, allocation, and conservation rather than for creation.

How it works. The working method for judging the argument is to separate three questions that the argument runs together: whether the government created the resource, whether the government manages access to it, and whether the charge matches the management cost. A charge that matches management cost is defensible even on the argument's own terms, because the government did establish the management system. A charge far above management cost, or one that grants a private party exclusive control of a shared resource, is harder to defend, because it looks like the racket the argument describes. Courts and legislatures apply versions of this test when they review fees, requiring that a fee bear a reasonable relationship to the service or regulation it funds.

Use and limits. Use this record to hold both sides honestly: the argument is a genuine challenge to resource taxation, and the management defense is a genuine answer, and the real question in each case is whether the charge matches the management. Do not present the argument as established law, because it is a philosophical and political argument, not a court holding. JAH-TIGC-000009 — Carbon Pricing and the Cost of Pollution

Carbon pricing turns the note's logic inside out: instead of charging for a resource the government did not create, it charges for damage done to a shared resource, the atmosphere, that no one owns and everyone uses. A carbon tax sets a price per ton of carbon dioxide emitted, and a cap-and-trade system sets a total emissions cap and lets firms trade permits within it. Both systems make polluters pay for using the atmosphere as a dump, and both are defended as correcting a market failure: without a price, polluting is free to the polluter and costly to everyone else. The revenue may fund clean energy, cut other taxes, or return to citizens as dividends, and the choice of revenue use is itself a policy debate.

How it works. The working method of carbon pricing is measurement plus price: emissions must be measured or estimated by fuel and process, the price must be high enough to change behavior but predictable enough for planning, and the system must guard against leakage, where production moves to places without a price. Cap-and-trade adds a trading layer, letting firms that cut emissions cheaply sell permits to firms for which cutting is expensive, so the cap is met at the lowest total cost. Both designs face the same political problem, that the price is visible while the avoided damage is diffuse, which is why carbon prices are often set lower than economists recommend.

Use and limits. Use this record to show the mirror image of the note's argument: here the government charges not for creating a good but for protecting a commons from harm. Do not use carbon pricing as proof that any pollution charge is fair, because price levels, exemptions, and revenue uses determine fairness in each design. JAH-TIGC-000010 — Land Value Taxation: Taxing Location, Not Improvement

Land value taxation, associated with the nineteenth-century economist Henry George, proposes taxing the value of land itself while exempting the buildings and improvements on it, on the ground that land value comes from location and community, not from the owner's effort. A plot near a city center is valuable because of roads, jobs, schools, and neighbors, all created by others, so taxing that location value returns a socially created gain to the public, while exempting improvements rewards owners for building and maintaining. The idea has been tried in various forms, from full land taxes in some cities to split-rate systems that tax land more heavily than buildings, and it remains a live proposal in tax reform debates.

How it works. The working method of a land value tax is assessment in two parts: assessors estimate what the land alone would sell for, ignoring buildings, and apply the tax rate to that land value. The difficulty is practical, because land and buildings are usually sold together and separating their values requires judgment, and the politics are difficult, because owners who bought land at prices reflecting low taxes resist a change. Supporters argue the tax discourages speculation and encourages development of vacant city lots; critics argue it burdens owners on fixed incomes whose land rose in value through no action of their own.

Use and limits. Use this record to see a tax philosophy close to the note's argument but reaching the opposite conclusion: because land value is socially created, it is the fairest thing to tax. JAH-TIGC-000011 — Commons Trusts: The Alaska Permanent Fund Model

The Alaska Permanent Fund is the best-known commons trust: Alaska's constitution dedicates a share of oil revenue to a fund owned by the state's residents, the fund invests that revenue, and residents receive an annual dividend from its earnings. The model treats oil, a resource the state did not create, as the common property of its people, and it answers the distribution question that severance taxes leave open: instead of spending the revenue through government budgets alone, it pays a share directly to every resident. The fund's size and its dividends vary with oil revenue and investment returns, and the model has inspired proposals for trusts based on other resources, from spectrum to carbon permits to data.

How it works. The working method of a commons trust has three parts: a revenue stream from a shared resource, a fund that invests and preserves the principal, and a distribution rule that pays earnings to beneficiaries. The trust form matters because it constrains politicians: the principal cannot be spent casually, and the dividend rule is public, so residents can see what their share is worth. Critics note that dividends can become politically untouchable even when budgets are short, and that tying citizen income to oil revenue can discourage climate policy, which shows that even a popular trust design has trade-offs.

Use and limits. Use this record as a working example of the note's idea turned into an institution: a resource no one created, held in trust, with its value shared directly. Do not use Alaska's model as proof that every commons should pay dividends, because the model depends on a large, concentrated revenue stream that most commons lack. JAH-TIGC-000015 — Wind Rights and Offshore Leases

Wind rights are the newest commons to be leased: offshore wind developers pay for leases on areas of the outer continental shelf, bid competitively for those areas, and then pay rent and royalties if their projects produce power. The system copies oil and gas leasing, but for a resource that is never depleted, wind, which raises a fresh version of the note's question: if the wind is not used up, what exactly is being sold? The answer given by leasing law is not the wind itself but the exclusive right to occupy a defined area of seabed and to capture the wind passing through it for a defined period, with conditions for environmental review, fishing compensation, and decommissioning. Onshore wind raises a parallel question, because a wind farm's turbines capture wind that would otherwise cross a neighbor's land, and some jurisdictions have begun to treat wind as a severable interest that can be leased separately from the land.

How it works. The working method of offshore leasing is staged: the government identifies wind energy areas, conducts environmental review, auctions leases, and then requires site assessment, construction, and operations plans before any turbine is built. Each stage has its own public comment and its own conditions, and a lease that passes one stage can still fail the next. The staged method is deliberately slow, because offshore wind combines energy policy, fishing livelihoods, navigation, and marine habitat in a single decision.

Use and limits. Use this record to see the commons question in its newest form: a resource that is free, moving, and undepletable is still leased, zoned, and charged for, because exclusive occupation of space is what the lease sells. Do not use wind leases as proof that wind itself is owned, because the lease is for seabed occupation and capture rights, not for the wind. JAH-TIGC-000016 — Inherent Property and Identity: Name, Signature, and DNA in Law

The note's deepest claim concerns inherent property: a name, a signature, and DNA are attributes of a person that no government creates, and the question is whether they can be taxed, owned, or taken like other property. The law's answer is mixed and careful. A person's name and likeness are protected by publicity rights in many states, which let people control commercial uses of their identity, but those rights are limited, vary by state, and do not make identity untaxable income when it is licensed for money. A signature is treated as evidence of intent and as a means of authentication, not as a taxable asset in itself, though forgery and identity theft are crimes because they misuse it. DNA is protected by genetic privacy laws that limit how genetic information may be collected, used, and disclosed, and by laws against genetic discrimination, but DNA as a physical sample is handled through consent and biobank rules rather than through property taxation. In short, the law protects identity attributes without treating them as ordinary taxable property, and it taxes the income derived from them, such as endorsement earnings, rather than the attributes themselves.

How it works. The working method for analyzing any inherent property claim is to separate the attribute from its uses: the attribute itself, the name or the DNA, is protected by privacy and publicity rules; the uses, such as licensing a name for advertising or selling genetic data with consent, are governed by contract and by tax law on the income they produce. This separation explains why a person is not taxed for having a name, but is taxed on the money earned by licensing it, and why a signature is not taxed but a forged signature is a crime. The same separation applies to the note's other examples: air is not taxed, but the income from selling bottled air or from operating an airport is.

Use and limits. Use this record to answer the note's identity argument precisely: identity attributes are protected, not taxed as such, and the tax falls on income from their commercial use. Do not claim that identity is fully owned or fully unowned, because the law gives it a hybrid status that fits neither simple category.
