The Golden Gate Strait and the Idea of a Bridge Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: historical-claim Historical claim: the strait, its name, and the bridge dimensions are documented public history, stated here only as the published sources state them, with gaps named rather than filled.

The Golden Gate strait, the mile-wide channel between San Francisco Bay and the Pacific Ocean, and the long-discussed idea of bridging it.

The Golden Gate is a strait, which in plain words is a narrow channel of water joining two larger bodies of water. Here it joins San Francisco Bay to the Pacific Ocean, and for the people of San Francisco and Marin County it was a barrier: every crossing meant a ferry ride, and every ferry ride meant waiting on weather, schedules, and fares. The problem this record states is the one the bridge had to solve: how to carry a road across open, deep, fast-moving water, in fog and wind, without blocking the ships that used the channel. The strait is about a mile wide at its narrowest, and its tides run strong. Any bridge would need towers standing in open water, a span far longer than any road bridge yet built, and cables strong enough to hold a roadway high above the ships. The idea of such a bridge was discussed for decades before it was built, and the discussion itself is part of the record: a bridge across the Golden Gate was an idea before it was a plan, and a plan before it was steel.

History

The strait took its name in 1846, when John C. Fremont called it the Golden Gate, after the Golden Horn of Constantinople, because it was the entrance to a great harbor. For the rest of the nineteenth century the strait was crossed by ferry, and the city of San Francisco grew up on one side while Marin County stayed largely rural on the other. Talk of a bridge ran through those decades, but the span was too long, the water too deep, and the money too great for any proposal to hold. The idea survived because the need survived: the ferry crossing was slow, and the counties north of the bay wanted a road of their own. When Joseph Strauss put forward his proposal, he was answering a question the region had been asking for a generation, and the bridge that followed was built across the very channel Fremont had named.

The record

A traveler in the ferry era crossed the strait by boat, in weather that could delay the crossing and in fog that could hide the far shore. The bridge changed that crossing into a drive of minutes: a roadway 90 feet wide, six lanes of traffic with sidewalks on either side, carried 746 feet above the water at the towers and spanning 4,200 feet between them. The main span alone is longer than most bridges of its day were in total, and the whole structure runs 8,981 feet, about 1.7 miles, from shore to shore. The example shows what the idea had to become: not a shorter ferry, but a road in the air, held up by steel cables over water that no pier could cross.

Results as reported

The published record reports that the bridge as built spans the strait with a main span of 4,200 feet and a total length of 8,981 feet, carrying U.S. Route 101 on six lanes with sidewalks, its towers rising 746 feet above the water. The District reports the bridge was completed in the spring of 1937, under budget, at a construction cost reported as about 33.7 million dollars by SAH Archipedia against 35 million dollars in county bonds. The strait that once forced every crossing onto a ferry now carries a road in the air, and the published figures for that road are the ones this record repeats.

Science proof

The science of bridging a strait is the science of spanning and anchoring. A suspension bridge, in plain words, is a bridge whose roadway hangs from cables draped between towers, and a catenary, in plain words, is the curve a cable makes when it hangs freely between two supports under its own weight. The Golden Gate's main cables follow that curve between the towers and down to the anchorages, which in plain words are the great concrete blocks on each shore that hold the cable ends fast. The deck hangs from the main cables on vertical suspender cables, in plain words short steel ropes that carry the roadway's weight up to the main cable. The engineering problem was to make that hanging roadway stiff enough not to sway dangerously in wind, which is where deflection theory, in plain words the study of how much a bridge bends and moves under load, came in. Joseph Strauss and the First Proposal Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: historical-claim Historical claim: Strauss as chief engineer, his dates, and the opening dates are documented public history, stated here only as the published sources state them.

Joseph Baermann Strauss, chief engineer of the Golden Gate Bridge, and the proposal that turned the idea of a bridge into a plan.

A bridge across the Golden Gate needed more than an idea: it needed an engineer willing to stake his name on a span no one had built before, and a public body willing to pay for it. The problem Strauss faced was both technical and political. Technically, the main span had to be longer than any suspension span in service, the towers had to stand in open water, and the whole structure had to survive wind, fog, and earthquakes. Politically, the bridge had to be paid for without state or federal funding, by counties that had never built anything of this size. Strauss proposed the bridge, became its chief engineer, and carried the project through the years of argument that came before a single cable was spun. The problem this record states is how one engineer moved a discussed idea into a designed and funded bridge.

History

Joseph Baermann Strauss was born in 1870 and died in 1938, a year after the bridge opened. He was a bridge engineer by trade, and he took up the Golden Gate proposal and made it his own, promoting it to the counties around the bay and defending it against engineers and officials who said the span could not be built or could not be paid for. As chief engineer he held the project together through design, financing, and construction, and he signed the Report of the Chief Engineer to the Board of Directors of the Golden Gate Bridge and Highway District, dated September 1937 and published in January 1938. That report is the primary source for this archive: its full text runs to 22,415 lines, and it names the engineering staff who built the bridge. Strauss did not live long to see his bridge in service, but the bridge stands as the work he is remembered for.

The record

Strauss the promoter and Strauss the engineer were the same man doing two jobs at once. As promoter he carried the proposal to the counties and argued that a bridge could be built across the strait and paid for with bonds repaid from tolls. As chief engineer he oversaw the design and the construction, from the tower piers in open water to the spinning of the main cables, and he reported on the whole work to the District board in September 1937. The example shows the shape of his job: the proposal was not a drawing handed over and forgotten, but a project he led from argument to opening day, and the report he signed is the record of how it was done.

Methods

Read the Chief Engineer's Report of 1937, the primary source, and the District history for Strauss as chief engineer; Record Strauss by his full name and dates, Joseph Baermann Strauss, 1870 to 1938, as the published sources give them; Trace the proposal from idea to funded project, naming the financing by county bonds as the District reports it; Check the engineering staff named in the report against the report text itself; State plainly where the sources checked give no date for the first proposal, rather than supplying one.

Results as reported

The published record reports that Strauss carried the proposal from idea to opening day: construction began on 5 January 1933, the bridge was completed in the spring of 1937, it opened to pedestrians on 27 May 1937 and to vehicles on 28 May 1937, and it came in under budget. The Chief Engineer's Report he signed in September 1937, published in January 1938, stands as the project's own account of itself, naming the staff, the contractors, and the work in full. Those are the results this record reports, and they are the results the sources checked agree on.

Skeptical analysis

The honest limits of this record begin with credit. Strauss was chief engineer and the public face of the project, but the structural design and calculations were the work of Charles Alton Ellis, and the record of Ellis's firing in November 1931 and his later unpaid work is part of the story this archive tells in its own record. A reader who takes Strauss as the sole designer would be misled, and this record says so plainly. The sources checked also give no single dated first proposal, so this record does not invent one, and the proposal story is told as a campaign over years rather than as a dated event.
