Energy Budget Rank 1 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

An energy budget totals every load in a lighting system and sets it against its supply, so the design's power claim can be checked by addition rather than trusted.

Public energy budgeting lists each load class — facade lighting, street lighting, interior lighting, controls, and auxiliary systems — with its wattage and its operating hours, then sums them into daily and annual energy. The budget states its assumptions beside its totals: which hours, which dimming levels, and which season, because a budget without hours is a wattage list, not an energy claim. A budget whose loads do not add to its total is wrong, and a budget whose supply does not cover its total is a design problem, not a rounding error.

Working demo program

# Energy Budget
facade_w = 4000
street_w = 6000
interior_w = 8000
controls_w = 500
total_w = facade_w + street_w + interior_w + controls_w
hours_per_day = 12
daily_kwh = total_w * hours_per_day / 1000
print("Total connected load in watts: %s" % (total_w,))
print("Daily energy at 12 hours in kWh: %s" % (daily_kwh,))

Load Estimation Rank 2 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Load estimation predicts a lighting system's electrical load before it is built, from fixture counts, wattages, and diversity factors.

Public estimation multiplies fixture counts by per-fixture wattage including driver losses, then applies a diversity factor where not every zone runs at full level at once. Driver losses are stated, not absorbed silently into the fixture wattage, and the diversity factor is justified per zone — a festival plaza and a maintenance corridor do not share one factor honestly. The estimate is checked after commissioning against metered load, and the difference is recorded as estimating error, which is how the next estimate gets better.

Working demo program

# Load Estimation
fixtures = 240
w_per_fixture = 45
driver_loss_pct = 0.1
raw_w = fixtures * w_per_fixture
total_w = raw_w * (1 + driver_loss_pct)
diversity = 0.8
demand_w = total_w * diversity
print("Raw fixture load in watts: %s" % (raw_w,))
print("Load including driver losses in watts: %s" % (round(total_w),))
print("Estimated demand at 0.8 diversity in watts: %s" % (round(demand_w),))

Energy Balance Computation Rank 3 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Energy balance computation checks that supply, storage flow, and consumption balance over a chosen period, so no energy is created or lost in the accounts.

Public balance practice writes the equation plainly: energy supplied plus energy drawn from storage equals energy consumed plus energy stored plus losses, over the same period. Every term is metered or estimated with its method stated, and the residual — the difference between the two sides — is reported, not hidden.

A residual within the metering tolerance confirms the balance; a residual outside it means a meter, a load, or a loss term is wrong, and the balance record says which is being investigated. Balancing the books is not the same as powering the city, and this record claims only the arithmetic.

Working demo program

# Energy Balance Computation
supplied_kwh = 500
from_storage_kwh = 60
consumed_kwh = 480
to_storage_kwh = 40
losses_kwh = 35
lhs = supplied_kwh + from_storage_kwh
rhs = consumed_kwh + to_storage_kwh + losses_kwh
residual = lhs - rhs
print("Supply side in kWh: %s" % (lhs,))
print("Use side in kWh: %s" % (rhs,))
print("Residual in kWh: %s" % (residual,))

Power Distribution Rank 4 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Power distribution carries electrical power from its source to its loads through feeders, panels, and branch circuits, sized so voltage stays within tolerance at the farthest fixture.

Public distribution practice sizes conductors for both current capacity and voltage drop, protects each circuit with a breaker matched to its conductor, and documents every panel schedule: circuit, load, breaker, and conductor size. Voltage drop is computed for the longest run at full load, because a system that works at the panel and fails at the far fixture is not a working system. Distribution for lighting is separated from distribution for other loads where the design requires it, so a non-lighting fault cannot darken a walkway.

Working demo program

# Power Distribution
load_a = 12
length_m = 150
resistivity = 0.0175
area_mm2 = 16
voltage_v = 230
current_a = load_a * 1000 / voltage_v
drop_v = 2 * length_m * resistivity * current_a / area_mm2
print("Load current in amps: %s" % (round(current_a, 1),))
print("Voltage drop in volts: %s" % (round(drop_v, 2),))
print("Drop as percent of 230 V: %s" % (round(drop_v / voltage_v * 100, 2),))

Power Microgrid Rank 5 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

A power microgrid is a local energy system that can run connected to the main grid or islanded on its own generation and storage, with a defined switch between the two.

Public microgrid practice names its generation sources, its storage, its controllable loads, and its point of connection, and it proves islanding by actually disconnecting in a test window and running the islanded loads. The microgrid controller sheds non-essential loads first when generation falls short, protects the island's frequency and voltage, and resynchronises before reconnecting to the main grid. A microgrid that has never islanded in a test is a design, not an operating microgrid, and this record labels it accordingly.

Working demo program

# Power Microgrid
generation_kw = 120
storage_kw = 40
load_kw = 140
deficit_kw = load_kw - generation_kw - storage_kw
print("Generation in kW: %s" % (generation_kw,))
print("Storage discharge in kW: %s" % (storage_kw,))
print("Load in kW: %s" % (load_kw,))
print("Shortfall in kW (negative means surplus): %s" % (deficit_kw,))

Storage and Backup Rank 6 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Storage and backup hold energy for use when generation stops or the main feed fails, sized in both power and energy so neither rating is silently short.

Public storage practice sizes a battery or backup system twice: its power rating must cover the load it backs, and its energy rating must cover that load for the required duration. Both ratings are stated, with the usable energy distinguished from the nameplate energy, because depth-of-discharge limits and conversion losses reduce what can actually be delivered. Backup systems are tested by load test on a schedule, and a battery that has never delivered its rated load for its rated duration is not yet proven at that rating.

Backup Capacity Rank 7 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Backup capacity is the computed answer to one question: how long can the essential loads run when the normal supply fails.

Public capacity computation lists the essential loads separately from the sheddable loads, sums only the essential loads, and divides the usable stored energy by that sum to get autonomy hours. Sheddable loads are excluded from the autonomy claim, because a backup sized for everything and a backup sized for essentials are different promises, and confusing them is how a backup fails its first real outage. The capacity record states its load list, its usable energy, and its resulting hours, and it is re-computed whenever either input changes.

Working demo program

# Backup Capacity

usable_kwh = 240
autonomy_h = usable_kwh / essential_kw
print("Essential load in kW: %s" % (essential_kw,))
print("Usable stored energy in kWh: %s" % (usable_kwh,))
print("Autonomy in hours: %s" % (autonomy_h,))

Cooling and Heat Rejection Rank 8 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Cooling and heat rejection remove the waste heat that lighting and power equipment produce, so drivers, controllers, and enclosures stay within their rated temperatures.

Public cooling practice totals the heat each device rejects, then sizes ventilation, heat sinks, or active cooling to carry that heat away at the design ambient temperature, with a margin. Enclosure temperatures are measured at the hottest device, not at the enclosure average, because the hottest device is the one that fails first. A cooling design that works at a mild ambient and fails at the site's summer maximum is not a cooling design for that site, and this record requires the design ambient to be stated.

# Cooling and Heat Rejection
heat_w = 2500
airflow_m3h = 500
air_density = 1.2
cp = 1005
mass_flow = airflow_m3h * air_density / 3600
dt = heat_w / (mass_flow * cp)
print("Heat to reject in watts: %s" % (heat_w,))
print("Air temperature rise in kelvin: %s" % (round(dt, 1),))

Thermal Simulation Rank 9 · feasibility: achievable

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Thermal simulation predicts temperatures across a space or an enclosure from heat sources, materials, and ventilation, before anything is built or installed.

Public thermal simulation builds a nodal or computational model: heat sources with wattages, surfaces with thermal properties, airflows with rates, and boundary conditions with ambient temperatures. The model is validated against at least one measured case — a comparable enclosure or a prototype — and its validation error is stated, because an unvalidated model is a colourful guess. Simulation results are reported with their boundary conditions attached, since the same model gives different temperatures under different ambients, and a result without its ambient is unrepeatable.

print("Predicted surface temperature in Celsius: %s" % (surface_c,))

Engineering view

Light layout is the plan of where luminaires go, at what spacing and height, so a street or facade meets its illuminance and uniformity targets.

Public layout practice starts from the target illuminance and uniformity, then chooses luminaire spacing from the manufacturer's utilisation data and the mounting height, and checks the result by point calculation at the design grid. Spacing-to-height ratios are stated, because a layout that looks even on paper and fails at the grid is a failed layout. Layout is the bridge between the control pair's uniformity controller and this pair's photometric tests: the layout proposes, the controller trims, and the tests verify, and the three records cross-link rather than repeat each other.

Engineering view

Photometric tests measure a lighting installation's actual light output and distribution with calibrated instruments, and compare the results with the design intent.

Public photometric testing measures illuminance at the design grid with a calibrated lux meter, and measures luminaire output and distribution in a laboratory or with a goniophotometer where distribution data is required. Each test record names its instrument, its calibration date, its grid, its tolerance, and its result per point.

This pair owns the test procedures; the light-controls pair owns verification as a control loop, and the two cross-link rather than duplicate. A test with an uncalibrated meter is not a test, whatever its numbers say.

Glare Safety Rank 13 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:23-25 pictures a city whose light needs no sun or moon and whose gates never shut by day, with no night there. That picture is the design brief for radiant-city lighting; it is not, by itself, evidence that any particular control system achieves it.

Engineering view

Glare safety protects viewers from dazzling light in a radiant installation, using viewer positions, shielding, and dimming limits that are verified by measurement.

Public glare safety starts from the same viewer positions as glare-aware dimming, then adds safety rules: no bare high-intensity source in a normal line of sight, shielding angles stated per luminaire type, and a maximum luminance toward viewer positions that is checked with a luminance meter, not estimated from wattage. Where a radiant surface is itself the design — a glowing facade or street — its luminance is measured and compared with the comfort limit for its viewing distance, and the design is adjusted by dimming or diffusion when it exceeds it. Safety limits are not aesthetic preferences; they protect eyesight and attention on walkways and roads.

Microgrid Resilience Rank 14 · feasibility: achievable

Engineering view

Microgrid resilience is the measured ability of a microgrid to keep essential loads running through faults, outages, and generation shortfalls, over stated durations.

Public resilience practice scores a microgrid on defined events: a main-grid outage of stated duration, a generator failure, a storage outage, and a sudden load step, each with its required essential-load survival. The resilience record reports, per event, whether essentials survived and for how long, and the overall score is the share of events survived, not a single heroic test. A microgrid that survives one short outage and fails the rest is not resilient; it is lucky once, and this record says so.

Energy Acceptance Tests Rank 16 · feasibility: achievable

Text-faithful view

engineering system — no direct passage: this record describes a control, measurement, or energy system rather than a passage of Revelation, stated plainly so the engineering target is never confused with the text.

Engineering view

Energy acceptance tests are the pass-or-fail checks a city energy system must clear before handover, written before the system is tested.

The acceptance set covers energy budget addition, load estimation versus metered load, energy balance residual, voltage drop at the farthest fixture, islanding, storage duration at rated load, backup autonomy for essentials, cooling at design ambient, photometric grid results, glare safety from viewer positions, and resilience event survival. Each test names its method, its tolerance, and its pass condition in advance. A system passes acceptance when every test passes or every failure is formally accepted with a reason and a retest date; a system with an unrecorded failure has not been accepted.
