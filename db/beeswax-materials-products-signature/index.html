Beeswax Grades: Yellow, White, and Filtered simple · grades · beginner

Signature edition: an original Signature design in the same family. Beeswax grades describe the wax itself as a material: its color, clarity, composition, and melting behavior. Yellow beeswax is the natural rendered wax, white beeswax is the same wax lightened by air and sun or by filtering, and filtered grades are graded by how clean and clear they are. All grades share the same core composition, mostly esters of long-chain alcohols and acids with hydrocarbons, and the same melting range near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius. This record specifies the grades and their test figures. It is a materials record; the beeswax-uses records cover methods such as candle making and polishing, while this record covers the wax grades those methods start from. This is an original design, not a historical public record.

Materials

  • Raw beeswax cappings and comb
  • Rendering pot and strainer (double boiler only)
  • Fine filter cloth
  • Molds for blocks
  • Thermometer
  • Color and clarity samples
  • Scale

Method

  1. Render cappings and old comb gently in a double boiler and strain the liquid wax through fine cloth.
  2. Let the wax settle and cool, then scrape any sediment from the underside of the block.
  3. Grade the block by color: deep gold for yellow grade, pale cream for light grade, and near-white for white grade.
  4. Re-melt and re-filter any block with visible sediment, and record the grade on the block.
  5. Store graded blocks cool, dry, and labeled, away from dust and strong smells, since wax picks up odors.

Safety

Flammability and heat: beeswax is flammable and melts near 62 to 64 degrees Celsius. Melt it in a double boiler or a dedicated wax melter, never over a direct flame, keep water away from hot wax, and keep a lid nearby to smother any flare. Work in a ventilated room, keep children and pets out of the work area, and never leave hot wax unattended. Blends are labeled with their actual composition and are never presented as pure beeswax. Made without any slaughter-derived ingredient; plant oils and plant waxes are used in blends. Rendering uses hot wax and hot water: keep the work area ventilated and keep water out of the melting wax pot. Wax blocks are not food unless they are certified food-grade (E901).

Yield

A 500 g batch of filtered wax fills 5 blocks; see the demo.

Variations

  • Yellow grade for general craft.
  • Light grade for pale products.
  • White grade for light-colored products.
  • Triple-filtered grade for fine work.

Beeswax Furniture Polish Paste simple · polishes · beginner

Signature edition: an original Signature design in the same family. Beeswax furniture polish paste is a finished product: a soft paste of beeswax, a harder plant wax for durability, and plant oil, sold in tins for rubbing into wood. The product is specified by its wax share, hardness, coverage, and finish, so a buyer can compare pastes instead of guessing from a recipe. The beeswax-uses record on wood polish covers the method of polishing; this record covers the paste as a product, with its grades and coverage figures. Materials

  • Beeswax, graded
  • Carnauba wax (plant wax) for hardness
  • Plant oil
  • Tins
  • Double boiler
  • Stirrer
  • Scale

Method

  1. Melt the beeswax and carnauba wax together with the plant oil in a double boiler until fully clear.
  2. Stir the blend as it cools so the waxes do not separate, and pour it into tins before it sets.
  3. Let the paste cool fully, then test its hardness by pressing a fingertip into the surface.
  4. Test coverage on a measured board and record the grams per square metre.
  5. Label each tin with the full composition, the coverage figure, and the date.

Paste for food-contact boards, such as cutting boards, must use food-safe ingredients only; general furniture paste is not used on food-contact surfaces.

Batik resist wax blends are graded wax products for resist dyeing: the wax is drawn onto cloth to protect areas from dye, and the blend sets how much the wax cracks, which makes the fine crackle lines of batik. Pure beeswax cracks little and gives smooth resists; blends with plant wax or paraffin crack more. Every blend is labeled with its actual composition, and paraffin blends are never sold as beeswax. This record specifies the resist blends as products; the beeswax-uses batik record covers the dyeing method. This is an original design, not a historical public record.

Materials

  • Plant wax for crackle blends
  • Paraffin for crackle blends (labeled as such)
  • Block molds
  • Double boiler
  • Stirrer
  • Scale

Method

  1. Melt beeswax with the blend ingredient in the grade's ratio in a double boiler until clear.
  2. Pour the blend into blocks and cool slowly.
  3. Test each blend on cloth: draw lines, dye a sample, and check the crackle pattern and resist quality.
  4. Record the crackle grade for each blend and label the blocks with full composition.
