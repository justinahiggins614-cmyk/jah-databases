Signature archive design for the Rhine card-guessing experiments: how the Signature archive organizes the Duke card tests of the 1930s as a teachable record.

The design problem for the Rhine record is one of fairness: how do you present the founding experiments of a field honestly when their results are famous, their methods are dated, and their conclusions are disputed? A design that only reports Rhine's numbers would mislead the reader; a design that only reports the critics' objections would hide why the experiments mattered. The Signature design solves this by giving the record two equal halves — the experiment as Rhine ran it, with its deck, its procedure, and its reported scores, and the criticism as it developed, with the shuffling problem, the recording problem, and the selection problem stated in full — and by putting the chance arithmetic between them, so that the reader can see exactly what the numbers say before hearing what either side says they mean. The design also has to teach: the Rhine record is the first record in the archive's ladder, so it must introduce the vocabulary — chance expectation, standard deviation, z-score — that every later record will use.

History

The design organizes the history the public record establishes: Rhine's arrival at Duke in 1930, the card tests with students, the 1934 book Extra-Sensory Perception, the founding of the Parapsychology Laboratory in 1935 and the Journal of Parapsychology in 1937, and the long series of card-guessing runs through the 1940s. The design presents that history as a timeline with three turns: the striking early results, the tightening of controls in response to criticism, and the shrinking of the effects as the controls tightened. It does not add new historical claims; it arranges the published history so that the pattern — the decline effect — is visible at a glance. The design's historical note states plainly that the laboratory's papers are held in the Duke University Archives, so that a reader who wants the primary material knows where to find it.

The experiment or case

The design's worked example is a single run of twenty-five cards, presented as the archive's first teaching example. The reader is shown a shuffled deck, five symbols, and a receiver's twenty-five calls; the calls are scored, giving seven hits against an expectation of five; the z-score is computed as one, and the design explains that one standard deviation above chance is the kind of result random guessing produces regularly. The example then shows the same run scored a second way — counting only the runs in which the receiver scored high, and ignoring the rest — and the design explains that this is the selection problem in miniature. The worked example is the design's core teaching device: it lets the reader feel, in one small run, the difference between a result and a selected result.

Methods

Open with the deck: present the five symbols and the twenty-five-card structure before any result, so the chance baseline of one in five is understood first; Present the procedure as a numbered sequence — shuffle, send, call, record, repeat — with the controls of the period stated beside each step; Present the reported scores as Rhine reported them, with the odds against chance as he computed them, and label them as reported; Present the three criticisms in order — imperfect shuffling, hand recording, and selection of high scorers — each with the evidence for it; Close with the chance arithmetic worked out in full, so the reader can check the numbers rather than take either side's word for them; Link outward to the Zener deck record, the Pearce-Pratt record, and the statistics record, so the design is a node in the archive, not an island.

Results as reported

The design reports the results as Rhine reported them — scores above the chance expectation of five in twenty-five, with a few subjects far above chance — and it labels them as reported, not as established. It reports the critics' results beside them: the demonstration that imperfect shuffling can manufacture patterns, and the observation that the effects shrank as controls tightened. The design's own result is the presentation itself: a record in which the claim and the criticism sit side by side, with the arithmetic between them, so that the reader can judge rather than be told.

Skeptical analysis

The design carries the skeptical analysis as a structural half, not as an afterthought: the shuffling problem, the recording problem, and the selection problem are presented with the same prominence as the reported scores. The design's skeptical point is that a laboratory, a journal, and a statistics textbook do not by themselves make a science — what makes a science is independent replication under conditions the skeptics accept, and the Rhine results never achieved that. The design does not soften this: it states it as the reason the telepathy claim remains unproven, and it invites the reader to check the arithmetic and the history for themselves.

Science proof

The science the design teaches is the null hypothesis, introduced here for the first time in the archive's ladder. With five symbols, a random guesser is right one time in five, so twenty-five trials should give about five hits with a standard deviation of two; the design shows that arithmetic, then shows the demo simulating it with a fixed seed, so that the reader sees random guessing produce a chance-level score with their own eyes. The design's scientific point is that a clean baseline is what makes a test a test: unlike a seance or a dream, a card call is right or wrong, and the arithmetic is exact. Working demo program

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'Rhine Card-Guessing Experiments'
trials = 25
choices = 5
seed = 1101
claimed_note = 'As reported: early Rhine series claimed average scores above the chance expectation of 5 hits in 25 trials; this design presents the claim and the criticism side by side.'

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        sent = rng.randrange(choices)
        guessed = rng.randrange(choices)
        if sent == guessed:
            hits += 1
    return hits

p = 1.0 / choices
expected = trials * p
sd = math.sqrt(trials * p * (1 - p))
hits_a = run_once()
hits_b = run_once()
z_a = (hits_a - expected) / sd
result = {"title": title, "trials": trials, "choices": choices,
          "seed": seed, "hits": hits_a, "expected": expected, "z": round(z_a, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Experiment: " + title)
print("Trials: %d  Choices per trial: %d  Chance probability: 1/%d" % (trials, choices, choices))
print("Chance expectation: %.1f hits in %d trials" % (expected, trials))
print("Simulated hits (random guessing, fixed seed %d): %d" % (seed, hits_a))
print("z-score: %.3f (hits minus expectation, divided by the chance standard deviation %.2f)" % (z_a, sd))
print("Claimed result as reported: " + claimed_note)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:
    print("Deterministic: MATCH - both runs produced the same simulated result.")
else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")
print("Note: this demo simulates random guessing only. It demonstrates the statistics of chance expectation; it cannot detect telepathy, and a high simulated hit count would be chance, not evidence.")
print("Design study: this record describes a Signature archive design, not an established public record.")

Signature archive design for the ganzfeld experiments: how the Signature archive organizes the sensory-deprivation test and its meta-analyses as a teachable record.

The design problem for the ganzfeld record is one of vividness: the experiment's results are hit rates — thirty-five percent against twenty-five percent chance — but its material is vivid and human, a receiver describing water and falling while a sender watches a waterfall. A design that only reports the hit rate would be dry and unconvincing; a design that only tells the vivid story would mislead the reader about what the numbers say. The Signature design solves this by pairing every vivid example with its arithmetic: each session description is followed by the chance baseline, the pooled hit rate, and the skeptical mechanisms that could produce it. The design also has to handle the debate: the ganzfeld is the most argued-over experiment in the archive, and the design must present Honorton's meta-analysis and Hyman's critique as a single argument with two competent sides, not as a winner and a loser.

History

The design organizes the history the public record establishes: the adaptation of the ganzfeld from Gestalt psychology, Honorton's development of the design at Maimonides in the 1970s, the 1985 meta-analysis of twenty-eight studies, the Hyman critique and the 1986 joint communique, the autoganzfeld studies of the 1990s at Princeton, and the later meta-analyses by Storm and colleagues. The design presents that history as a sequence of designs, each one built to answer the last one's critics, so that the reader sees the experiment improving over time — and sees the hit rate declining as it improves. The design's historical note states that the raw session-level data for most early studies are not publicly archived, and it says so where the numbers come from published summaries.

The experiment or case

The design's worked example is a single ganzfeld session, presented in full. The receiver, in the ganzfeld state, says 'water, falling, white, loud'; the sender has been watching a waterfall clip; the receiver is shown four clips and ranks the waterfall first. The design then asks the reader to consider the other three clips — a desert road, a city street, a forest — and to notice that 'water, falling, white, loud' matches the waterfall far better than it matches any of the others, which is the target non-independence problem in miniature: the free description is more likely to match some targets than others, even if the sender sent nothing. The example then shows the same session scored as a hit and as a near-miss under different judging rules, and the design explains that the judging rule is part of the result. The worked example teaches the reader that a hit rate is not a fact of nature but a product of a design.

Methods

Open with the state: present the ping-pong balls, the white noise, and the red light, and explain why the design quiets the senses; Present the four-choice structure before any result, so the twenty-five percent baseline is understood first; Walk through a single session — sending, free description, ranking — with the receiver's words quoted and the target revealed at the end; Present the pooled hit rates as reported, with the study counts and the meta-analyses named, and label them as reported; Present the skeptical mechanisms in order — sensory leakage, judging bias, target non-independence, and the file drawer — each with the evidence for it; Close by linking to the Honorton design, the Hyman-versus-Honorton design, and the meta-analyses design, so the debate is navigable.

Results as reported

The design reports the hit rates as reported — about thirty-five percent in Honorton's 1985 meta-analysis, about thirty-two percent in the later meta-analyses and the autoganzfeld series, against twenty-five percent chance — and it labels them as reported. It reports the critics' results beside them: the flaws Hyman found, the target-matching problem, and the decline of the hit rate as controls improved. The design's own result is the table that puts the study, the session count, the hit rate, and the controls side by side, so that the pattern is visible without commentary.

Skeptical analysis

The design carries the skeptical analysis as a structural element: the four mechanisms — leakage, judging bias, target non-independence, and the file drawer — are presented with the same prominence as the hit rates, and the design's worked example demonstrates the target-matching problem rather than merely asserting it. The design's skeptical point is that a hit rate a few points above chance is the signature of a small, persistent flaw, and that the burden is on the design to exclude the flaw, not on the skeptic to prove it. The design does not resolve the debate; it makes the debate legible, so that the reader can see exactly where the two sides disagree.

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'The Telepathy Archive Field Guide Index'
trials = 25
choices = 5
seed = 3424
claimed_note = "As reported: this index lists the archive's 24 designs and states its honesty rule — telepathy is unproven, and no design here presents a claimed result as established fact."

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        sent = rng.randrange(choices)
        guessed = rng.randrange(choices)
        if sent == guessed:
            hits += 1
    return hits

p = 1.0 / choices
expected = trials * p
sd = math.sqrt(trials * p * (1 - p))
hits_a = run_once()
hits_b = run_once()
z_a = (hits_a - expected) / sd
result = {"title": title, "trials": trials, "choices": choices,
          "seed": seed, "hits": hits_a, "expected": expected, "z": round(z_a, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Experiment: " + title)
print("Trials: %d  Choices per trial: %d  Chance probability: 1/%d" % (trials, choices, choices))
print("Chance expectation: %.1f hits in %d trials" % (expected, trials))
print("Simulated hits (random guessing, fixed seed %d): %d" % (seed, hits_a))
print("z-score: %.3f (hits minus expectation, divided by the chance standard deviation %.2f)" % (z_a, sd))
print("Claimed result as reported: " + claimed_note)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:
    print("Deterministic: MATCH - both runs produced the same simulated result.")
else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")
print("Note: this demo simulates random guessing only. It demonstrates the statistics of chance expectation; it cannot detect telepathy, and a high simulated hit count would be chance, not evidence.")
print("Design study: this record describes a Signature archive design, not an established public record.")
