What Is Biofonics Rank 1 · simple

Biofonics is the study of the sounds of living things: the calls, songs, clicks, buzzes, and rustles that organisms make, and the way those sounds travel through air, water, and ground. It draws on biology, acoustics, and ecology, and it treats a living soundscape as data, not decoration. A single morning recording from a woodland edge can hold dozens of overlapping voices, each shaped by the body that made it and the habitat it must cross. Biofonics asks what each voice is for, how far it carries, and what changes when voices disappear. The field covers three soundscape layers that must be kept distinct: biophony, the sounds of living organisms; geophony, the sounds of the earth itself, such as wind, water, and weather; and anthrophony, human-made sound, from voices and music to engines and machinery. This record covers the biophony layer, and it cross-links the soundscape records that cover the other two layers rather than blurring them together.

Science proof

Science proof: sound is a pressure wave that needs a medium. In air at about 20 degrees Celsius it travels at roughly 343 metres per second, and its wavelength equals speed divided by frequency. A 440 hertz tone therefore has a wavelength of about 0.78 metres, which is why low sounds bend around obstacles and carry further than high sounds of the same energy. Animal ears and vocal organs are tuned to this physics: large bodies make low sounds efficiently, small bodies make high sounds, and every call is a compromise between carrying distance and the risk of being overheard.

Math proof

Math proof: wavelength equals speed divided by frequency, so 343 / 440 = 0.7795 metres, about 0.78 metres. The demo prints 343 / 440 = 0.78 m, 343 / 1000 = 0.34 m, and 343 / 20000 = 0.02 m, showing that higher frequency means shorter wavelength at the same speed.

The Dawn Chorus Rank 2 · simple

The dawn chorus is the burst of bird song that rises just before and after sunrise, most famously in spring in temperate regions, when many male birds sing at once to defend territory and attract mates. It is not a choir singing in unison: each species sings on its own schedule, in its own frequency band, and from its own perch, so the chorus is a layered negotiation over acoustic space. Birds sing hardest at dawn partly because cool, still morning air carries sound well and partly because the night's fasting leaves the day's foraging still ahead, so song is cheap advertising before the work of feeding begins. The chorus fades as the sun climbs and birds switch to feeding, and it returns, quieter, at dusk. Listening to which species sing, and in what order, gives a skilled listener a census of the local breeding community without ever seeing a bird.

Science proof

Science proof: the dawn chorus is documented in ornithology as a daily peak in singing activity tied to the breeding season, territory defence, and mate attraction. Singing at dawn is energetically costly, which is why song quality and persistence can honestly signal a singer's condition to rivals and mates. Recordings made at the same site across years show the chorus changing as species arrive, depart, or decline, which is why long-term dawn-chorus recordings are used as an ecological monitoring tool.

Math proof

Math proof: if 12 species each sing for an average of 20 minutes inside a 90-minute dawn window, the total singing time is 12 x 20 = 240 minutes, an average of 240 / 90 = 2.67 species singing at any one moment. The demo prints total 240 minutes and average 2.67 simultaneous singers, plus the share of the window covered by one species, 20 / 90 = 22.2 percent.

Bernie Krause and the Biophony Concept Rank 3 · simple

Bernie Krause, an American musician turned field recordist and soundscape ecologist, gave soundscape ecology its central vocabulary. He proposed that a natural soundscape has three distinct layers: biophony, the collective sound of living organisms; geophony, the non-biological sounds of the earth, including wind, water, rain, and thunder; and anthrophony, the sounds humans make, from speech and music to engines and industrial noise. Krause argued that healthy habitats sound organised, with species partitioning frequency and time so their voices do not mask one another, and that degraded habitats sound thinned or dominated by human noise. His book The Great Animal Orchestra, published in 2012, carried the argument to a wide audience, and his field recordings, some spanning decades at the same sites, are cited as evidence that soundscapes change measurably as habitats are altered. This record covers all three layers as distinct categories, and it is the suite's authority for keeping them separate.

Science proof

Science proof: Krause's acoustic niche hypothesis states that in an undisturbed habitat, vocal species evolve to occupy distinct frequency bands and time slots, reducing acoustic competition. Field comparisons support the broad pattern: intact habitats typically show sound spread across many frequency bands, while heavily disturbed sites show gaps where species have vanished and a concentration of low-frequency human noise. The hypothesis is a working model, not a law, and later research has refined it, but the three-layer vocabulary it introduced is now standard in soundscape ecology.

Math proof

Math proof: if a habitat's audible band from 100 to 10000 hertz is divided into 10 equal bands of 990 hertz each, and 8 of the 10 bands hold biophony, the biophony band occupancy is 8 / 10 = 80.0 percent. The demo prints band width 990 Hz, occupied bands 8 of 10, and occupancy 80.0 percent, plus the anthrophony share if 2 bands are dominated by human noise, 2 / 10 = 20.0 percent.

Birdsong: Learning and Repertoire Rank 4 · simple

Many songbirds learn their songs rather than inheriting them fully formed. A young bird listens to adult tutors, practises in a quiet subsong phase, and gradually matches its output to a memorised template, a process studied most famously in songbirds such as the zebra finch and the white-crowned sparrow. Some species keep a small repertoire of a few song types, while others, such as the common nightingale, are reported to command repertoires of well over a hundred song types, and the superb lyrebird of Australia famously mimics other birds, mammals, and even mechanical sounds. Repertoire size, song complexity, and delivery rate can all signal a singer's age, experience, and condition, so a single song carries information far beyond species identity. Because song is learned, local dialects arise: birds in one valley may sing measurably different versions of the same species song than birds in the next valley.

Science proof

Science proof: laboratory and field studies show that songbirds raised without hearing adult song develop abnormal, simplified songs, while birds exposed to tutors develop normal species-typical songs, demonstrating that learning is required. Brain research has identified dedicated song-control nuclei that grow and change with the seasons in many species, linking song production to neural structures. Dialect studies, in which recordings from neighbouring populations are compared spectrogram by spectrogram, confirm that learned song varies geographically within a species.

Math proof

Math proof: if a nightingale repertoire holds 180 song types and a bird sings 40 songs in a session with no repeats, the share of the repertoire used is 40 / 180 = 22.2 percent. The demo prints repertoire 180, session songs 40, share 22.2 percent, and the sessions needed to cover the whole repertoire at 40 new songs each, 180 / 40 = 4.5 sessions.

Frog Choruses and Amphibian Calls Rank 5 · simple

On warm nights near ponds and wetlands, male frogs and toads gather and call in choruses that can be heard hundreds of metres away. Each species has a characteristic call: a trill, a peep, a banjo-like pluck, or a deep croak, produced by passing air over vocal cords and amplifying it with a vocal sac that inflates like a balloon. Calling is the main way males attract females, and females choose partly by call features such as pulse rate and dominant frequency, which correlate with body size and condition. Choruses are competitive and cooperative at once: males space themselves and alternate calls to avoid masking one another, yet the combined chorus also helps females find the breeding site and may reduce each individual's predation risk. Because amphibians are sensitive to water quality, disease, and climate, a thinning frog chorus is treated by ecologists as an early warning signal for wetland health.

Science proof

Science proof: amphibian calls are species-specific and are the standard identification tool for field surveys: trained listeners and automated recorders identify species from call structure alone. Experiments that play back altered calls show females discriminating by pulse rate and frequency, confirming that call features carry mate-choice information. Long-term acoustic monitoring programmes track calling activity across seasons to detect population declines, including declines linked to chytrid fungus disease.

Math proof

Math proof: if a chorus holds 60 calling males and each call lasts 0.5 seconds repeated every 2.0 seconds, each male's duty cycle is 0.5 / 2.0 = 25.0 percent, and the chorus produces 60 / 2.0 = 30.0 calls per second in total. The demo prints duty cycle 25.0 percent, calls per second 30.0, and calls per minute 1800.0.

Insect Sound: Crickets and Cicadas Rank 6 · simple

Insects are among the loudest small musicians on earth. Crickets sing by stridulation, rubbing a scraper on one wing against a file of teeth on the other, and the wing itself acts as a sounding board that radiates the chirp. Cicadas sing differently, using ribbed tymbal organs on the abdomen that buckle rapidly to produce pulses, amplified by hollow abdominal chambers; some cicada species are reported to reach about 120 decibels at close range, among the loudest sounds any insect makes. Both groups sing mainly to attract mates, and both are temperature-sensitive: crickets chirp faster as the air warms, a relationship formalised in Dolbear's law, which estimates air temperature from chirp rate. On a summer night, the insect layer often dominates the high-frequency band of the biophony, filling the space above bird song.

Science proof

Science proof: stridulation and tymbal mechanisms are documented by direct observation and high-speed imaging: the tooth-strike rate of a cricket's file sets the pulse rate of its chirp, and the tymbal buckling rate sets a cicada's pulse rate. Dolbear's law, published by Amos Dolbear in 1897, relates chirp rate to temperature and has been repeatedly tested: the snowy tree cricket's chirp count tracks thermometer readings closely enough that the relationship is still taught as a field-estimation method.

Math proof

Math proof: Dolbear's law in Fahrenheit is temperature = 50 + (chirps per minute - 40) / 4. For 120 chirps per minute, temperature = 50 + (120 - 40) / 4 = 70.0 degrees Fahrenheit. The demo prints 70.0 for 120 chirps, 60.0 for 80 chirps, and 80.0 for 160 chirps, each computed by the same formula.

Bat Echolocation Rank 7 · compounded

Bats that hunt in darkness navigate and find prey by echolocation: they emit ultrasonic pulses, mostly above the range of human hearing, and listen to the returning echoes to build a picture of their surroundings. Pulse design varies by hunting style. Open-air hunters use long, narrowband calls that carry far; clutter specialists use short, broadband frequency-modulated sweeps that resolve fine detail among leaves; and some bats adjust pulse rate as they close on prey, ending in a rapid feeding buzz of many pulses per second. Echo delay tells distance, echo strength and Doppler shift tell size and movement, and the two ears together tell direction. A bat can therefore intercept a moth in total darkness with a precision no human sense matches unaided. Echolocation calls are biophony of a special kind: they are communication with the environment itself, not with another animal.

Science proof

Science proof: echolocation was demonstrated experimentally in the 20th century when researchers showed that bats with blocked ears, but not bats with blocked mouths or eyes, failed to avoid obstacles, and later work recorded the actual ultrasonic pulses with specialised microphones. Neurophysiological studies mapped auditory neurons tuned to echo delay, confirming that bat brains compute target distance from the time between pulse and echo. Species identification from recorded calls is now a standard survey method.

Math proof

Math proof: echo distance equals speed of sound times delay divided by two, because the sound travels out and back. For a delay of 0.006 seconds, distance = 343 x 0.006 / 2 = 1.029 metres, about 1.03 metres. The demo prints 1.03 m for 6 milliseconds, 2.06 m for 12 milliseconds, and 0.51 m for 3 milliseconds.

Whale Song and Ocean Bioacoustics Rank 8 · compounded

Humpback whales produce long, structured songs that can last tens of minutes and be repeated for hours, built from units grouped into phrases and themes in a hierarchy that researchers compare, cautiously, to the structure of human song. The songs were brought to public attention by Roger Payne's 1970 album Songs of the Humpback Whale, and decades of recording show that the songs change gradually over a season and that populations share song versions that evolve over years. Sound travels far better in water than in air: low-frequency whale calls can cross whole ocean basins under the right conditions, which is why ocean bioacoustics studies both communication and the masking effect of shipping noise. Other whales contribute their own voices, from the patterned clicks of sperm whales to the varied whistles and calls of dolphins, making the ocean a deep, slow, long-distance biophony layer.

Science proof

Science proof: spectrogram analysis of humpback recordings shows repeatable phrase and theme structure that changes in a shared, directional way across a population over time, which is the evidence for cultural transmission of song. Hydrophone arrays have localised singing whales and tracked song exchange between populations. Playback and long-term monitoring studies link increased low-frequency shipping noise to changes in calling behaviour, including whales calling louder or shifting frequency, consistent with masking effects.

Math proof

Math proof: sound in seawater travels at roughly 1500 metres per second, so a call lasting 20 minutes covers a path length of 1500 x 1200 = 1,800,000 metres, or 1800 kilometres, if it travelled uninterrupted. The demo prints path length 1800.0 km for a 20-minute song, 900.0 km for 10 minutes, and the travel time for a 150-kilometre distance, 150000 / 1500 = 100.0 seconds.

Elephant Infrasound Rank 9 · compounded

Elephants communicate partly in infrasound, sound below about 20 hertz, the lower limit of typical human hearing. Their rumbles, produced in the larynx and felt as much as heard, carry through air and also couple into the ground as seismic waves that other elephants detect through their feet and trunk tips. Infrasound's long wavelength lets it travel kilometres across savanna with relatively little loss, so separated family groups can stay in contact, coordinate movement, and warn of danger over distances where higher sounds would fade. Researchers record these rumbles with specialised low-frequency microphones and seismometers, and playback experiments have shown elephants responding to rumbles played back from kilometres away. The elephant voice is therefore a reminder that a soundscape contains far more than humans can hear unaided.

Science proof

Science proof: spectrograms of elephant rumbles show strong energy below 20 hertz, and seismic recordings confirm ground-coupled components travelling through soil. Playback experiments in which recorded rumbles were played to wild elephants produced approach, bunching, and vocal replies consistent with the rumbles' social meanings, demonstrating that the signals are received and acted on. Anatomical studies link rumble production to the larynx and to the large nasal passages that shape the sound.

Math proof

Math proof: at 15 hertz in air, wavelength = 343 / 15 = 22.87 metres, about 22.9 metres, far longer than a human is tall, which is why infrasound bends around trees and terrain. The demo prints 22.87 m at 15 Hz, 34.30 m at 10 Hz, and 17.15 m at 20 Hz, each computed as 343 / frequency.

The Acoustic Niche Hypothesis Rank 10 · compounded

The acoustic niche hypothesis, associated with Bernie Krause's soundscape ecology, proposes that in an undisturbed habitat, vocal species divide the available sound space by frequency and by time, so that each species' voice occupies its own niche and masking is minimised. Dawn may belong to birds in the middle bands, night to insects in the high bands and frogs in the lower bands, and a stream's geophony may sit in a steady low band beneath them all. The hypothesis predicts that intact habitats show sound spread across the spectrum, while disturbed habitats show gaps where species have disappeared and crowding where remaining species overlap. It is best read as a useful model rather than a strict law: later studies have found counterexamples, seasonal exceptions, and habitats where species overlap freely, but the niche framing remains the standard way to describe how a healthy biophony organises itself. This record covers all three layers, because a niche only makes sense against the geophony and anthrophony that share the spectrum.

Science proof

Science proof: multi-species recordings from intact tropical forests show different species concentrated in different frequency bands at different times of day, matching niche predictions more closely than chance. Comparisons between intact and logged or fragmented sites show reduced spectral spread and increased overlap in disturbed sites. Critiques note that some species overlap substantially without apparent cost, so modern soundscape ecology treats the hypothesis as a hypothesis with supporting and contradicting evidence, reported as such.

Math proof

Math proof: suppose five species occupy bands centred at 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, and 8000 hertz. The gaps between adjacent centres are 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 hertz, and the total span is 8000 - 500 = 7500 hertz. The demo prints the four gaps and the total span 7500 Hz, plus the mean gap, (500 + 1000 + 2000 + 4000) / 4 = 1875.0 Hz.

Soundscape Indices: NDSI and ACI Rank 11 · compounded

Counting every voice in a recording by ear is slow, so soundscape ecology uses indices that summarise a recording's structure numerically. The Normalised Difference Soundscape Index, NDSI, compares biophony power in a higher band, typically 2 to 11 kilohertz, with anthrophony power in a lower band, typically 1 to 2 kilohertz, giving a value from -1, dominated by human noise, to +1, dominated by biological sound. The Acoustic Complexity Index, ACI, measures how much sound intensity varies from moment to moment within each frequency band, on the reasoning that animal calls vary rapidly while steady human noise does not. Neither index identifies species, and both can be fooled by wind, rain, or a loud insect, but together they let researchers compare hundreds of recordings, track sites over years, and flag changes for closer listening. This record covers the biophony and anthrophony layers, because NDSI is defined by their contrast.

Science proof

Science proof: NDSI was introduced and validated against recordings from sites with known levels of human disturbance, where it tracked the biological-to-human sound ratio as predicted. ACI was validated against bird-rich and bird-poor recordings, where it rose with avian vocal activity. Both indices are published with their formulas and limitations, and modern studies report them alongside spectrograms and species identifications rather than as replacements for listening.

Math proof

Math proof: NDSI = (biophony - anthrophony) / (biophony + anthrophony). For biophony power 0.8 and anthrophony power 0.2, NDSI = (0.8 - 0.2) / (0.8 + 0.2) = 0.60. The demo prints 0.60 for those powers, -0.60 for the reversed powers, and 0.00 for equal powers 0.5 and 0.5.

Bioacoustics Field Recording Methods Rank 12 · complex

Field bioacoustics begins with a recorder, a microphone, and a plan. A recordist chooses a site, notes the time, weather, habitat, and equipment, and records long, unbroken takes rather than short clips, because context matters: a call means more when the listener can hear what came before and after it. Microphones are chosen for the job, with parabolic reflectors to focus distant bird song, hydrophones for underwater sound, contact microphones for substrate vibrations, and ultrasonic microphones for bats. Calibration matters: without a reference tone or a known microphone sensitivity, two recordings cannot be compared for loudness. Recordings are archived with metadata, and spectrograms, plots of frequency against time, are the standard way to view and analyse them. The ethics of the craft are simple: record without disturbing the animals, keep out of the recording yourself, and label every file honestly. This record covers all three layers, because a field recording captures them together and the recordist's job is to separate them in analysis, not in the field.

Science proof

Science proof: the spectrogram is the standard bioacoustic analysis tool because it displays the frequency, duration, and modulation of calls in a form that can be measured and compared. Standard practice requires reporting sampling rate, because a recorder sampling at 44100 hertz can only capture frequencies up to half that rate, 22050 hertz, by the Nyquist limit. Archival standards in bioacoustics require metadata on location, time, equipment, and calibration so that recordings remain comparable across years and researchers.

Math proof

Math proof: the Nyquist limit is half the sampling rate. For a sampling rate of 44100 hertz, the highest capturable frequency is 44100 / 2 = 22050 hertz. The demo prints 22050 Hz for 44100 Hz sampling, 24000 Hz for 48000 Hz sampling, and 96000 Hz for 192000 Hz ultrasonic sampling, each computed as rate / 2.

Noise Pollution and the Anthrophony Layer Rank 13 · complex

Anthrophony, the human-made layer of the soundscape, has grown louder and more widespread in the last century, and its effect on wildlife is now a major bioacoustics research topic. Traffic, aircraft, industry, and shipping raise background levels, especially at low frequencies, and can mask animal calls, forcing animals to call louder, higher, or at different times, or to abandon noisy areas altogether. Urban birds in several studied species sing at higher minimum frequencies than their rural counterparts, a shift consistent with avoiding low-frequency traffic noise, though the shift may also reflect other urban pressures. In the ocean, shipping noise overlaps the low-frequency calls of large whales, and documented responses include louder calling and altered call rates. The research does not claim that all human sound is harmful: human voices, music, and traditional soundscapes are part of anthrophony too, but the masking physics is the same for every source, and a soundscape dominated by steady broadband noise leaves less room for biophony.

Science proof

Science proof: masking is a physical effect: when background noise in a frequency band approaches or exceeds a signal's level in that band, the signal becomes undetectable to a listener, animal or human. Field studies comparing noisy and quiet sites show reduced species richness and altered calling behaviour at noisy sites, and controlled playback experiments confirm that added noise alone can change calling and foraging behaviour. The decibel scale is logarithmic, so a 10-decibel increase represents a tenfold increase in sound intensity, not a small step.

Math proof

Math proof: adding two equal sound sources raises the level by 10 x log10(2) = 3.01 decibels, about 3 dB. The demo prints the combined level of two 60 dB sources as 63.01 dB, of a 60 dB and a 70 dB source as 70.41 dB, and of ten equal 60 dB sources as 70.00 dB, using 10 x log10 of the summed intensities.

Soundscape layer(s)

Coral Reef Soundscapes Rank 14 · complex

A healthy coral reef is loud. Snapping shrimp produce sharp broadband snaps as their claws close, creating cavitation bubbles whose collapse makes the crackle that dominates many reef recordings; grazing fish scrape algae, urchins rasp, and reef fish grunt, pop, and croak, especially at dawn and dusk. Researchers have found that reef sound levels and complexity track reef health: degraded, overfished reefs are measurably quieter and less complex than protected, fish-rich reefs. Sound also guides young reef life: larvae of some fish and coral species orient toward reef sound when choosing where to settle, so a quiet reef may recruit fewer newcomers. Reef recordings therefore serve as a rapid health check for ecosystems that are hard to survey by eye, and playback of healthy reef sound is being tested as a way to encourage settlement on restoration sites. This record covers biophony, the reef animals, and geophony, the water and waves that carry their sounds.

Science proof

Science proof: hydrophone surveys comparing protected and degraded reefs report higher sound levels and higher acoustic complexity at healthy sites, with snapping shrimp snaps as the dominant broadband source. Settlement experiments show larvae of several reef species responding to reef sound playbacks in laboratory and field trials, supporting sound's role in habitat selection. Acoustic monitoring is now used alongside visual surveys in reef assessment programmes.

Math proof

Math proof: if a healthy reef records 3000 snaps per minute and a degraded reef records 900 snaps per minute, the degraded reef's snap rate is 900 / 3000 = 30.0 percent of the healthy rate, a reduction of 70.0 percent. The demo prints the healthy rate 3000, the degraded rate 900, the ratio 30.0 percent, and the reduction 70.0 percent.

Biofonics Conservation: Listening as Monitoring Rank 15 · complex

Acoustic monitoring has become a core conservation tool because it listens continuously, cheaply, and without disturbing wildlife. Autonomous recorders left in a forest, wetland, or reef for weeks or months capture dawn choruses, night choruses, breeding seasons, and intrusions such as chainsaws or gunshots, producing a record no visiting survey team could match. The recordings are analysed with indices, spectrogram review, and increasingly with machine-learning classifiers trained to recognise species calls, though every classifier output is checked against known call libraries before it is trusted. Long-term acoustic datasets reveal trends: species arriving earlier with warming springs, choruses thinning as populations decline, and soundscapes recovering after protection. The method's limits are honest ones: a recorder hears only what is audible, misses silent species, and can be fooled by wind and rain, so acoustic monitoring complements, and never replaces, field observation. This record covers all three layers, because a monitoring recorder hears them together and conservation decisions depend on separating them.

Science proof

Science proof: autonomous recorder networks have documented species presence, breeding phenology, and illegal logging sounds across tropical forests and wetlands, with results published alongside traditional survey data for comparison. Studies that pair acoustic indices with on-the-ground species counts find that acoustic measures track richness and activity, with known error rates that are reported rather than hidden. The method is now standard in several national biodiversity monitoring programmes.

Math proof

Math proof: if one recorder captures 24 hours per day for 30 days, it collects 24 x 30 = 720 hours of audio. At a storage rate of 100 megabytes per hour, that is 720 x 100 = 72000 megabytes, or 72.0 gigabytes. The demo prints 720 hours and 72.0 gigabytes, plus the total for 10 recorders, 7200 hours and 720.0 gigabytes.
