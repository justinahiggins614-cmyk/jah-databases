Running Stitch Rank 1 · hand sewing · beginner

The running stitch is the simplest hand stitch: the needle weaves in and out of the fabric in one even dashed line. It gathers, bastes, quilts, and sews light seams, and it is the first stitch every hand sewer learns.

Materials

  • Plain-weave fabric (cotton or linen)
  • Hand-sewing needle (sharps, size 7-9)
  • Thread to match or contrast the fabric
  • Scissors
  • Marking chalk or washable pen
  • Embroidery hoop (optional, for even tension)

Gauge and tension

Aim for 4 to 6 stitches per inch for a visible basting or gathering line; 6 to 8 per inch for a finer seam. Keep every stitch the same length and every gap the same width so the dashed line reads evenly.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Mark a straight line on the fabric with chalk or a washable pen, and thread the needle with no more than an arm's length of thread, knotted at the end.
  2. Bring the needle up from the back of the fabric at the start of the line, pulling the knot snug against the back.
  3. Push the needle down about one stitch-length ahead, then bring it back up the same distance ahead, so the needle weaves in and out in one motion.
  4. Repeat along the line, loading several stitches onto the needle before pulling the thread through when the fabric allows it.
  5. Keep the stitch length and the gap between stitches equal, and pull the thread just firmly enough that the fabric lies flat without puckering.
  6. To finish, take one tiny backstitch on the back of the work, or weave the thread through the last few stitches, and trim the excess.

Stitch-repeat chart

One row, worked left to right: up, down, up, down, in an even rhythm. Written as a repeat: [up 1 stitch-length, down 1 stitch-length] across the marked line. The chart reads as a dashed line: - - - - - - - -.

Finished uses

Basting seams before machine sewing, gathering fabric into ruffles, hand quilting through layers, simple mending, and decorative dashed outlines on embroidery.

Variations

  • Basting stitch: longer stitches (about 1/4 inch) with wider gaps, meant to be removed later.
  • Gathering stitch: two parallel rows of running stitch, pulled together to draw the fabric up evenly.
  • Running stitch quilting: small, even stitches worked through a quilt top, batting, and backing.

Working demo program

import sys

TITLE = 'Running Stitch'
REPEAT = '- - '
WIDTH = int(sys.argv[1]) if len(sys.argv) > 1 else 24


def main():
    unit = len(REPEAT)
    reps = WIDTH // unit
    rem = WIDTH % unit
    row = (REPEAT * reps + REPEAT[:rem]).rstrip()
    print(TITLE + " - stitch-repeat planner")
    print("Width: %d stitches" % WIDTH)
    print("Repeat unit: %r (%d stitches)" % (REPEAT, unit))
    print("Repeats across: %d, remainder: %d" % (reps, rem))
    for r in range(1, 5):
        print("Row %d: %s" % (r, row))
    print("Total stitches in 4-row swatch: %d" % (WIDTH * 4))

Backstitch Rank 2 · hand sewing · beginner

The backstitch is the strongest hand-sewing stitch. Each new stitch steps back into the end of the previous one, so the front of the work shows an unbroken line while the back shows overlapping stitches.

Materials

  • Woven fabric
  • Hand-sewing needle (sharps)
  • Thread matched to the fabric
  • Scissors
  • Marking pen or chalk

Gauge and tension

Stitches about 1/8 inch long for a firm seam; the finished line looks continuous from the front, like machine stitching, because each new stitch overlaps the last.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Mark the seam line and knot the thread, bringing the needle up at the start of the line.
  2. Take one small stitch forward (about 1/8 inch) and bring the needle up again one stitch-length ahead of where the thread emerged.
  3. Insert the needle back into the hole where the previous stitch ended, so the new stitch fills the gap exactly.
  4. Bring the needle up one stitch-length ahead of the current position and repeat, always stepping back into the last hole.
  5. Keep the tension even so the line stays straight and the fabric does not pucker.
  6. Finish with two tiny stitches on top of each other on the back of the work and trim the thread.

Stitch-repeat chart

Worked left to right, each stitch steps backward before moving forward: stitch 1 covers positions 1-2, stitch 2 covers positions 2-3, stitch 3 covers positions 3-4, so the front line is continuous: ========.

Finished uses

Hand-sewn seams that must hold under strain, attaching zippers by hand, repairing split seams, outlining embroidery shapes with a crisp continuous line, and sewing buttons and fastenings.

Variations

  • Prick stitch: a backstitch with very short stitches and longer gaps, used for fine tailoring.
  • Stem stitch: a close cousin worked diagonally, used for embroidered stems and outlines.
  • Whipped backstitch: a second thread whipped over the backstitch line for a corded outline.

Chain Stitch Rank 3 · embroidery · beginner

The chain stitch forms a linked chain of loops on the fabric surface, like a crochet chain laid flat. It makes bold outlines, fills shapes with texture, and is the basis of tambour work and many traditional embroideries.

Materials

  • Firm fabric (linen or cotton twill)
  • Chenille or embroidery needle
  • Stranded embroidery floss or perle cotton
  • Embroidery hoop
  • Scissors
  • Marking pen

Gauge and tension

Chains about 1/4 inch long for a bold line, or 1/8 inch for fine detail. Keep each loop the same size so the chain reads as a smooth braid.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Hoop the fabric taut and mark the line to follow, threading the needle with floss and knotting the end.
  2. Bring the needle up at the start of the line and hold the thread down on the fabric with the thumb of the free hand, forming a loop.
  3. Insert the needle back into the same hole it came out of, and bring it up a short distance ahead, inside the loop of thread.
  4. Pull the thread gently through so a neat oval loop lies on the fabric, then hold the new thread down to form the next loop.
  5. Repeat, always re-entering at the exit hole of the previous loop, so every loop is chained to the one before it.
  6. To end a line, take a tiny anchoring stitch over the last loop to hold it flat, then fasten off on the back.

Stitch-repeat chart

A vertical chain of linked ovals: each row is one loop, anchored by the next loop's entry stitch. Written as a repeat: [loop, anchor] down the line, reading as: o-o-o-o-o-o.

Finished uses

Bold outlines, borders, and lettering in embroidery; filling leaves and petals with a textured braid; traditional chain-stitch rugs and garments; and the foundation of tambour and crochet-style surface work.

Variations

  • Detached chain (lazy daisy): single loops anchored individually to form petals.
  • Heavy chain: worked with two threads for a thicker braid.
  • Open chain: loops worked wider apart so the fabric shows between them.

French Knot Rank 4 · embroidery · intermediate

The French knot is a tiny raised dot of thread wound around the needle and anchored to the fabric. Clustered together, French knots become flower centers, textured fields, eyes, and stippled shading.

Materials

  • Fabric held taut in a hoop
  • Embroidery needle (milliner's or sharp)
  • Stranded embroidery floss
  • Scissors

Gauge and tension

One wrap gives a small knot about 1/16 inch across; two wraps give a medium knot; three wraps give a bold knot. Use the same number of wraps for every knot in a group so they match.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Bring the needle up through the fabric at the exact spot for the knot, holding the thread taut with the free hand.
  2. Wrap the thread around the needle once, twice, or three times, keeping the wraps snug and close together near the fabric.
  3. Holding the wraps firmly with the thumb, insert the needle back into the fabric as close as possible to the exit hole, but not through the same hole.
  4. Pull the thread slowly through, keeping tension on the wraps until the knot settles onto the fabric surface.
  5. Release the wraps only when the knot is seated, and check that it sits upright rather than pulling flat.
  6. For a cluster, work knots close together with the same wrap count so the group reads as one texture.

Stitch-repeat chart

A single point on the chart, marked with a dot; the number of wraps is noted beside it. A field of knots reads as a scatter of dots: (o) (o) (o) (o).

Finished uses

Flower centers and stamens, eyes on embroidered figures, textured fur and foliage, dotted shading, and decorative scatter patterns on samplers.

Variations

  • Colonial knot: a figure-eight wrap that sits slightly taller and rounder.
  • Bullion knot: many wraps forming a long raised bar rather than a dot.
  • Pistil stitch: a French knot on a straight stem stitch, like a pinhead flower.

Satin Stitch Rank 5 · embroidery · intermediate

Satin stitch fills a shape with flat, parallel stitches laid edge to edge, producing a smooth, lustrous surface. It is the classic fill for petals, leaves, letters, and monograms.

Materials

  • Firm fabric in a hoop
  • Embroidery needle
  • Stranded embroidery floss
  • Scissors
  • Marking pen for the shape outline

Gauge and tension

Stitches lie side by side with no gaps and no overlap, so the filled shape looks like a smooth sheet of silk. Work the outline first if the shape has sharp edges.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Mark the shape outline on hooped fabric and thread the needle with floss, using all strands for a bold fill or fewer for a fine one.
  2. Bring the needle up just outside one edge of the shape and take it down just outside the opposite edge, following the angle chosen for the fill.
  3. Bring the needle up immediately beside the previous stitch, with no gap and no overlap, and take it down beside the previous stitch on the far side.
  4. Continue across the shape, keeping every stitch parallel and the same tension, so the surface stays smooth and the edges stay crisp.
  5. For wide shapes, split the fill into sections worked at slightly different angles, or pad the shape first with running stitch or a second layer of satin stitch.
  6. Fasten off on the back by weaving the thread under the last few stitches, and trim.

Stitch-repeat chart

Parallel bars filling the outlined shape, all at the same angle: ||||||||||. The chart shades the shape solid and notes the stitch direction with an arrow.

Finished uses

Filling petals, leaves, and monogram letters; smooth lettering on samplers; applique-style motifs; and any shape that needs a flat, glossy, unbroken surface.

Variations

  • Padded satin stitch: a layer of running stitch or chain stitch under the satin layer for a raised, cushioned fill.
  • Slanted satin stitch: stitches worked on the diagonal for a sense of movement.
  • Long-and-short stitch: staggered stitch lengths that blend colors for shaded fills.

Single Crochet Rank 6 · crochet · beginner

Single crochet is the shortest, densest basic crochet stitch: one yarn-over and one pull-through per stitch. It makes firm, tight fabric for dishcloths, bags, amigurumi, and sturdy garments.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Crochet hook matched to the yarn (usually 5.0-5.5 mm for worsted yarn)
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle for weaving in ends
  • Stitch markers

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on a 5.5 mm hook, about 4 single crochet stitches per inch across and 4 rows per inch. Check gauge on a 4-inch swatch and change hook size if the fabric is too stiff or too loose.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Make a slip knot, place it on the hook, and chain the number of stitches called for, plus one turning chain.
  2. Insert the hook into the second chain from the hook, yarn over, and pull a loop through, leaving two loops on the hook.
  3. Yarn over again and pull through both loops on the hook; one single crochet is complete.
  4. Work one single crochet into each chain across the row, then chain one and turn the work.
  5. Work one single crochet into each stitch across, always inserting the hook under both top loops unless the pattern says otherwise.
  6. At the end of the project, cut the yarn leaving a tail, pull it through the last loop, and weave the tail in with the yarn needle.

Stitch-repeat chart

A grid of small x marks, one per stitch, with one row of chain at the base. Written as: Row 1: sc in 2nd ch from hook, sc across; Row 2: ch 1, turn, sc in each sc across; repeat Row 2.

Finished uses

Dishcloths and washcloths, amigurumi and stuffed toys, sturdy bags and baskets, dense hat brims, and any fabric that must hold its shape.

Variations

  • Single crochet in the back loop only: creates a ribbed, stretchy texture.
  • Waistcoat stitch: worked through the center of the stitch for a knit-look fabric.
  • Extended single crochet: adds an extra chain step for a slightly taller, softer stitch.

Double Crochet Rank 7 · crochet · beginner

Double crochet is the workhorse of crochet: taller than single crochet, faster to build, and open enough to drape. It forms the basis of granny squares, blankets, garments, and lace.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Crochet hook matched to the yarn (usually 5.0-5.5 mm for worsted yarn)
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle
  • Stitch markers

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on a 5.5 mm hook, about 3 double crochet stitches per inch across and 2 rows per inch. The fabric is taller, softer, and drapes more than single crochet.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Chain the number of stitches called for, plus three turning chains, which count as the first double crochet of the row.
  2. Yarn over once, insert the hook into the fourth chain from the hook, yarn over, and pull a loop through, leaving three loops on the hook.
  3. Yarn over and pull through the first two loops, then yarn over and pull through the remaining two loops; one double crochet is complete.
  4. Work one double crochet into each chain across, then chain three and turn the work.
  5. Work one double crochet into each stitch across, taking care not to work into the turning chain unless the pattern says to.
  6. Finish by cutting the yarn, pulling the tail through the last loop, and weaving in the ends.

Stitch-repeat chart

A grid of tall T marks, one per stitch, with a 3-chain turning edge. Written as: Row 1: dc in 4th ch from hook, dc across; Row 2: ch 3, turn, dc in each dc across; repeat Row 2.

Finished uses

Blankets and afghans, summer garments and shawls, granny squares and motifs, market bags, and any project where speed and drape matter more than density.

Variations

  • Front-post double crochet: worked around the post of the stitch below for raised ribs and cables.
  • Double crochet two together: decreases the stitch count by working two stitches into one.
  • V-stitch: two double crochets worked into the same stitch, forming an open V.

Treble Crochet Rank 8 · crochet · intermediate

Treble crochet is twice as tall as double crochet: two yarn-overs before entering the stitch, and three pull-throughs to finish. It builds height quickly and makes open, lacy fabric.

Materials

  • Medium-weight or light-weight yarn
  • Crochet hook matched to the yarn
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle
  • Stitch markers

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on a 5.5 mm hook, about 2.5 treble crochet stitches per inch across and 1.5 rows per inch. The fabric is tall, airy, and very fast to build.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Chain the number of stitches called for, plus four turning chains, which count as the first treble crochet of the row.
  2. Yarn over twice, insert the hook into the fifth chain from the hook, yarn over, and pull a loop through, leaving four loops on the hook.
  3. Yarn over and pull through the first two loops, yarn over and pull through the next two, then yarn over and pull through the last two; one treble crochet is complete.
  4. Work one treble crochet into each chain across, then chain four and turn the work.
  5. Work one treble crochet into each stitch across, keeping the yarn-overs even so every stitch is the same height.
  6. Finish by cutting the yarn, pulling the tail through the last loop, and weaving in the ends.

Stitch-repeat chart

A grid of tall double-barred marks, one per stitch, with a 4-chain turning edge. Written as: Row 1: tr in 5th ch from hook, tr across; Row 2: ch 4, turn, tr in each tr across; repeat Row 2.

Finished uses

Lacy shawls and wraps, openwork blankets, tall motifs and edgings, quick summer tops, and any project where height and openness are wanted.

Variations

  • Double treble: three yarn-overs for an even taller stitch.
  • Treble two together: decreases two trebles into one for shaping.
  • Crossed treble: a treble worked into a stitch skipped earlier, crossing over the stitches between.

Garter Stitch Rank 9 · knitting · beginner

Garter stitch is knitting every row: knit stitch on every row, back and forth. It makes a squishy, reversible, ridged fabric that lies flat, and it is the first pattern every knitter learns.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Knitting needles matched to the yarn (usually 5.0-5.5 mm for worsted yarn)
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle
  • Row counter (optional)

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on 5.5 mm needles, about 4 stitches per inch across and 8 rows per inch, because garter rows are squat. The fabric is reversible and does not curl.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Cast on the number of stitches called for, using a long-tail or knitted cast-on.
  2. Hold the needle with the cast-on stitches in the left hand, insert the right needle into the first stitch from front to back, wrap the yarn around it, and pull a new loop through.
  3. Repeat across the row, knitting every stitch, then turn the work.
  4. Knit every stitch of every row, turning at the end of each row, until the piece measures the length called for.
  5. Count ridges on the fabric to check length: each visible ridge is two rows.
  6. Bind off loosely in knit stitch, cut the yarn, pull the tail through the last loop, and weave in the ends.

Stitch-repeat chart

Every row is a knit row, so the chart is a solid grid of knit marks: vvvvvv on every row. Written as: knit every stitch of every row.

Finished uses

Scarves, dishcloths, baby blankets, sweater borders and button bands, and any edge that must lie flat without curling.

Variations

  • Garter stitch in the round: alternate one knit round with one purl round to keep the garter ridges.
  • Seeded garter: occasional purl stitches break the ridges for texture.
  • Garter stripes: change color every two rows for clean, even stripes.

Stockinette Stitch Rank 10 · knitting · beginner

Stockinette stitch is the smooth face of knitting: knit one row, purl one row, repeated. The front shows neat columns of V shapes, and it is the default fabric for sweaters, socks, and hats.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Knitting needles matched to the yarn
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on 5.5 mm needles, about 4.5 stitches per inch across and 6 rows per inch. Stockinette shows the classic smooth V pattern on the front and bumps on the back.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Cast on the number of stitches called for.
  2. Row 1 (right side): knit every stitch across, then turn the work.
  3. Row 2 (wrong side): purl every stitch across, then turn the work.
  4. Repeat Rows 1 and 2, keeping the knit rows on the front face and the purl rows on the back face.
  5. Check that the front shows smooth V columns and the back shows horizontal bumps; if the faces swap, one row was worked wrong.
  6. Because stockinette curls at the edges, work a garter or ribbed border if the piece must lie flat, then bind off loosely and weave in the ends.

Stitch-repeat chart

Alternating rows: knit rows marked v, purl rows marked with a dash. Written as: Row 1 (RS): knit across; Row 2 (WS): purl across; repeat Rows 1-2.

Finished uses

Sweater bodies and sleeves, socks, hats, smooth blanket centers, and any garment where a clean, smooth fabric is wanted.

Variations

  • Reverse stockinette: the purl side worn as the front, for a nubby texture.
  • Stockinette in the round: knit every round, since the front always faces out.
  • Broken stockinette: occasional purl rows interrupt the smooth face for subtle texture.

Rib Stitch Rank 11 · knitting · beginner

Rib stitch alternates columns of knit and purl stitches, making a stretchy, springy fabric that hugs cuffs, hems, collars, and hat brims, and pulls the edge neatly inward.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Knitting needles one size smaller than the body of the garment
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle

Gauge and tension

Ribbing pulls inward, so measure it relaxed and slightly stretched; a 2x2 rib on 4.5 mm needles with worsted yarn gives about 5 stitches per inch unstretched. The fabric stretches wide and springs back.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Cast on a multiple of four stitches plus two edge stitches for a 2x2 rib, using a stretchy cast-on.
  2. Row 1: knit two, purl two across the row, ending with knit two if the count allows, then turn.
  3. Row 2: work the stitches as they appear, knitting the knit stitches and purling the purl stitches, so the columns stay aligned.
  4. Repeat Row 2 for every following row, always knitting the knits and purling the purls, so each column continues unbroken.
  5. Check the columns from the front: knit columns stand proud as ribs, purl columns sink as valleys.
  6. Bind off in pattern, working knit over knit and purl over purl, and bind off loosely so the edge still stretches.

Stitch-repeat chart

Vertical columns: K K P P K K P P across every row, with knit columns marked v and purl columns marked with a dash. Written as: Row 1: *k2, p2; repeat from * across; repeat Row 1, working stitches as they appear.

Finished uses

Cuffs, hems, collars, hat brims, sock tops, and any edge that must stretch and spring back into shape.

Variations

  • 1x1 rib: knit one, purl one, for a finer, tighter edge.
  • 3x3 rib: knit three, purl three, for a bolder, chunkier edge.
  • Fisherman's rib: a brioche-style rib with extra depth and softness.

Shell Stitch Rank 12 · crochet · intermediate

Shell stitch groups several double crochets into one stitch, then skips stitches between shells, so the fabric fans into scallops. It edges blankets, shawls, and garments with a classic shell border.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Crochet hook matched to the yarn
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle
  • Stitch markers

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on a 5.5 mm hook, one 5-double-crochet shell plus its spacing spans about 1.5 inches. The fabric fans out in scallops and drapes softly.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Chain a multiple of six stitches plus two, then work a base row of single crochet across, chain one, and turn.
  2. Row 1: single crochet in the first stitch, skip two stitches, work five double crochets into the next stitch, skip two stitches, single crochet in the next stitch; repeat this shell sequence across.
  3. Row 2: chain three (counts as a double crochet), work two double crochets into the first single crochet, single crochet into the center double crochet of the shell below, work five double crochets into the next single crochet; repeat across, ending with three double crochets in the last stitch.
  4. Repeat Row 2, stacking each new shell into the single crochet between the shells below, so the scallops line up in columns.
  5. Keep the skipped stitches consistent, since the skip count sets the shell spacing and the scallop width.
  6. Finish with a row of single crochet if a straight edge is wanted, then cut the yarn and weave in the ends.

Stitch-repeat chart

Fans of five tall marks grouped into one stitch, separated by single-crochet anchors: (@) (@) (@). Written as: *sc, skip 2, 5 dc in next st, skip 2; repeat from *.

Finished uses

Scalloped blanket and shawl edges, baby garments, table runners, pillow edgings, and any border that should fan into soft scallops.

Variations

  • Three-double shell: a smaller, tighter fan for fine yarn.
  • Seven-double shell: a wider, more dramatic fan for chunky yarn.
  • V-shell: two double crochets separated by a chain, for a lighter, lacier fan.

Cable Stitch Rank 13 · knitting · advanced

Cable stitch crosses groups of stitches over each other, twisting columns of knitting into ropes, braids, and plaits. It is the signature texture of Aran sweaters and cozy winter knitting.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn
  • Knitting needles matched to the yarn
  • Cable needle
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle

Gauge and tension

Cables pull the fabric inward, so a cable panel measures narrower than the same stitch count in stockinette; allow about 4 stitches per inch in the cable panel on 5.5 mm needles with worsted yarn, and swatch the full cable repeat.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Cast on and work a few rows of ribbing or garter stitch for a stable base, then set up the cable panel with purl stitches framing each cable column.
  2. Work the cable column in stockinette (knit on the right side, purl on the wrong side) for the number of rows between crossings, usually four or six.
  3. On a cable-crossing row, slip the first half of the cable stitches onto the cable needle and hold it in front of the work for a left-leaning cable, or behind for a right-leaning cable.
  4. Knit the second half of the cable stitches from the left needle, then knit the stitches from the cable needle, completing the cross.
  5. Work the wrong-side row as the stitches appear, purling the cable stitches and knitting the framing stitches.
  6. Repeat the crossing every set number of rows, keeping the crossings evenly spaced so the rope twists at a steady pitch, then bind off loosely.

Stitch-repeat chart

A grid where the cable column is drawn as two strands crossing at the crossing row: ><>< with the cross marked on the crossing row. Written as: C4F = slip 2 to cable needle, hold in front, k2, k2 from cable needle; C4B = hold in back.

Finished uses

Aran and fisherman sweaters, cozy hats and scarves, blanket panels, pillow covers, and any winter piece that should look richly textured.

Variations

  • Four-stitch cable: the classic two-over-two rope, leaning left or right.
  • Six-stitch braid: three strands plaited for a wide braid panel.
  • Honeycomb cable: small cables crossing alternately to form a honeycomb texture.

Lace Stitch Rank 14 · knitting · advanced

Lace stitch pairs yarn-overs, which make holes, with decreases, which keep the stitch count steady, so the fabric opens into leaves, fans, and eyelets. It makes shawls, scarves, and heirloom wraps.

Materials

  • Light-weight or fingering yarn
  • Knitting needles matched to the yarn (often one size larger than usual for openness)
  • Stitch markers
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle
  • Blocking pins and mat

Gauge and tension

Lace gauge is measured after blocking, since blocking opens the pattern; a typical fingering lace on 4.0 mm needles gives about 5.5 stitches per inch after blocking. Always block the swatch before measuring.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Cast on a multiple of the lace repeat plus edge stitches, and place stitch markers between repeats to catch mistakes early.
  2. Row 1 (right side): work the repeat, for example knit two together, yarn over, knit one, yarn over, slip-slip-knit, keeping the yarn-over count equal to the decrease count.
  3. Row 2 (wrong side): purl every stitch, including the yarn-overs, so each eyelet stays open.
  4. Repeat the two rows, checking after each right-side row that the stitch count between markers has not changed.
  5. If a mistake appears, drop back to the last correct row rather than pressing on, since lace mistakes compound quickly.
  6. Bind off loosely, then soak and pin the piece out to its measurements on a blocking mat and let it dry fully, which opens the lace pattern.

Stitch-repeat chart

A grid where yarn-overs are marked o, knit-two-together as a right-leaning slash, and slip-slip-knit as a left-leaning slash: o/\o. Written as: Row 1: *k2tog, yo, k1, yo, ssk; repeat from *; Row 2: purl across.

Finished uses

Shawls and wraps, lacy scarves, heirloom baby blankets, openwork table runners, and any piece that should feel light and airy.

Variations

  • Feather and fan: a four-row wave of increases and decreases that ripples like waves.
  • Leaf lace: paired increases and decreases that shape leaf motifs.
  • Eyelet rows: single rows of yarn-over holes spaced across plain stockinette.

Granny Square Rank 15 · crochet · intermediate

The granny square is a crochet motif worked in rounds from the center out: clusters of three double crochets separated by chain spaces, with chain corners that let the square grow. Squares join into blankets, bags, garments, and more.

Materials

  • Medium-weight yarn in several colors
  • Crochet hook matched to the yarn
  • Scissors
  • Yarn needle

Gauge and tension

For worsted yarn on a 5.0 mm hook, a four-round granny square measures about 4 inches across. Squares must match each other in size to join neatly, so keep the tension consistent from square to square.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Make a slip knot and chain four, then join into a ring with a slip stitch, or start with a magic ring.
  2. Round 1: chain three (counts as a double crochet), work two double crochets into the ring, chain two, work three double crochets into the ring, chain two; repeat twice more, then join with a slip stitch to the top of the starting chain. Four clusters and four corner spaces are complete.
  3. Round 2: slip stitch to the first corner space, chain three, work two double crochets, chain two, and three double crochets into the same corner; work three double crochets into each side space and a corner cluster into each corner space, joining at the end of the round.
  4. Continue in rounds, working a three-double-crochet cluster into every side space and a corner cluster (three double crochets, chain two, three double crochets) into every corner space.
  5. Change color at the start of a round by joining the new yarn with a slip stitch in a corner space, for classic multicolor squares.
  6. Fasten off by cutting the yarn, pulling it through the last loop, and weaving in every tail on the back of the square.

Stitch-repeat chart

A square chart read from the center outward: clusters of three tall marks in each space, corners marked with chain-two spaces. Written as: Rnd 1: (3 dc, ch 2) x 4 in ring; Rnd 2+: 3 dc in each space, (3 dc, ch 2, 3 dc) in each corner.

Finished uses

Blankets and afghans, bags and totes, cardigans and vests, pillow covers, and any project built by joining many small motifs.

Variations

  • Solid granny square: no side spaces, worked as continuous double crochet rounds for a dense square.
  • Sunburst granny: puff-stitch rounds in the center for a flower-like square.
  • Hexagon granny: six corners instead of four, joined into honeycomb layouts.

Cross-Stitch Alphabet Sampler Rank 16 · cross-stitch · advanced

The cross-stitch alphabet sampler is the traditional showpiece of cross-stitch: the full alphabet, numbers, borders, and small motifs, each cross worked over one fabric square, so letters form in clean, countable pixels of thread.

Materials

  • Evenweave linen or Aida cloth (14-count Aida is standard for beginners)
  • Stranded embroidery floss
  • Tapestry needle (blunt tip)
  • Embroidery hoop or scroll frame
  • Scissors
  • Water-soluble marking pen for the grid center

Gauge and tension

On 14-count Aida, 14 stitches per inch; each cross fills one square of the fabric grid. A full alphabet sampler on 14-count cloth measures about 10 by 12 inches depending on the chart.

Row-by-row instructions

  1. Find the center of the cloth and the center of the chart, and mark the cloth center lightly, so the design stays centered.
  2. Thread the needle with two strands of floss for 14-count cloth, knotting or tucking the tail under the first stitches on the back.
  3. Work each cross as two diagonal stitches: first the bottom-left to top-right diagonal across the square, then the top-left to bottom-right diagonal, so every cross slants the same way.
  4. Work row by row within one letter or motif before moving on, carrying the thread behind only short distances, and keeping the tension even so the crosses lie flat.
  5. Keep all top stitches slanting the same direction across the whole sampler, which is what gives finished cross-stitch its smooth, uniform look.
  6. Add backstitch outlines last for letter edges and fine details, then wash gently, press face-down on a towel, and frame or finish the sampler.

Stitch-repeat chart

A counted grid chart: each square is one cross, symbols in the squares map to floss colors, and letters are drawn block by block on the grid. Written as: work each chart symbol as one full cross over one fabric square; backstitch per the chart's heavy lines.

Finished uses

Framed samplers, birth and wedding announcements, personalized gifts, holiday ornaments, and teaching pieces that record a stitcher's full alphabet of stitches.

Variations

  • Mini sampler: a single initial or monogram instead of the full alphabet.
  • Band sampler: horizontal bands of different alphabets and motifs rather than one block.
  • Three-dimensional sampler: the finished sampler made into a pillow, box lid, or wall hanging.
