A design for a photo collage maker that puts two photos in one frame, with each photo given its own space and neither crowded out. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

Two photos in one frame usually fight: one is squeezed into a corner, the other is cropped in half, and the frame shows neither photo well. The maker places them by feel, and the feel is often wrong, because two photos of different shapes rarely fit one frame without care. A creator that asks for the frame first, gives each photo its own space, and checks that neither photo is squeezed or cropped past its subject, turns two photos into one picture: the frame shows both photos well, the balance is clear, and the maker sees the proof before export. The frame is the collage's simplest shape, and the creator makes it work.

Worked example: two beach photos share one frame, each with its own space, neither cropped past its subject, and the frame reads as one picture.

History

Two photos in one frame are as old as double picture frames on a mantelpiece, where two prints shared one frame and neither was crowded. The creator applies the same balance, on a screen.

Proposed solution

Ask for the frame first, before placing any photo; Give each photo its own space, and keep the spaces balanced; Check that neither photo is squeezed or cropped past its subject; Show the frame at full size before export; Let the maker swap the photos with one click.

Dependencies

A frame named first; two balanced spaces; a subject check per photo; a full-size preview.

A field guide index to the twenty-three records in this database: a map of the whole set, ordered simple to compounded to complex, so a reader can find any record by number and by name. This is a design index for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

This database holds twenty-three full records about the photo collage maker, ordered from simple to compounded to complex, and a reader arriving without a map cannot tell which record to read first, which records belong together, or where the harder designs begin. The field guide index solves that by listing every record by number and by name, in order, with a one-line account of what each record covers and which rung of the ladder it sits on. Without the index, the simple records and the complex records blur together, and a reader who needs the basics may open a complex design first, while a reader who wants the hardest designs may never find them. The index is the map that keeps the ladder readable, and it is the record that turns twenty-three separate designs into one set a reader can walk through from start to finish.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order; Give each record a one-line account of what it covers; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.

Dependencies

The twenty-three records themselves, listed in order; the ladder ranks simple, compounded, and complex; agreement that an index lists, and never replaces, the records it points to.

Feasibility

buildable-now — An index is a list with one-line accounts, written with tools that exist today; only care in listing stands in the way.
