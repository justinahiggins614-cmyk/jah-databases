Flatbed Tower Movement: The Horizontal Frame JAH-TWC-000001

Specifications. A flatbed tower movement is built on a horizontal cast-iron or steel frame, typically 60 to 120 centimetres wide, carrying the going train, striking train and motion work on vertical arbors. The frame sits on a masonry or steel bed in the tower chamber, and the weight drums hang in a shaft below it. Going barrels on large examples are 20 to 35 centimetres in diameter, and the pendulum is usually a seconds pendulum about 994 millimetres long, though larger towers use 1.5 to 2 metre pendulums.

Mechanism. Gravity from the driving weights turns the great wheel, and the gear train steps that slow rotation up to the escape wheel. A gravity escapement or a deadbeat escapement releases the train in small, regular steps, and the pendulum regulates those steps. The motion work behind the dial converts the centre-wheel rotation into the twelve-hour rotation of the hour hand and the hourly rotation of the minute hand.

Maintenance. Oil the train pivots sparingly once a year with a clock oil suited to large pivots, and check the frame bolts and bed for movement after storms. Keep the chamber dry and ventilated, because damp rusts arbors and swells wooden beat boards. Inspect the weight lines or cables every visit for fraying, and never let a weight rest on the chamber floor.

Build and restoration guidance. Restoring a flatbed movement starts with photographing and labelling every wheel and arbor before dismantling. Clean pivots and bush worn pivot holes with new bronze bushes rather than forcing oversized oil. Reassemble on the frame, check each wheel for free running, and only then rehang the pendulum and set the beat.

Tools. Frame spanners, pivot polisher, bushing tool, oil cup and fine oiler, spirit level, weight-line gauge.

The Gravity Escapement: Denison's Double Three-Legged Design JAH-TWC-000002

Specifications. The double three-legged gravity escapement, developed for large tower clocks in the nineteenth century, uses two gravity arms that fall a fixed distance onto the escape wheel at each step. It is designed for pendulums of 1 to 2 metres and for escape wheels of 15 to 25 centimetres diameter. Because the driving force on the pendulum comes from the falling arms rather than directly from the train, it tolerates large variations in drive force.

Mechanism. The escape wheel lifts each gravity arm in turn, and the arm then falls freely, giving the pendulum a small, constant impulse. The pendulum itself never carries the train load, so wind pressure on large external hands and friction changes in the train barely affect the rate. The escapement beats once per pendulum swing, and the distinctive double-step action gives tower clocks their steady, audible beat.

Maintenance. Check the gravity arms for free fall and equal drop, and keep their pallets clean and lightly oiled. A sticking arm shows up as an uneven beat or a gaining rate. Inspect the escape-wheel teeth for wear flats, and adjust the arm clearance only in tiny steps, re-checking the beat after each change.

Build and restoration guidance. When rebuilding a gravity escapement, make the arms as a matched pair from the same steel stock and harden the pallet faces evenly. Set the drop so both arms fall the same distance, and test with the hands loaded, because wind load is the condition the design exists to resist.

Tools. Escapement file set, micrometer, depthing tool, beat amplifier or listening tube, oil cup.

Weight-Driven Tower Movements and Their Shafts JAH-TWC-000003

Specifications. A weight-driven tower clock typically carries a going weight of 50 to 300 kilograms and a striking weight of similar size, hanging in a shaft that may run 10 to 40 metres down the tower. The weights fall slowly, often only a few centimetres per day on the going side, and the movement is wound weekly on most installations. The drums, pulleys and lines are sized so a single winding lifts the weights for seven to eight days.

Mechanism. The falling weight turns the barrel, and the gear train divides that motion down to the escapement. A maintaining-power device, such as a bolt-and-shutter or a spring arrangement, keeps the train driven while the clock is being wound, so winding never stops the pendulum. The striking weight is released only when the strike train is triggered by the going train at the hour.

Maintenance. Wind on the same day each week and record the weight positions, so a sudden change in fall rate warns of added friction. Grease pulleys lightly, inspect gut, wire or steel cable lines for broken strands, and keep the shaft clear of debris and nesting material. Never wind past the top stop, and never let a weight hang on a damaged line.

Build and restoration guidance. When re-roping a tower clock, use the original line material and diameter where the drums and pulleys are grooved for it. Fit a maintaining-power check after any barrel work, and prove the clock runs through a full winding cycle before leaving it.

Tools. Winding crank or key, line gauge, pulley grease, shaft lamp, weight-position logbook.

External Tower Dials: Size, Legibility and Illumination JAH-TWC-000004

Specifications. External tower dials commonly measure 1 to 7 metres across, sized so the time can be read from the street the tower serves. A dial 3 metres across typically carries minute hands about 1.2 metres long and hour hands about 0.8 metres long, with Roman numerals 25 to 35 centimetres tall. Dials are made of stone, metal, glass or GRP panels, with counterweighted hands to balance wind load.

Mechanism. The motion work in the chamber drives a long leading-off rod or bevel gearing up to the dial, turning the minute hand once per hour and the hour hand once per twelve hours. The hands must be balanced about their arbors, or their weight will drag the movement and spoil the rate. Illuminated dials are backlit or edge-lit at night, and the lighting must not heat the dial enough to distort it.

Maintenance. Inspect dial fixings, glazing and hand counterweights annually, and repaint or re-gild hands before corrosion pits the surface. Check that the hands clear the dial and each other at every position, and that the leading-off rods turn without binding in summer heat and winter cold.

Build and restoration guidance. When making a new dial, lay out the numerals from a full-size drawing and test legibility from the intended viewing distance before fixing anything. Balance each hand on a knife edge, fit counterweights, and only then connect the leading-off work and set the hands to the movement.

Tools. Dial drawing board, hand balancer, gilding brushes, glazing suction cups, torque wrench.

Hour Striking: How a Tower Clock Sounds the Hour JAH-TWC-000005

Specifications. An hour-striking tower clock sounds one blow per hour number, from one blow at one o'clock to twelve at twelve, on a bell or bells hung in the belfry. The strike train is a separate gear train with its own weight or spring, governed by a fly that limits its speed. The locking plate, or count wheel, carries notches that determine how many blows are struck for each hour.

Mechanism. At the hour, the going train lifts the strike warning and then releases the strike train. The count wheel turns step by step as each blow falls, and when the notch for the current hour is reached, the train locks again. The hammer is lifted by a cam and falls on the bell, and the fly spins to keep the blows evenly spaced rather than tumbling out at once.

Maintenance. Listen to a full twelve-hour strike sequence at least once a year and count the blows against the dial. Adjust hammer clearance so the hammer rests clear of the bell between blows, or it will muffle the tone. Oil the strike train pivots and check the fly for bent vanes, which make the striking run fast or uneven.

Build and restoration guidance. When rebuilding a strike train, prove the count wheel sequence on the bench through all twelve hours before refitting it in the tower. Set the warning interval so the strike never starts early, and test the hammer fall on the actual bell, not on a bench substitute.

Tools. Strike counter, hammer gauge, fly straightening pliers, oil cup, stethoscope or listening tube.

Quarter Chiming: The Westminster Quarters JAH-TWC-000006

Specifications. The Westminster quarters, also called the Cambridge quarters, sound a five-change sequence on four bells at each quarter hour, followed by the hour strike on the great bell. The four bells sound the notes conventionally described as E, D, C and G in the original Cambridge pattern, though the absolute pitch depends on the bells installed. The chime barrel or chiming train repeats a different permutation at each quarter so the sequence never sounds identical twice in the hour.

Mechanism. A quarter-chime train, separate from the hour-strike train, is released at each quarter by the going train. A chime barrel with pins lifts four hammers in the programmed order, and the fly governs the speed so the phrases are clearly spaced. At the hour, the quarter sequence completes and the hour strike follows on the tenor or great bell.

Maintenance. Check the chime barrel pins for looseness and the hammer leathers for wear, since a loose pin can silence one bell in the phrase. Keep the quarter train oiled and verify the sequence at all four quarters, not just at the hour. A chime that drifts out of order usually points to a slipped barrel or a bent hammer lever.

Build and restoration guidance. When restoring a chime barrel, map every pin position to its bell before removing any pin. Replace worn pins with matching stock, re-pin one phrase at a time, and test the full hour of quarters before the movement goes back into service.

Tools. Chime sequence chart, pin gauge, hammer leather stock, fly oil, sequence logbook.

Installing a Tower Clock: Chamber, Bed and Weatherproofing JAH-TWC-000007

Specifications. A tower clock chamber needs a level, rigid bed that carries the movement without flexing, headroom to remove the longest arbor, and a weight shaft clear of stairs and ladders. The chamber should be weather-tight but ventilated, with the dial opening sealed against driven rain and the belfry louvres screened against birds. Access must allow one person to wind safely and two people to lift a bell hammer.

Mechanism. The installation works only if the movement bed, the pendulum suspension and the dial leading-off work are all rigid and aligned. A bed that flexes with temperature or foot traffic changes the pendulum length and the beat. The pendulum suspension bracket must be fixed to masonry or a steel frame, never to a timber floor that moves with humidity.

Maintenance. After installation, visit weekly for the first two months and log the rate against a reliable reference, because the building settles and the pendulum suspension beds in. Recheck every fixing after the first severe storm, and keep a chamber log of winding dates, rates and any work done.

Build and restoration guidance. Build the bed first and prove it level under load before the movement arrives. Fit the dial and leading-off work before final alignment of the movement, so the hands and the train are set together rather than forced to match afterwards.

Tools. Mason's level, steel bed frame, dial sealant, bird mesh, chamber logbook.

Turret Clock Restoration: Working with Historic Movements JAH-TWC-000008

Specifications. A historic turret clock is a tower movement made before electric winding, usually of cast iron with brass wheels, a verge or anchor escapement on early examples and a gravity or deadbeat escapement on later ones. Such movements often ran for a century or more with only local repairs, so a restoration may find replaced wheels, re-cut pinions and adapted strike work. The aim is a working clock that keeps its historic fabric, not a new movement in an old frame.

Mechanism. The restorer's first job is to read the movement: which parts are original, which are repairs, and how the train was meant to run. The going train, strike train and any chime train are tested separately, and the escapement is assessed with the pendulum it was built for. Replacing a worn wheel with a modern equivalent is acceptable only where the original is beyond repair and the replacement is documented.

Maintenance. Once restored, a historic turret clock needs the same weekly winding and annual oiling as any tower clock, plus a written record of every part replaced. Keep original parts that were removed, labelled and stored, even when they are no longer fitted. Do not polish away maker's marks, dates or repair inscriptions.

Build and restoration guidance. Document before dismantling, conserve before replacing, and prove each train on the bench before reassembly. Set the beat with the dial hands fitted, because their wind load is part of the working condition, and record the final rate over at least a fortnight.

Tools. Camera and labels, conservation brushes, bushing stock, bench test frame, parts archive boxes.

Electric Impulse and Slave-Dial Tower Systems JAH-TWC-000009

Specifications. An electric impulse tower system uses a master clock, often a precision pendulum or quartz master, that sends a short electrical pulse every minute or every half-minute to slave dials around a building or site. Each slave dial contains a small stepping mechanism that advances the minute hand by one step per pulse. The system suits sites with many dials, such as stations, schools and factories, where one master keeps every dial in agreement.

Mechanism. The master clock closes a contact at each pulse, energising the slave magnets. Each magnet pulls an armature that advances a ratchet by one tooth, moving the minute hand one minute and, through gearing, the hour hand by one twelfth of that step. If power fails, the slaves stop together and restart together when pulses resume, though they then need resetting to the correct time.

Maintenance. Clean master contacts regularly, because pitted contacts send weak or double pulses. Check slave wiring for breaks, especially at tower entries where weather attacks cable, and test every dial after any power cut. Keep a master-clock rate log, since all slaves inherit the master's error.

Build and restoration guidance. When converting a mechanical tower dial to impulse drive, keep the original movement intact and stored, and fit the slave mechanism so the conversion can be reversed. Label every cable run, and prove the pulse timing at the farthest dial before commissioning.

Tools. Contact burnisher, multimeter, cable tester, slave reset key, master rate log.

Astronomical Tower Dials and Calendar Work JAH-TWC-000010

Specifications. An astronomical tower dial shows more than the time of day: it may show the date, the phase of the moon, the position of the sun and moon against the zodiac, and sometimes the positions of planets. The dial is usually large, 2 to 5 metres across, with concentric rings and moving pointers driven by extra gearing from the going train. Such dials are display and teaching instruments as much as timekeepers, and their calendar gearing must track the real sky, not just the hours.

Mechanism. Extra wheels divide the going-train rotation into daily, monthly and yearly motions. A moon disc, for example, is geared to turn once in about 29.53 days, and a sun pointer turns once per day against a zodiac ring. The gear ratios are chosen to approximate the astronomical periods closely, and the small remaining error is corrected by hand at intervals, which is why astronomical dials carry setting instructions.

Maintenance. Check the calendar indications against a reliable almanac at each visit, and correct the moon and date displays before the error becomes visible to the public. Oil the calendar gearing sparingly, because it turns slowly and excess oil collects dust. Keep the dial glazing clean, since astronomical detail is lost behind dirty glass.

Build and restoration guidance. When rebuilding calendar gearing, calculate each ratio from the astronomical period it must track and record the residual error per year. Fit hand-setting access so the calendar can be corrected without dismantling the dial, and test a full month of indications on the bench where possible.

Tools. Almanac, calendar ratio chart, fine oilers, glazing cloths, setting keys.

Bell Systems for Tower Clocks: Hammers, Frames and Tone JAH-TWC-000011

Specifications. Tower clock bells are cast in bell bronze, an alloy of roughly four parts copper to one part tin, and range from small quarter bells of tens of kilograms to great bells of several tonnes. Each bell hangs in a frame of timber or steel, struck by an external hammer for clock use rather than swung, so the bell itself stays still and only the hammer moves. The bell's note depends on its diameter, profile and weight, and the frame must carry the hammer impact without transmitting it into the tower masonry as vibration.

Mechanism. The strike or chime train lifts the hammer through a lever and lets it fall on the bell's sound bow. The hammer head is sized to the bell, and its fall distance sets the loudness. Because the bell does not swing, the frame can be compact, but the hammer lever geometry must give a clean, single blow with no double-strike or resting contact.

Maintenance. Inspect bell frames, hammer leathers and lever pivots annually, and listen for a dull or buzzing tone, which can mean a cracked bell, a loose crown staple or a hammer resting on the bell. Never strike a cracked bell, and have any suspected crack examined by a bell founder before further use.

Build and restoration guidance. When fitting a new hammer, match its weight to the bell and set the fall so the blow is firm but not violent. Prove the tone from the ground outside the tower, since a bell that sounds well in the belfry can sound harsh or weak at street level.

Tools. Hammer gauge, frame spanners, bell-founder's inspection lamp, tone logbook.

Self-Winding and Electric Rewinding Tower Movements JAH-TWC-000012

Specifications. A self-winding tower movement keeps its weights raised by a small electric motor that winds the barrel automatically when the weight reaches a lower limit, or by an electric motor that replaces the weight drive altogether. Automatic winding removes the weekly winding visit, and the motor is sized to lift the full weight load within a few minutes. A battery or mains failure backup is usually fitted so a power cut does not stop the clock immediately.

Mechanism. A limit switch or a weight-position sensor triggers the motor, which winds through a reduction gear and a ratchet that prevents back-driving. The maintaining-power arrangement still applies during winding, so the pendulum is never left undriven. On fully electric movements, a synchronous or stepping motor drives the train directly, and the pendulum or a quartz reference regulates the rate.

Maintenance. Test the auto-wind cycle monthly by letting the weight fall to its trigger point and watching a full wind. Check motor brushes or bearings, limit switches and backup supply, and keep the manual winding gear usable, because the day the motor fails is the day manual winding matters. Log every auto-wind fault.

Build and restoration guidance. When fitting auto-wind to a historic movement, mount the motor so it can be removed without altering the original frame, and keep the manual winding path intact. Set the wind limits with margin at both ends so the weight never hits a stop.

Tools. Motor test meter, limit-switch gauge, backup battery tester, manual winding crank.

Tower Clock Inspection: A Yearly Safety and Rate Routine JAH-TWC-000013

Specifications. A yearly tower clock inspection covers the movement, the pendulum suspension, the weight lines and shaft, the dial and hands, the bell hammers and frames, and the chamber itself. The inspector records the rate over a measured period, the weight fall per week, the strike and chime sequences, and the condition of every fixing. The inspection is a safety check as well as a timekeeping check, because falling weights, loose hands and cracked bells are hazards, not just faults.

Mechanism. The inspection follows the power path: weights and lines, barrels and train, escapement and pendulum, motion work and dials, strike and chime trains, hammers and bells. Each stage is checked for wear, freedom and correct action before moving on, so a fault found late in the chain can be traced back to its cause rather than patched at the symptom.

Maintenance. Keep the inspection report with the clock, and act on safety items before cosmetic ones. A frayed line, a loose hand fixing or a cracked bell frame outranks a faded dial. Re-inspect after any storm, lightning strike or building work in the tower.

Build and restoration guidance. Build the inspection into the clock's life from installation: fit safe access, a winding platform with a guard, and a logbook station in the chamber. A clock that is hard to reach is a clock that will not be inspected.

Tools. Inspection checklist, rate reference, line gauge, hand-fixing spanners, chamber logbook.

Synchronised Tower Clocks: Radio and Satellite Masters JAH-TWC-000014

Specifications. A synchronised tower clock system takes its time from a radio time signal or a satellite navigation signal and corrects the tower dials automatically, often once per hour or once per day. The receiver decodes the time, compares it with the dial position, and advances or pauses the dial drive until they agree. Such systems are used where several public dials must agree exactly, such as transport sites and civic centres.

Mechanism. The master receiver drives the dial through a stepping motor or a corrected impulse train. Small errors are corrected by pausing the drive for a few seconds or by stepping the hands forward quickly, never by spinning the hands backwards through the mechanism. The correction is deliberately gentle, so the public never sees the hands jump.

Maintenance. Check the receiver aerial and signal strength after building work or new nearby structures, since a blocked signal leaves the dials to free-run. Log corrections, because a dial that needs large corrections every day has a mechanical fault, not a signal fault. Keep a manual setting procedure posted in the chamber.

Build and restoration guidance. When fitting synchronisation to a mechanical tower clock, keep the mechanical movement as the timekeeper and use the correction only to nudge it, or replace the drive cleanly and store the original movement. Never fit a correction drive that can force the hands backwards against the train.

Tools. Signal-strength meter, correction log, manual setting key, aerial spanner.

Conserving a Historic Tower Clock in Place JAH-TWC-000015

Specifications. Conservation in place means keeping a historic tower clock working in its own tower with the least intervention that achieves safe, accurate running. The conservator records the movement as found, stabilises rust and decay, repairs only what prevents running, and leaves honest evidence of past repairs visible. The clock's history, including its scars and adaptations, is part of its value, and a conservation keeps that history readable.

Mechanism. The work follows the same power path as an inspection, but every intervention is weighed against loss of original fabric. A worn pivot hole is bushed, not re-drilled oversize; a cracked frame is stabilised, not replaced wholesale; a missing hand is remade to match its partner, not redesigned. The escapement is adjusted to run with the original pendulum wherever possible.

Maintenance. After conservation, the clock needs a gentler regime than a new movement: more frequent observation, lighter oiling, and a written conservation report that future caretakers can follow. Any new part is marked and dated, so later workers can tell old from new at a glance.

Build and restoration guidance. Photograph everything before touching it, work in small reversible steps, and stop to record whenever a new discovery changes the plan. A conservation that is not documented is only half done, because the next caretaker will have to guess what was changed.

Tools. Conservation camera, rust stabiliser, marking stamps, conservation report forms.

Designing a Modern Tower Clock: LED Dials and New Materials JAH-TWC-000016

Specifications. A modern tower clock may use an illuminated dial of glass or composite panels, LED backlighting, composite or aluminium hands, and a quartz or radio-controlled drive, while keeping the traditional dial layout and hand proportions. LED dials draw far less power than older lamp systems, run cooler, and can be dimmed at night. The hands must still be balanced and weatherproof, and the drive must survive power cuts without losing the time.

Mechanism. The drive is usually a stepping motor under master control, advancing the hands in small steps that look continuous from the ground. A battery backup holds the time through a power cut, and the dial lighting is controlled separately from the timekeeping so a lighting fault never stops the clock. The design is judged by the same test as any tower clock: can the time be read clearly, day and night, from the intended distance, in wind and rain?

Maintenance. Inspect LED drivers and seals annually, clean dial panels before grime dims the illumination, and test the battery backup by simulating a power cut. Keep spare LED modules and a spare drive motor on site, because a dark public dial is noticed immediately.

Build and restoration guidance. Design the dial, hands and drive as one system, and test the full assembly at height in wind before final fixing. Provide safe access for relamping and drive replacement, and document the control settings so a future caretaker can restore them after a fault.

Tools. LED tester, dial panel cleaner, battery backup tester, spare drive motor.
