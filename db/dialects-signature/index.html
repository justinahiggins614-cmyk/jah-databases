Signature treatment of dialect vs language: an original design-study treatment of the same topic, built in the Signature line. A dialect is a variety of a language distinguished by vocabulary, grammar and pronunciation, while a language is conventionally the umbrella under which its dialects are grouped; the boundary between the two is social and political as much as linguistic. Linguists often quote the saying that a language is a dialect with an army and a navy, because mutual intelligibility alone does not settle the question: some mutually intelligible varieties are called separate languages, and some mutually unintelligible varieties are called dialects of one language. The Signature treatment reaches the same answer as the public record for this topic, and adds a checkable design layer: the demo's computed verdict and counts. There is no purely linguistic test that always works: the distinction rests on a combination of mutual intelligibility, shared history and shared norms, plus social and political facts such as a common standard language and a common name. The demo's worked-example verdict follows the intelligibility estimate, and the record states plainly where that estimate ends and the social facts begin. As a design-study, the added layer is presented as a model for teaching and testing, not as established fact about the world.

Limits

As a design-study, this treatment is a model, not a finding; Mutual intelligibility is asymmetric (speakers of A may understand B better than the reverse), it depends on exposure and attitude, and it is a gradient, not a switch. Regional dialects are varieties of a language tied to a place: speakers in one region systematically use different sounds, words and grammatical forms from speakers elsewhere. Examples include the rhotic and non-rhotic accents of English, the northern and southern vowel systems of American English, and the many regional varieties of Arabic, German and Italian.
