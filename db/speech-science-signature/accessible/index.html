Speech Production

Speech production is the making of speech sounds by the body: the lungs push air, the vocal folds vibrate it into voice, and the tongue, lips, and jaw shape it into consonants and vowels. Each sound is a gesture of the vocal tract, and the gestures are timed so finely that a syllable takes a tenth of a second. Speech production is studied by watching the moving parts, by measuring the air, and by listening to the result. Articulation

Articulation is the movements that make speech sounds: the placing of the tongue, the closing of the lips, and the opening of the jaw, each timed to the millisecond. A t is the tongue touching the ridge behind the teeth, a p is the lips closing and opening, and a vowel is the tongue held in one place while the voice sounds. Articulation is the visible half of speech, and it is what a listener watches when the sound is unclear. Vocal Folds

The vocal folds are the two folds of tissue in the larynx that make the voice: when air from the lungs passes between them, they vibrate hundreds of times a second, and the vibration is the voice. The folds open for voiceless sounds, close for voiced sounds, and stretch for high pitch, and their vibration is the source of all voiced speech. The folds are small, about a centimetre long, and they do the work of the whole voice. Acoustic Phonetics

Acoustic phonetics is the study of the sound waves of speech: the frequencies, the amplitudes, and the durations that make one sound different from another. A vowel is recognised by its formants, the bands of energy in its spectrum, and a consonant by its noise and its transitions. Acoustic phonetics turns speech into numbers, and the numbers let machines recognise speech and let scientists measure it. Formants

Formants are the resonant bands of a vowel: the frequencies where the vocal tract amplifies the voice, and the pattern that makes one vowel different from another. The vowel in beat has a low first formant and a high second, the vowel in boot has both low, and the ear hears the pattern as the vowel's quality. Formants are the most important acoustic cue in speech, and they are measured in hertz. Spectrogram

A spectrogram is a picture of speech sounds: time runs across, frequency runs up, and darkness shows energy, so that vowels appear as bands, consonants as noise, and silence as white. The spectrogram lets the eye see what the ear hears, and it is the working picture of phonetics, used to measure sounds, to compare speakers, and to read disordered speech. Speech Perception

Speech perception is the hearing of speech sounds: how listeners turn sound waves into words, even when the sounds are unclear, fast, or accented. Listeners use the context, the grammar, and the speaker's voice to fill in what the sound leaves out, and they hear categories, not raw sound: the many versions of t are all heard as t. Speech perception is the other half of speech, and it is as active as production. Categorical Perception

Categorical perception is the hearing of sounds as categories: a sound between b and p is heard as one or the other, never as something in between, even though the sound itself changes gradually. The boundary is sharp, and sounds on one side are heard as the same, while sounds across the boundary are heard as different. Categorical perception shows that the ear does not hear raw sound: it hears the categories of the listener's language. Coarticulation

Coarticulation is the blending of sounds into their neighbours: a sound is never made alone, and its shape is pulled by the sounds before and after it. The k in key is made further forward than the k in cool, because the vowel that follows pulls the tongue, and the s in street is shaped by the t that follows. Coarticulation is why speech is fast and fluent, and why no sound is ever exactly the same twice. Prosody

Prosody is the music of speech: the rhythm, the stress, and the melody that ride on top of the words. Prosody says which word matters, whether the sentence is a question, and how the speaker feels, and it does so with pitch, loudness, and timing. Without prosody, speech would be a flat string of words; with it, the same words can ask, order, or joke. Intonation

Intonation is the melody of a sentence: the rise and fall of pitch that says whether a sentence is a question, a statement, or a surprise. English questions often rise at the end, statements fall, and the same words with a different melody mean different things. Intonation is the most audible part of prosody, and it is what a listener hears first when the words are unclear. Rhythm

Rhythm is the timing of speech: the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables that makes one language sound different from another. English is stress-timed, with stressed syllables at roughly equal intervals, while French is syllable-timed, with syllables at roughly equal intervals, and the difference is heard at once. Rhythm is part of prosody, and it is what makes speech sound like music or like a march. Voice Quality

Voice quality is the colour of a voice: whether it is breathy, creaky, harsh, or clear, and what that colour says about the speaker. Aphasia

Aphasia is the loss of language after brain damage, usually from a stroke: the person can think clearly but cannot find words, cannot build sentences, or cannot understand what is said. Aphasia takes many forms, from the halting speech of Broca's aphasia to the fluent but empty speech of Wernicke's aphasia, and it is treated by retraining the language that remains. Aphasia is a disorder of language, not of intelligence, and it is the most studied disorder in neurolinguistics. Apraxia

Apraxia is the condition in which the movements of speech will not come: the person knows what to say and can move the mouth, but cannot plan the movements that make the sounds. Speech is slow, effortful, and full of errors, and the same word may come out differently each time. Apraxia is treated by practising the movements step by step, until they become automatic again. Stuttering

Stuttering is the interruption of speech by repetitions and blocks: the speaker repeats a sound, as in b-b-b-book, or blocks on it, unable to start the word. Stuttering begins in childhood, affects more boys than girls, and is worse under stress and better when singing or speaking alone. Its cause is not known for certain, but it runs in families and is linked to the timing of speech movements. Voice Therapy

Forensic Phonetics

Forensic phonetics is the use of speech as evidence in court: the comparison of a recorded voice with a suspect's voice, the transcription of disputed recordings, and the analysis of what was said. The phonetician measures the voice, compares its features, and reports how likely it is that the two samples come from the same speaker. Forensic phonetics is used in criminal cases, and its findings must survive cross-examination. Speaker Recognition

It is used to unlock phones, to verify callers, and to find speakers in recordings, and it must work across microphones, noise, and time. Speaker recognition is about who is speaking, not what is said, and it is different from speech recognition. Auditory Processing

Auditory processing is the handling of sound by the ear and the brain: the turning of vibrations into the experience of speech, music, and noise. The ear splits sound into frequencies, the brain groups them into streams, and attention picks out the voice that matters from the noise around it. Auditory processing is the first step of hearing, and when it fails, speech is hard to follow even when the ears work. Speech Development

Speech development is the learning of speech by children: from babbling in the first year, through first words, to the full sounds of the language by the age of five. Children learn the sounds by listening and by trying, and they master the hard sounds last, so that r and th are among the last to be learned in English. Speech development is watched by parents, teachers, and therapists, because delay can be the first sign of a difficulty. Accents differ by region, by class, and by history, and every speaker has one, including those who think they do not. Accent is the most audible part of identity in speech, and it is judged, imitated, and studied more than any other feature of language.
