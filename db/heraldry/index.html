What Heraldry Is Rank 1 · simple

Heraldry is the system of identifying people, families, and institutions by designs on shields, developed in medieval Europe when armor hid faces and a visible sign was needed in battle and tournament. A coat of arms is a design granted to or recognized for a person or body, governed by rules of design, inheritance, and authority that differ by country. Heraldry is a historical and artistic system, not a shopping list of family badges: arms belong to individuals and bodies, not to surnames in general, and that correction is the most important fact in this pair. This pair teaches the system in order, from the shield to the grant, and every record names its source.

The Shield and Its Divisions Rank 2 · simple

The shield is the field on which arms are displayed, and its divisions shape every design: a shield may be divided vertically, horizontally, diagonally, or in quarters, and each division carries its own colors and charges. The chief is the top band, the base is the bottom, the dexter side is the bearer's right as viewed, and the sinister side is the bearer's left, which appears on the viewer's right. Divisions are described in blazon from the bearer's viewpoint, so a reader who describes from the viewer's viewpoint will reverse left and right. The shield's shape varies by period and country, but its divisions follow the same grammar everywhere heraldry is practiced.

Tinctures: Colours and Metals Rank 3 · simple

Tinctures are the colors and metals of heraldry: the metals gold and silver, shown as yellow and white, and the colors red, blue, black, green, and purple, with furs such as ermine and vair as a third group. The rule of tincture states that metal must not be placed on metal, nor color on color, because a design must be visible at a distance, and the rule is the first test of whether a design follows heraldic practice. Exceptions exist for historical arms granted before the rule hardened, and those exceptions are themselves evidence of age. A design that breaks the rule without a historical reason is either modern invention or error, and this pair flags it as such.

Charges: Animals, Plants, and Objects Rank 4 · simple

Charges are the figures placed on a shield: animals such as lions, eagles, and stags, plants such as roses and trees, objects such as swords, keys, and stars, and geometric shapes such as crosses, chevrons, and bends. Each charge has a standard attitude: a lion rampant stands rearing, a lion passant walks, an eagle displayed spreads its wings, and the attitude is part of the blazon, not decoration. Charges may be repeated, combined, and differenced, and a charge's meaning is rarely a simple symbol: a lion does not prove courage, and a rose does not prove a place, unless a grant's own record says so. Charges are read as design, and symbolism is claimed only where a source states it.

Blazon: The Language of Arms Rank 5 · simple

Blazon is the precise language for describing arms, written in a fixed order: field first, then divisions, then charges, with tinctures named after each element. A blazon is a recipe: a herald who reads it should be able to draw the arms without seeing them, and two heralds reading the same blazon should draw the same design. Blazon uses old French terms, such as gules for red, azure for blue, and argent for silver, and its word order is the opposite of ordinary description, with the noun before its description. Learning blazon is learning to read arms exactly, and a blazon that is vague is a design that cannot be reproduced, which is a failure of the description, not of the arms.

Crests, Helmets, and Mantling Rank 6 · simple

Crests, helmets, and mantling sit above the shield in a full achievement: the helmet's style shows rank, the mantling is the cloth that protected the helmet, shown as flowing drapery, and the crest is the figure atop the helmet, often repeating a charge from the shield. A crest is not the coat of arms itself, and a crest used alone, as in a badge or a seal, is a shortened form, not the full arms. Supporters, the figures flanking the shield, belong only to certain ranks and bodies, and mottoes, where present, are words attached to the arms, not part of the blazon's design. A full achievement is read from shield outward, and each added part has its own rules.

Grants of Arms: How Arms Were Granted Rank 7 · compounded

Grants of arms are the legal acts by which arms are given: in England, grants are made by the College of Arms, founded in 1484, under the authority of the Crown, through Kings of Arms who sign the grant. A grant is recorded in the College's books, with a blazon, a painting of the arms, and often a pedigree showing the grantee's descent, which makes grants a source for family history as well as for heraldry. Grants were not free, and they were not automatic: a petitioner had to show standing, and the heralds checked the pedigree before granting. A grant record is therefore evidence of a person, a date, and a standing, and it is cited by its College reference, not by a dealer's print.

Arms and Inheritance Rank 8 · compounded

Arms and inheritance follow rules that differ by country, but the general principle is that arms descend like property, with differences marking younger sons: in England, cadency marks such as labels, crescents, and stars distinguish sons during their father's lifetime and after. Marshalling combines arms by marriage: a wife's arms may be impaled beside her husband's, or quartered with them where she is an heiress, and quartering over generations builds a shield that displays many families at once. A shield with many quarters is a pedigree in pictures, and it is read quarter by quarter, with each quarter's origin checked against its grant or marriage record. Inheritance without a grant record is assumption, not heraldry.

Civic Heraldry: Towns and Institutions Rank 9 · compounded

Civic heraldry covers towns, cities, counties, and institutions, whose arms are granted or recognized like personal arms but belong to the body, not to any individual. Town arms often show local trade, geography, or history: a port's ship, a market town's woolpack, or a county's landscape feature, each chosen at grant and recorded in the grant. Civic arms appear on seals, buildings, and documents, and they are a public record of a body's identity, used to authenticate its acts. A town's arms are not the arms of everyone who lives there, and a resident who displays them as personal arms misunderstands the system.

Ecclesiastical Heraldry Rank 10 · compounded

Ecclesiastical heraldry covers bishops, dioceses, abbeys, and other church bodies, whose arms follow the same grammar with additions: a bishop's shield is often impaled with his diocese's arms, and ecclesiastical hats, called galeros, with tassels marking rank, replace helmets above the shield. Diocesan arms often show local saints, symbols, or geography, and they are granted or recognized through the same heraldic authorities. Ecclesiastical arms belong to the office or body, and a bishop's personal arms, where he has them, are distinct from his diocese's. Reading church arms requires both grammars: the shield's design and the ecclesiastical additions above it.

Heraldry in Scotland: Lord Lyon Rank 11 · compounded

Heraldry in Scotland is governed by the Court of the Lord Lyon, whose authority over Scottish arms is older and stricter than the English system in some respects: Scottish arms are protected by law, and the Lyon Court prosecutes unauthorized use. Scottish practice includes distinctive features: the use of crests and mottoes within a badge for clansmen, and a strict system of differencing for cadets. A Scottish grant is recorded in the Lyon Register, and a Scottish pedigree in heraldry must be checked against that register, not against an English grant. The Scottish system is a reminder that heraldry is national law, not a single European custom.

Heraldry Beyond Britain: Continental Systems Rank 12 · complex

Heraldry beyond Britain includes the continental systems of France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and others, each with its own authorities, or lack of them, and its own traditions. French heraldry was largely abolished and later revived in regulated and unregulated forms, German heraldry includes burgher arms for non-noble families, and Italian heraldry varies by city and region, with civic arms of great age. A continental coat must be read under its own country's rules: a design that is lawful in one system may be assumed, ungrantable, or meaningless in another. Comparative heraldry is therefore a study of systems, not a single rulebook, and this record introduces each system by its authority and its practice.

Heraldic Fraud and Assumed Arms Rank 13 · complex

Heraldic fraud and assumed arms are the trade in arms sold to people who have no grant: dealers who attach a surname to a coat and sell it as a family's arms, regardless of whether any bearer of that name was ever granted those arms. The fraud works because buyers believe arms belong to surnames, which they do not, and because a printed coat looks authoritative. The test is the grant record: arms without a grant, or with a grant to a different person of the same name, are not the buyer's arms, and displaying them as such is assumption, not inheritance. This record states the test plainly so that no reader of this pair is misled by a sale, and it names no dealer and no buyer.

Reading Arms in Churches and Monuments Rank 14 · complex

Reading arms in churches and monuments is a field method: arms carved on tombs, painted on walls, and set in windows identify the people commemorated, their marriages, and their deaths, when the arms are read with their blazons and dates. A tomb's arms may show quarterings that trace a pedigree, impalements that show a marriage, and differencing that shows a younger son, and a monument's date fixes the arms in time. Monuments concern people long deceased, and their arms are historical evidence, not personal data about anyone living. A monument read without its blazon is a picture, and a monument read with its blazon and its date is a record.

Heraldry and Family History: What Arms Prove Rank 15 · complex

Heraldry and family history meet in the grant record, which often contains a pedigree, and in the quartered shield, which displays a pedigree, but arms prove only what their grant or monument states. Arms prove that a named person bore those arms at that date, and, through quartering, that certain marriages occurred, but they do not prove that every bearer of a surname descends from that person, and they do not prove a pedigree beyond the grant's own statements. The family historian uses arms as a clue to a grant, a will, or a monument, and then proves the pedigree from those records, not from the shield alone. Arms are evidence, and like all evidence, they are strong within their limits and weak beyond them.
