Alpine Skiing Rank 1 · simple · skiing · beginner

Alpine skiing is racing and skiing downhill on marked courses, and it is the best-known form of skiing, practised at resorts worldwide. Racers compete in downhill, super-G, giant slalom, and slalom, which differ by course length, gate spacing, and speed, with downhill the fastest and slalom the most technical. Recreational skiers use the same skills at gentler grades: edging, carving turns, controlling speed, and stopping safely. Alpine skiing has been an Olympic sport since 1936, and its World Cup circuit, first held in 1967, is the sport's leading annual competition. Helmets are standard, and avalanche awareness matters when skiing beyond marked runs.

History

Alpine skiing grew from Alpine travel and military skiing in the nineteenth century and became a resort sport and Olympic discipline in the twentieth.

Rules

Racers pass gates in order and are timed; missing a gate disqualifies a run, and the fastest combined or single-run time wins.

Techniques

Carving turns, edging, pole plants, and speed control through turn shape are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets are standard, bindings release in falls, and off-piste skiing requires avalanche training and equipment.

Cross-Country Skiing Rank 2 · simple · skiing · beginner

Cross-country skiing is skiing across flat and rolling terrain on narrow skis, using striding, skating, and double-poling techniques, and it is both a racing sport and a winter travel method. Racers compete in sprint, individual, pursuit, mass start, and relay events over courses from about one kilometre to fifty kilometres, using classic technique in prepared tracks or skate technique on groomed snow. The sport trains the whole body and rewards efficiency, since races are long and energy must last. Cross-country skiing has been an Olympic sport since the first Winter Games in 1924, and its leading events include the Olympic programme, the World Championships, and the World Cup.

History

Cross-country skiing grew from Scandinavian winter travel and military skiing, and it has been an Olympic sport since Chamonix 1924.

Rules

Classic races run in prepared tracks and skate races on groomed snow, with mass starts, pursuits, and relays among the formats.

Techniques

Diagonal stride, double poling, kick double-pole, and skate techniques including V1 and V2 are the core skills.

Training

Training pairs technique sessions with long endurance skis, roller skiing in summer, and strength work.

Working demo program

print('Distance: %.2f km in %.1f minutes' % (DIST_KM, MINUTES))
print('Pace: %.2f minutes per km' % pace)
print('Average speed: %.2f km per hour' % speed)

Ski Jumping Rank 3 · simple · skiing · beginner

Ski jumping is launching from a ramp and flying as far as possible before landing, judged on distance and style. Jumpers race down an inrun in a crouched position, take off from the edge of the table, lean forward into a V-shaped ski position in flight, and land in a telemark position with one ski ahead of the other. Hills are sized by their construction point, from normal hills of about ninety metres to ski flying hills above two hundred metres, and judges score style alongside distance points. Ski jumping has been an Olympic sport since 1924, and its leading events include the World Cup and the Four Hills Tournament, first held in 1953.

History

Ski jumping grew in Norway in the nineteenth century and has been an Olympic sport since the first Winter Games in 1924.

Rules

Points combine distance and style marks from five judges, with the highest and lowest style marks discarded.

Techniques

The inrun crouch, the take-off, the V-style flight position, and the telemark landing are the core phases.

Safety

Jumpers progress from small hills to large ones only when landings are clean, and coaches supervise all jumping.

Snowboarding Rank 4 · simple · snow · beginner

Snowboarding is riding down snow on a single wide board, with both feet bound sideways, and it grew from surfing and skateboarding influences in the 1960s and 1970s. Riders compete in parallel giant slalom, snowboard cross, halfpipe, slopestyle, and big air, which differ by racing, jumping, and trick judging, and recreational riders carve groomed runs and ride parks. The core skills are edge control on heel and toe sides, carving turns, and jumping and landing with balance. Snowboarding entered the Olympic Games in 1998, and its leading events include the Olympic programme, the World Cup, and the X Games.

History

Snowboarding grew from early boards such as the Snurfer in the 1960s and became a resort and Olympic sport in the 1990s.

Rules

Parallel giant slalom is head-to-head racing, snowboard cross races four riders at once, and halfpipe, slopestyle, and big air are judged on tricks.

Techniques

Heel-side and toe-side edges, carving, ollies, grabs, and spins are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets and wrist guards are standard, beginners learn on gentle slopes, and park features are progressed by size.

Biathlon Rank 5 · simple · skiing · beginner

Biathlon pairs cross-country skiing with rifle shooting, and it grew from Scandinavian military ski patrols into one of the Winter Olympic Games' most watched sports. Athletes ski loops and stop at a range to shoot five targets, skiing a penalty loop for each miss in most formats, and races include sprint, pursuit, individual, mass start, and relay events. The sport's drama comes from the switch between all-out skiing and the stillness shooting demands, since a racing heart makes a steady rifle hard to hold. Biathlon has been an Olympic sport since 1960 for men and 1992 for women, and its World Cup is the sport's leading annual circuit.

History

Biathlon grew from military ski patrol competitions and entered the Olympic Games at Squaw Valley in 1960.

Rules

Athletes shoot five targets at each range visit, and each miss costs a penalty loop or added time, depending on the format.

Techniques

Skiing technique, range routine, breathing control, and trigger control under fatigue are the core skills.

Safety

Rifles are handled only on the range under supervision, and shooting is taught as a disciplined, safety-first skill.

Bobsleigh Rank 6 · compounded · sliding · intermediate

Bobsleigh is racing a sled down an ice track, in which a crew pushes the sled at the start, loads in, and steers through banked curves at high speed. Two-person and four-person sleds race on the same tracks, and the start push is so important that teams recruit sprinters and train explosive starts. The driver steers with rings or a steering mechanism, while brakemen help push and brake at the finish, and races are decided by combined times over several runs. Bobsleigh has been an Olympic sport since 1924, and its tracks, such as St Moritz in Switzerland, are among the sport's most famous venues.

History

Bobsleigh grew from tobogganing at St Moritz in the late nineteenth century and has been an Olympic sport since 1924.

Rules

Two-person and four-person events are decided by combined times over several runs, and the fastest total wins.

Techniques

The explosive start push, clean loading, and precise steering through banked curves are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets are mandatory, tracks are inspected, and crews progress from slower starts to full-speed runs.

Luge Rank 7 · compounded · sliding · intermediate

Luge is racing a small sled down an ice track feet-first, lying on the back, and it is the fastest of the sliding sports, with speeds above 130 kilometres per hour on some tracks. Athletes start by paddling with spiked gloves on the ice, then lie back and steer with small leg and shoulder movements and by pressing on the sled's runners. Singles and doubles events are raced, and doubles pairs lie one atop the other, and races are decided by combined times over several runs. Luge has been an Olympic sport since 1964, and its tracks include famous venues such as Oberhof in Germany and Lake Placid in the United States.

History

Luge grew from Alpine sledding traditions and became an Olympic sport at Innsbruck in 1964.

Rules

Singles and doubles events are decided by combined times over several runs, and the fastest total wins.

Techniques

The paddling start, the flat lying position, and steering by small leg and shoulder pressures are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets and protective gear are mandatory, athletes progress from slower starts, and tracks are inspected before racing.

Skeleton Rank 8 · compounded · sliding · intermediate

Skeleton is racing a small sled down an ice track head-first, lying face-down, and it is the oldest of the sliding sports in Olympic history, having appeared at St Moritz in 1928 and 1948 before returning permanently in 2002. Athletes sprint beside the sled, dive onto it, and steer with small body movements and by dragging a toe, reaching high speeds on the same tracks used by bobsleigh and luge. Races are decided by combined times over several runs, and the sport demands sprinting power, courage, and precise line choice through banked curves. Skeleton is raced by men and women in singles events, and team events pair skeleton with bobsleigh athletes.

History

Skeleton was raced at St Moritz in 1928 and 1948 as the Cresta Run's Olympic cousin, then returned permanently at Salt Lake City in 2002.

Rules

Singles events are decided by combined times over several runs, and the fastest total wins.

Techniques

The sprint start, the dive onto the sled, and steering by body movement and toe drag are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets and protective gear are mandatory, athletes progress from slower starts, and tracks are inspected before racing.

Speed Skating Rank 9 · compounded · skating · intermediate

Speed skating is racing on ice over oval tracks, in which skaters race in pairs against the clock in long-track events or in packs in short-track events, and long-track races run from 500 metres to 10000 metres. Long-track skaters race in lanes and change lanes each lap on the back straight, while mass-start races put many skaters on the ice together. The sport demands a deep crouched position, long powerful pushes, and efficient cornering, and its leading events include the Olympic Games, the World Championships, and the World Cup. Speed skating has been an Olympic sport since 1924, and its most famous venue is the high-altitude rink at Calgary, known for fast ice.

History

Speed skating grew from Dutch canal skating and became an Olympic sport at Chamonix in 1924, with women's events added in 1960.

Rules

Long-track races are timed in pairs with lane changes each lap, and mass-start races are pack races decided by finish order and points.

Techniques

The deep crouch, the long side push, the arm swing, and cornering on crossovers are the core skills.

Training

Training pairs ice sessions with cycling, weights, and dry-land imitation drills that rehearse the skating position.

Short-Track Speed Skating Rank 10 · compounded · skating · intermediate

Short-track speed skating is pack racing on a small ice oval, in which four to six skaters race together over distances from 500 metres to 5000 metres in relays, with passing, strategy, and pack tactics deciding races. Races are fast and physical, with skaters leaning deep into corners, touching the ice with a hand for balance, and passing on the outside or inside when a gap opens. Falls and disqualifications are common, since pack racing at speed leaves little room, and relay teams exchange by pushing the next skater. Short-track became an Olympic sport in 1992, and its leading events include the Olympic programme and the World Championships.

History

Short-track grew from pack racing on small rinks and became an Olympic sport at Albertville in 1992.

Rules

Pack races are decided by finish order, and skaters are disqualified for impeding, pushing, or cutting inside markers.

Techniques

Deep cornering, hand-to-ice balance, drafting, and passing on gaps are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets, gloves, and cut-resistant suits are standard, and rink padding protects skaters who fall into the boards.

Figure Skating Rank 11 · complex · skating · advanced

Figure skating is performing jumps, spins, and step sequences on ice to music, judged on technical execution and artistic presentation, and it is one of the Winter Games' most watched sports. Singles skaters perform short and free programmes, pairs combine lifts, throws, and synchronised jumps, and ice dancers perform pattern dances and free dances without overhead lifts or jumps. Jumps are named by their take-off edge and rotation, from the single axel to the quadruple jumps now landed by leading skaters, and spins range from upright spins to sit spins and layback spins. Figure skating has been an Olympic sport since 1908, first at the Summer Games, and its leading events include the Olympic programme, the World Championships, and the Grand Prix series.

History

Figure skating grew from nineteenth-century skating clubs and became an Olympic sport at London in 1908, before the Winter Games existed.

Rules

Programmes are scored for technical elements and programme components, with deductions for falls and errors.

Techniques

Jumps, spins, step sequences, and for pairs, lifts and throws, are the core elements.

Training

Training pairs ice sessions with off-ice jumps, ballet, strength, and flexibility work.

Curling Rank 12 · complex · snow · advanced

Curling is sliding granite stones across ice toward a target called the house, while teammates sweep the ice ahead of a moving stone to control its speed and curl. Teams of four take turns delivering stones, and the skip directs strategy from the far end, calling shots such as draws, guards, and takeouts, while sweepers judge weight and line. Points are scored for each stone closer to the centre than the opponent's best stone, and games usually run eight or ten ends. Curling has been an Olympic sport since 1998, though it appeared at the first Winter Games in 1924, and its leading events include the Olympic programme and the World Championships.

History

Curling grew in Scotland from the sixteenth century, with stones slid on frozen lochs, and it became an Olympic sport at Nagano in 1998.

Rules

Each team delivers eight stones per end, points go to stones closer to the centre than the opponent's best, and games run eight or ten ends.

Techniques

The slide delivery, releasing with a turn, sweeping for weight and line, and calling shots are the core skills.

Strategy

Guards protect stones, draws seek the house, takeouts remove opponents' stones, and the skip plans each end.

Freestyle Skiing Rank 13 · complex · skiing · advanced

Freestyle skiing covers the aerial and mogul disciplines of skiing, in which athletes jump, spin, and flip off kickers, ski through mogul fields, and race through courses with jumps. Mogul skiing judges turns through bumps, two jumps, and speed; aerials judges jumps off large kickers for height, form, and landing; and ski cross races four skiers head-to-head over jumps and banked turns. The sport grew from freestyle exhibitions in the 1960s and 1970s and entered the Olympic Games in 1992, with slopestyle and big air added later. Training pairs trampoline and water-ramp practice with on-snow sessions, so aerial skills are learned safely before snow.

History

Freestyle skiing grew from 1960s and 1970s exhibitions and became an Olympic sport at Albertville in 1992.

Rules

Moguls are judged on turns, jumps, and speed; aerials on height, form, and landing; ski cross by finish order.

Techniques

Mogul turns, aerial take-offs and landings, grabs, spins, and flips are the core skills.

Safety

Trampoline and water-ramp training precede snow aerials, helmets are mandatory, and features progress by size.

Ski Mountaineering Rank 14 · complex · skiing · advanced

Ski mountaineering is travelling through mountains on skis, climbing with skins attached to the ski bases for grip and then descending, and it combines ski touring, mountaineering, and racing. Races run over marked mountain courses with climbs, descents, and transitions between skinning and skiing, and recreational ski touring covers the same skills at a gentler pace. The sport demands route finding, avalanche awareness, fitness for long climbs, and confident descending in varied snow. Ski mountaineering entered the Olympic programme at Milano Cortina 2026, and its leading events include the World Cup and the World Championships.

History

Ski mountaineering grew from Alpine winter travel and military ski patrols and became an organised racing sport in the twentieth century.

Rules

Races run over mountain courses with climbs, descents, and transitions, and athletes must carry safety equipment.

Techniques

Skinning uphill, kick turns, transitions, and descending in varied snow are the core skills.

Safety

Avalanche training, transceiver, shovel, and probe are mandatory, and routes are chosen by conditions.

Ice Climbing Rank 15 · complex · snow · advanced

Ice climbing is climbing frozen waterfalls and ice formations with ice axes and crampons, and it is practised as a mountain skill, a recreation, and a competition sport on artificial ice structures. Climbers swing axes into the ice, place their crampon points, and move upward with efficient, balanced technique, and competition ice climbing races athletes up artificial walls and overhangs. The sport demands cold-weather clothing, rope and protection skills, and the ability to read ice, which varies from soft and plastic to hard and brittle with temperature. Ice climbing competitions are run by the international climbing federation, and the sport's leading events include the World Cup.

History

Ice climbing grew from Alpine mountaineering in the nineteenth century and became a competition sport on artificial structures in the twentieth.

Rules

Competition climbing is timed or judged on height reached, and climbers use two axes and crampons on artificial or natural ice.

Techniques

Swinging axes cleanly, placing crampon points, and moving with balance and efficiency are the core skills.

Safety

Helmets are mandatory, ropes and protection are used, and ice is assessed for temperature and stability before climbing.

Snowshoeing Rank 16 · complex · snow · advanced

Snowshoeing is walking on snow with snowshoes, which spread the walker's weight so they do not sink deeply, and it is one of the oldest winter travel methods, used for hunting, trapping, and travel across snowy regions. Modern snowshoes are light frames with decking and bindings, and they are used for winter hiking, trail running, and racing, with races from short sprints to long distance events. The core skills are walking with a wider gait, climbing and descending in snowshoes, and navigating winter trails, and snowshoeing is accessible to beginners, since it builds directly on walking. Snowshoe races are run by national federations, and the sport appears at winter multi-sport events.

History

Snowshoeing is ancient, used by Indigenous peoples of North America and by travellers across snowy regions for thousands of years.

Rules

Snowshoe races run over marked courses, and racers must wear snowshoes for the whole course.

Techniques

Walking with a wider gait, climbing with toe crampons, and descending with control are the core skills.

Safety

Layers, weather checks, and avalanche awareness on steep terrain are standard, and groups carry navigation gear.
