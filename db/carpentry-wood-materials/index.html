Pine Rank 1 · simple · softwood · beginner

Pine is the most common softwood for general carpentry: light, soft, inexpensive, and easy to cut, nail, and glue. Its grain is straight with prominent darker growth rings and occasional knots, and it takes stain unevenly unless a pre-stain conditioner is used first. Eastern white pine is softer and clearer than yellow pine, which is denser and stronger and suits structural work. Pine moves noticeably with humidity, so interior pine should be acclimated in the room for several days before it is cut. Pine dust is a respiratory irritant, so sanding calls for a dust mask and extraction.

Specifications

Boards commonly sold 1x6, 1x8, and 1x12, actually 3/4 inch thick; density about 25 to 36 pounds per cubic foot depending on species

  • Density: eastern white pine about 25 lb per cubic foot, southern yellow pine about 36 lb per cubic foot
  • Janka hardness: white pine about 380 lbf, yellow pine about 690 lbf
  • Cost tier: low
  • Workability: cuts, nails, and glues easily; planes cleanly with sharp blades
  • Movement: about 0.002 inch per inch of width per 1 percent moisture change (tangential)
  • Best uses: shelves, trim, framing, painted furniture

Materials:

  • Sample board (pine, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the material guide itself

Tolerances: Board dimensions within 1/32 inch; moisture content 6 to 8 percent for interior work

Arithmetic note: Board feet check out: thickness x width x length in inches divided by 144, times the board count. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Acclimate the pine boards in the room for 3 to 5 days, stickered with air gaps.
  2. Inspect each board for knots, splits, and twist, and mark the best face.
  3. Cut test pieces and check how the stain or paint behaves on an offcut.
  4. Apply pre-stain conditioner if staining, then stain or paint as planned.
  5. Let the finish cure fully before stacking boards face to face.

Tools required

  • Tape measure
  • Pencil
  • Moisture meter
  • Straightedge
  • Hand plane or sander for test pieces

Safety gear:

  • Safety glasses
  • Dust mask

Safety notes: Wear a dust mask when sanding and cutting, and use dust extraction where available. Check every board for nails or staples before machining.

Oak Rank 2 · simple · hardwood · intermediate

Oak is a strong, open-grained hardwood in two main kinds: red oak, which is pinkish-brown and porous, and white oak, which is browner, denser, and more water-resistant. Its prominent grain and ray fleck make it a classic furniture and flooring wood, and it holds screws and joints exceptionally well. Red oak's open pores drink up finish and need grain filler for a smooth top, while white oak suits outdoor use better because its pores are plugged with tyloses. Oak dust is a strong irritant, so sand with a mask and good extraction, and avoid iron fasteners in wet oak, which stains the wood black.

Specifications

Boards commonly 4/4 (about 3/4 inch finished) and 8/4; density about 44 to 47 pounds per cubic foot

  • Density: red oak about 44 lb per cubic foot, white oak about 47 lb per cubic foot
  • Janka hardness: red oak about 1,290 lbf, white oak about 1,360 lbf
  • Cost tier: medium to high
  • Workability: machines well; pre-drill for screws near edges to avoid splitting
  • Movement: about 0.0037 inch per inch per 1 percent moisture change (tangential, red oak)
  • Best uses: furniture, flooring, cabinetry, white oak for outdoor pieces

Materials:

  • Sample board (oak, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the material guide itself

Tolerances: Board dimensions within 1/32 inch; moisture content 6 to 8 percent for interior work

Arithmetic note: Weight checks out: board volume in cubic feet (inches cubed divided by 1728) times density. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Acclimate oak boards for at least a week in the finished room.
  2. Sort boards by color and grain so the finished piece reads as one wood.
  3. Pre-drill screw holes, especially near ends and edges.
  4. Fill the open grain if a glass-smooth top is wanted, then sand to 220-grit.
  5. Finish with oil or polyurethane and let each coat cure before recoating.

Maple Rank 3 · simple · hardwood · intermediate

Maple is a pale, dense hardwood with a fine, even grain that finishes beautifully and resists dents, which is why it is the standard wood for butcher blocks, workbench tops, and cutting boards. Hard maple is much denser than soft maple, and figured maple with curl or birdseye commands a premium for visible panels. Maple burns easily under dull router bits and saw blades, so sharp tooling and steady feed matter, and it can blotch under stain, so a conditioner or a gel stain gives a more even color. Maple dust calls for a mask, and maple glue joints are among the strongest in woodworking when the surfaces are fresh and flat.

Specifications

Boards commonly 4/4 and 8/4; density about 44 pounds per cubic foot for hard maple

  • Density: hard maple about 44 lb per cubic foot, soft maple about 34 lb per cubic foot
  • Janka hardness: hard maple about 1,450 lbf, soft maple about 950 lbf
  • Cost tier: medium; figured maple high
  • Workability: machines cleanly with sharp tools; burns with dull bits
  • Movement: about 0.0035 inch per inch per 1 percent moisture change (tangential)
  • Best uses: workbench tops, butcher blocks, cutting boards, cabinetry

Materials:

How to

  3. Test stain on an offcut with conditioner before committing to the piece.
  4. Glue up panels with fresh, flat surfaces and even clamp pressure.
  5. Sand to 220-grit and finish with oil or polyurethane for a hard-wearing top.

Walnut Rank 4 · simple · hardwood · advanced

Walnut is a dark chocolate-brown hardwood prized for fine furniture, and its color varies from pale sapwood at the edge to deep heartwood in the center. It machines, glues, and finishes superbly, and a simple oil finish brings out a depth that few other woods match. Walnut is expensive, so careful layout matters: plan cuts to keep sapwood out of visible faces, or steam the sapwood color in where a uniform look is wanted. Walnut dust can irritate skin and lungs, so wear a mask and gloves when sanding, and keep walnut shavings away from horses, for which they are toxic.

Specifications

Boards commonly 4/4 and 8/4; density about 38 pounds per cubic foot

  • Density: about 38 lb per cubic foot
  • Janka hardness: about 1,010 lbf
  • Cost tier: high
  • Workability: excellent; machines, glues, and finishes with little trouble
  • Movement: about 0.0027 inch per inch per 1 percent moisture change (tangential)
  • Best uses: fine furniture, cabinetry, boxes, gunstocks

Materials:

How to

  2. Acclimate boards for a week and sort by color before cutting.
  3. Cut the most visible parts from the darkest, most even boards.
  4. Sand to 220-grit and test an oil finish on an offcut.
  5. Apply oil in thin coats, wiping off all excess, and let each coat cure.

Cherry Rank 5 · simple · hardwood · intermediate

Cherry is a warm reddish-brown hardwood that darkens with age and light exposure, so a new cherry piece will deepen in color over its first months in a sunny room. Its grain is fine and straight with occasional gum pockets, and it finishes to a smooth, glowing surface with oil or lacquer. Cherry burns readily under dull tools, so sharp blades and steady feed are essential, and it blotches under stain unless a conditioner is used. Because cherry darkens unevenly, keep finished pieces out of patchy sunlight and let the whole piece age evenly.

Poplar Rank 6 · compounded · hardwood · beginner

Poplar is a light, soft hardwood that is the workhorse of painted projects: it is inexpensive, stable, easy to machine, and paints to a smooth surface with no visible grain telegraphing through. Its natural color runs from pale cream to greenish-brown streaks, which is why it is rarely used for clear finishes, though the green tones fade with light. Poplar takes screws and glue well, dents more easily than oak or maple, and is an ideal first hardwood for learning joinery. Poplar dust still calls for a mask, as with all wood dust.

Specifications

Boards commonly 4/4 and 8/4; density about 28 pounds per cubic foot

  • Cost tier: low to medium
  • Workability: excellent; machines, glues, and paints easily

Cedar Rank 7 · compounded · softwood · beginner

Cedar is a light, aromatic softwood that resists rot and insects naturally, which makes it the standard wood for outdoor furniture, decking, and closets. Western red cedar is soft, straight-grained, and weathers to a silver-grey if left unfinished, while aromatic red cedar's strong scent repels moths in closets and chests. Cedar splits easily, so screws near ends must be pre-drilled, and its softness means it dents under heavy use. Cedar dust can irritate lungs and skin, so sand outdoors or with extraction and a mask, and never burn cedar scraps in a closed stove without checking local rules, since the oils smoke heavily.

Plywood Rank 8 · compounded · sheet · beginner

Plywood is made of thin wood veneers glued with their grain directions alternating, which makes a sheet that is strong, flat, and far more stable than solid wood of the same size. Grades run from cabinet-grade birch and maple plywood with clean faces down to construction CDX with knots and patches, and the right grade depends on whether the face will show. Plywood cuts best with a fine-tooth blade and a scoring pass or tape on the cut line to prevent tear-out on the face. Plywood dust and the adhesives in it call for a mask, and formaldehyde-free options exist for interior cabinetry.

Specifications

Sheets 4 feet by 8 feet, thicknesses 1/4, 1/2, and 3/4 inch; 3/4-inch sheet weighs about 60 to 70 pounds

  • Sheet size: 48 in x 96 in (32 sq ft)
  • 3/4 in birch plywood weight: about 65 lb per sheet
  • Veneer layers in 3/4 in sheet: typically 7 to 13 plies
  • Cost tier: low (construction) to high (cabinet-grade hardwood)
  • Movement: very low across the sheet, about 0.0002 in per in per 1 percent moisture change
  • Best uses: cabinetry, shelving, drawer boxes, subfloors

Materials:

  • Sample board (plywood, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the material guide itself

Tolerances: Board dimensions within 1/32 inch; moisture content 6 to 8 percent for interior work

Arithmetic note: Sheet yield checks out: floor of sheet width over piece width times floor of sheet height over piece height. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Choose the grade by whether the face will be visible.
  2. Support the full sheet and cut with a fine-tooth blade, good face down on a circular saw.
  3. Seal cut edges with edge banding or finish to keep moisture out.
  4. Pre-drill near edges to avoid splitting the face veneer.
  5. Sand faces lightly to 150-grit before finishing.

MDF Rank 9 · compounded · sheet · intermediate

MDF, medium-density fiberboard, is made of wood fibers pressed with resin into a dense, perfectly flat sheet with no grain, which makes it ideal for painted panels, routed profiles, and speaker boxes. It machines beautifully and takes paint to a glass-smooth surface, but it is heavy, it swells and falls apart if it gets wet, and its edges soak up paint unless sealed first. MDF dust is fine and heavy and must be controlled with extraction and a respirator, and screws need pilot holes and coarse threads or they will strip. MDF is never used where it will stay wet, and it is never used for structural spans without support.

OSB Rank 10 · compounded · sheet · beginner

OSB, oriented strand board, is made of wood strands pressed in layers with their directions alternating, and it is the standard structural sheathing for walls, floors, and roofs. It is strong and inexpensive, though its rough surface is never used as a finished face, and its edges swell if they stay wet for long. OSB panels carry span ratings stamped on the sheet, and those ratings must be followed for floors and roofs. OSB dust calls for a mask, and OSB offcuts are not used for fine furniture, since the surface cannot be finished smooth.

Hardwood Veneer Rank 12 · complex · veneer · advanced

Reclaimed Lumber Rank 13 · complex · reclaimed · advanced

Reclaimed boards are often old-growth wood, denser and more stable than new lumber of the same species, but they vary in thickness and need jointing and planing to a uniform size. Pressure-Treated Lumber Rank 15 · complex · treated · intermediate

Pressure-treated lumber is pine or fir forced full of preservative under pressure so it resists rot and insects in ground contact and outdoor use, and it is the standard wood for decks, fences, and posts. Modern treatments are copper-based and far less toxic than the old chromated copper arsenate, but treated lumber is still not used for cutting boards, food contact, or indoor furniture, and its dust and offcuts are never burned. Lumber Grading and Selection Rank 16 · complex · selection · advanced

Lumber grading is the system that sorts boards by strength and appearance, and knowing it saves money and prevents failures. Hardwood lumber is graded by the percentage of clear, defect-free wood in the board, from FAS at 83 percent clear down to Number 1 Common at 66 percent, while softwood dimension lumber is graded by strength, with Select Structural at the top and Number 2 as the common framing grade. Moisture content matters as much as grade: interior lumber should measure 6 to 8 percent, exterior lumber 12 to 15 percent, and a simple pin meter checks it in seconds. Selecting lumber means sighting down each board for bow, cup, twist, and crook, rejecting boards with splits at the ends, and buying boards a little longer than needed so defects can be cut away.

Specifications

Hardwood grading: FAS 83 percent clear, Number 1 Common 66 percent clear; softwood: Select Structural, Number 1, Number 2

  • FAS clear yield: at least 83 percent
  • Number 1 Common clear yield: at least 66 percent
  • Interior moisture target: 6 to 8 percent
  • Exterior moisture target: 12 to 15 percent
  • Reject twist over 1/4 in in an 8 ft board for fine work
  • Cost tier: grade decides price; FAS costs roughly twice Number 1 Common

Materials:

  • Sample board (lumber, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the material guide itself

Tolerances: Board dimensions within 1/32 inch; moisture content 6 to 8 percent for interior work
