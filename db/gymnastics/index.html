Men's Artistic Gymnastics Rank 1 · simple · artistic gymnastics · advanced

Men's artistic gymnastics is contested on six apparatus: floor, pommel horse, rings, vault, parallel bars, and horizontal bar, and it has been part of the Olympic Games since the first modern Games in 1896. Each routine is scored on difficulty and execution, with judges deducting for form breaks, steps on landing, and falls, and the all-around competition combines all six apparatus into one total. Floor routines mix tumbling passes with strength and balance elements, pommel horse demands continuous swinging and scissoring without stopping, and rings require still strength holds such as the iron cross. Vault is a single explosive effort over the table, parallel bars mix swings with holds, and horizontal bar ends with release moves and a dismount. The sport rewards strength, flexibility, air awareness, and the nerve to perform difficult skills without a safety net.

History

Men's artistic gymnastics has been Olympic since the first modern Games in 1896, with the six-apparatus format standardised over the twentieth century.

Rules

Routines score difficulty plus execution minus deductions. Falls, steps, and form breaks cost marks; the all-around sums all six apparatus.

Techniques

Tumbling, swinging, still holds, release moves, and stuck landings are the core technical elements.

Training

Daily sessions mix apparatus work, strength and conditioning, flexibility, and landing drills.

Women's Artistic Gymnastics Rank 2 · simple · artistic gymnastics · advanced

Women's artistic gymnastics is contested on four apparatus: vault, uneven bars, balance beam, and floor, and women have competed in Olympic gymnastics since 1928. Vault is a single powerful effort over the table, uneven bars link swinging, release moves, and transitions between the high and low bars, beam demands acrobatics and dance on a beam only ten centimetres wide, and floor combines tumbling passes with dance and artistry to music. Scoring follows the same difficulty-plus-execution model as the men's event, with deductions for wobbles, steps, and falls, and the all-around combines all four apparatus. The sport rewards power, flexibility, balance, and performance, because beam and floor ask gymnasts to be both athletes and performers.

History

Women's artistic gymnastics entered the Olympic Games in 1928, with the four-apparatus format standardised over the twentieth century.

Rules

Routines score difficulty plus execution minus deductions. The all-around sums vault, uneven bars, beam, and floor.

Techniques

Vaulting power, bar transitions and releases, beam balance and acrobatics, and floor tumbling with dance are the core elements.

Training

Daily sessions mix apparatus work, dance, strength and conditioning, flexibility, and landing drills.

Rhythmic Gymnastics Rank 3 · simple · rhythmic gymnastics · advanced

Rhythmic gymnastics combines dance, flexibility, and apparatus handling, performed to music with hoop, ball, clubs, ribbon, or rope, and it became an Olympic sport in 1984. Gymnasts perform routines that blend leaps, balances, pivots, and flexibility elements with continuous apparatus work: the hoop rolls and spins, the ball bounces and balances, the clubs juggle and mill, the ribbon draws patterns in the air, and the rope skips and throws. Judging rewards difficulty, artistry, and execution, with deductions for apparatus drops, loss of control, and form breaks. Group routines add a further dimension, with five gymnasts handling two apparatus types in synchronised exchanges and formations.

History

Rhythmic gymnastics developed from dance and expressive movement in the twentieth century and entered the Olympic Games in 1984.

Rules

Routines score difficulty, artistry, and execution. Apparatus drops and losses of control are deducted; routines run to music.

Techniques

Leaps, balances, pivots, flexibility elements, and continuous apparatus handling are the core elements.

Training

Daily sessions mix dance, flexibility, apparatus drills, and full routine runs.

Trampoline Rank 4 · simple · trampoline gymnastics · intermediate

Trampoline gymnastics is performed on a sprung trampoline bed, with athletes completing routines of ten skills, including somersaults and twists in tucked, piked, and straight positions, and it became an Olympic sport in 2000. Routines are judged on difficulty, execution, time of flight, and horizontal displacement, rewarding gymnasts who stay centred on the bed while performing high, controlled skills. A routine flows without pauses: each skill rebounds directly into the next, and stopping, travelling off-centre, or landing outside the marked area costs marks. Trampoline demands air awareness, timing, and the courage to twist and somersault many metres above the bed, and it is also a training tool for divers, skiers, and other aerial athletes.

History

Modern trampoline developed in the twentieth century as both a sport and a training tool, entering the Olympic Games in 2000.

Rules

Routines contain ten skills, judged on difficulty, execution, time of flight, and horizontal displacement.

Techniques

Somersaults and twists in tucked, piked, and straight positions, with centred, continuous rebounding, are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix bed work, somersault and twist drills, conditioning, and landing practice into foam pits.

Acrobatic Gymnastics Rank 5 · simple · acrobatic gymnastics · advanced

Acrobatic gymnastics is a partner discipline in which pairs and groups perform balances, throws, catches, and tumbling together, with bases supporting tops in human pyramids and dynamic elements. Partnerships compete as men's pairs, women's pairs, mixed pairs, women's groups of three, and men's groups of four, and routines combine a balance routine of held pyramids, a dynamic routine of throws and catches, and a combined routine. Trust and timing define the sport: a top must commit fully to a throw knowing the base will catch, and a base must hold still strength positions while the top balances above. Judging rewards difficulty, execution, and artistry, with deductions for wobbles, falls, and missed catches.

History

Acrobatic gymnastics grew from circus and partner balancing traditions into an organised international sport with world championships.

Rules

Pairs and groups perform balance, dynamic, and combined routines, judged on difficulty, execution, and artistry.

Techniques

Partner balances, throws, catches, and synchronised tumbling are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix partner drills, strength work, tumbling, and full routine runs with the same partners.

Aerobic Gymnastics Rank 6 · compounded · aerobic gymnastics · intermediate

Aerobic gymnastics is a high-energy discipline performed to music, combining aerobic dance patterns with strength, flexibility, and acrobatic elements in routines lasting about eighty seconds. Athletes compete individually, in pairs, trios, and groups, performing continuous movement with no pauses, and routines must show all four element groups: dynamic strength, static strength, jumps and leaps, and balance and flexibility. Judging covers artistry, execution, and difficulty, with deductions for breaks in movement, loss of synchronisation in groups, and form errors. The sport demands cardiovascular fitness, precise timing to the music, and the ability to perform difficult elements while already breathing hard.

History

Aerobic gymnastics grew from fitness aerobics in the late twentieth century into an international competitive sport with world championships.

Rules

Routines last about eighty seconds, show four element groups, and are judged on artistry, execution, and difficulty.

Techniques

Continuous aerobic patterns, strength elements, jumps, leaps, and flexibility elements are the core components.

Training

Sessions mix aerobic conditioning, dance, strength work, and full routine runs to music.

Tumbling Rank 7 · compounded · tumbling gymnastics · intermediate

Tumbling is a gymnastics discipline performed on a long sprung track, with athletes completing passes of linked somersaults and twists, usually eight skills per pass, at high speed. A tumbling pass runs the length of the track, building speed through a round-off and back handsprings into a series of somersaults with twists, and ends with a controlled landing. Judging rewards difficulty, execution, and the ability to maintain speed and form through the whole pass, with deductions for steps, pauses, and landing errors. Tumbling is also a foundation skill for artistic gymnastics, cheerleading, and acrobatic sports, and its athletes are among the fastest and most powerful in gymnastics.

History

Tumbling grew from circus and gymnastics traditions into an organised discipline with world championships alongside trampoline events.

Rules

Passes usually contain eight skills, judged on difficulty and execution, with landing and form deductions.

Techniques

Round-off entries, handspring connections, twisting somersaults, and controlled landings are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix track passes, somersault drills, sprint work, and landing practice.

Double Mini-Trampoline Rank 8 · compounded · trampoline gymnastics · intermediate

Double mini-trampoline is a trampoline discipline performed on a small two-part apparatus: a sloped mounting section and a flat dismount section, from which athletes launch into somersaults and twists before landing on a mat. Each pass has two contacts with the apparatus, a mount skill and a dismount skill, and athletes perform two passes in competition, with the combined score deciding the result. The apparatus is smaller and faster than a full trampoline, so timing is critical: the mount must set up the dismount, and the dismount must land cleanly on the mat. Judging covers difficulty and execution, with deductions for landing steps, falls, and loss of control.

History

Double mini-trampoline developed alongside trampoline gymnastics as a compact apparatus discipline with its own world championships.

Rules

Each pass has a mount and a dismount skill; two passes combine for the score, judged on difficulty and execution.

Techniques

Mount timing, dismount setup, somersault and twist control, and mat landings are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix apparatus passes, somersault drills, and landing practice.

Parkour Rank 9 · compounded · movement art · intermediate

Parkour is a movement discipline in which practitioners, called traceurs, move through urban and natural environments by running, jumping, climbing, vaulting, and rolling, aiming for efficient, fluid passage from one point to another. Training builds strength, coordination, and spatial awareness through vaults, wall runs, precision jumps between surfaces, and rolls that absorb landings, and practitioners progress from ground-level skills to larger obstacles only when technique is solid. Parkour has no standard competition format in its traditional form, though speed and style competitions exist, and its philosophy stresses self-improvement, respect for the environment, and safe progression. Safety comes from progressive training, landing technique, and knowing personal limits, because the obstacles are real and unforgiving.

History

Parkour developed in France in the late twentieth century from military obstacle training and movement practice, spreading worldwide through training communities.

Rules

Traditional parkour has no fixed competition rules; speed and style events use timed courses and judged runs.

Techniques

Vaults, wall runs, precision jumps, rolls, and climbing are the core movement vocabulary.

Training

Sessions mix conditioning, vault and jump drills, landing practice, and progressive obstacle work.

Freerunning Rank 10 · compounded · movement art · intermediate

Freerunning grew from parkour but emphasises creative, expressive movement, adding flips, spins, and stylish tricks to efficient travel, and it is often performed in judged competitions and showcases. Where parkour asks for the fastest efficient line, freerunning asks for the most creative one, blending gymnastics tumbling, tricking, and parkour vaults into flowing runs through urban spaces. Competitions judge difficulty, execution, flow, and creativity, with athletes performing runs through courses of obstacles. Training combines gymnastics, trampoline work, and parkour basics, and safety depends on progressive skill building, crash mats in training, and spotting for new tricks.

History

Freerunning developed from parkour in the early twenty-first century as practitioners added acrobatic tricks and competitive formats.

Rules

Competitions judge difficulty, execution, flow, and creativity over runs through obstacle courses.

Techniques

Flips, spins, vaults, and tumbling links, performed with flow, are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix gymnastics, trampoline, parkour basics, and spotted trick practice.

Cheerleading Rank 11 · compounded · team performance · intermediate

Cheerleading is a team performance sport combining stunts, pyramids, tumbling, jumps, and dance, performed in routines lasting about two and a half minutes to music. Teams build pyramids and perform basket tosses, in which a flyer is thrown into the air and caught, alongside standing and running tumbling, synchronised jumps, and dance sections. Competitive cheerleading is judged on difficulty, execution, synchronisation, and performance, with deductions for falls, drops, and safety violations, and it demands strength, timing, and trust between bases, spotters, and flyers. The sport has grown from sideline support into a major competitive discipline with national and world championships.

History

Cheerleading grew from sideline support in American schools into a competitive international sport with world championships.

Rules

Routines last about two and a half minutes, judged on difficulty, execution, synchronisation, and performance, with safety rules for stunts.

Techniques

Stunts, pyramids, basket tosses, tumbling, jumps, and dance are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix stunt practice, tumbling, dance, conditioning, and full routine runs.

Calisthenics Rank 12 · complex · bodyweight training · beginner

Calisthenics is bodyweight strength training and sport, built on pull-ups, dips, push-ups, squats, and their advanced progressions, and it is practised in parks, gyms, and homes worldwide with little or no equipment. Competitive calisthenics, often called street workout, adds freestyle routines on bars combining dynamic moves such as muscle-ups, swings, and releases with static holds such as the front lever, back lever, human flag, and planche. Training progresses step by step: beginners build basic strength and form, intermediates learn muscle-ups and levers, and advanced athletes combine elements into flowing routines. The sport rewards relative strength, body control, and consistency, and its low cost and accessibility have made it one of the fastest-growing movement practices.

History

Calisthenics descends from ancient bodyweight training and grew into modern street workout culture with international competitions.

Rules

Freestyle competitions judge difficulty, execution, and flow over bar routines; repetition competitions count clean reps.

Techniques

Pull-ups, dips, muscle-ups, levers, flags, and planches form the progression ladder.

Training

Sessions mix basic strength work, skill progressions, and routine practice, with rest for recovery.

Breaking Rank 13 · complex · dance sport · intermediate

Breaking, often called breakdancing, is a dance sport built on toprock, footwork, power moves, and freezes, performed in battles where dancers take turns in rounds to music played by a DJ. Dancers compete one on one or in crews, with judges scoring technique, vocabulary, execution, musicality, and originality, and battles run through rounds until a winner is decided. Power moves such as windmills, flares, and air flares demand gymnastics-level strength and control, while footwork and freezes demand speed, balance, and style. Breaking entered the Olympic programme at the Paris Games in 2024, bringing street dance culture to the world's largest sporting stage.

History

Breaking developed in New York in the 1970s from street dance culture, spreading worldwide and entering the Olympic programme at Paris 2024.

Rules

Battles run in rounds; judges score technique, vocabulary, execution, musicality, and originality.

Techniques

Toprock, footwork, power moves, and freezes form the movement vocabulary.

Training

Sessions mix dance practice, strength and conditioning, power-move drills, and battle practice.

Aerial Silks Rank 14 · complex · aerial arts · advanced

Aerial silks is an aerial art in which performers climb, wrap, and drop from two lengths of fabric suspended from above, combining strength, flexibility, and choreography high above the ground. Performers use wraps and locks to hold positions, create drops that unwind in controlled falls, and link movements into routines set to music, with judging in competition covering technique, artistry, and difficulty. Training builds grip strength, core strength, and flexibility, and progresses from low wraps and climbs to higher drops only when technique is secure. Safety depends on rigging inspected by professionals, crash mats below, and spotting during new skills, because the apparatus hangs high and mistakes have real consequences.

History

Aerial silks developed from circus aerial arts in the late twentieth century into a popular performance and competitive discipline.

Rules

Competitions judge technique, artistry, and difficulty over routines set to music.

Techniques

Climbs, wraps, locks, drops, and poses form the movement vocabulary.

Training

Sessions mix conditioning, flexibility, low-height skill work, and routine practice.

Aerial Hoop Rank 15 · complex · aerial arts · advanced

Aerial hoop, also called lyra, is an aerial art performed on a steel hoop suspended from above, in which performers spin, balance, and pose inside, above, and below the apparatus. Routines combine mounts, spins, balances, and drops, with the hoop's rotation adding difficulty and visual effect, and competition judging covers technique, artistry, and difficulty. Training builds grip strength, core strength, and flexibility, and progresses from low-hoop basics to spinning and dynamic skills only when control is secure. Slacklining Rank 16 · complex · balance sport · beginner

Slacklining is a balance sport performed on a flat webbing line tensioned between two anchors, usually trees, on which practitioners walk, balance, and perform tricks, with the line's bounce and sway adding constant challenge. Lines range from short, low beginner lines to long highlines rigged high above ground with safety harnesses, and tricks include jumps, turns, bounces, and yoga poses on the line. Training builds balance, core strength, and focus, and progresses from assisted walking on short lines to longer lines and tricks only as balance improves. Safety depends on sound anchors, proper tension, tree protection, and harnesses on highlines, because falls from height are serious.

History

Slacklining developed from climbing communities in the late twentieth century, where climbers balanced on webbing during rest days, into a worldwide sport.

Rules

There are no fixed competition rules in traditional slacklining; trickline and highline events use judged and timed formats.

Techniques

Walking, balancing, bouncing, turns, jumps, and yoga poses on the line are the core elements.

Training

Sessions mix balance drills, core work, and progressive line length and height.
