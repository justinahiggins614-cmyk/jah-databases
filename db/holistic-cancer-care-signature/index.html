Exercise During Cancer Treatment JAH-HCCSG-000001 supported for a specific symptom

Signature design: the Signature Care Movement Plan pairs a daily movement menu with a fatigue-first pacing rule, so the patient picks the lightest option on a bad day instead of skipping movement entirely or pushing through exhaustion. The plan's original element is its three-tier day card (green, amber, red) that maps morning fatigue and pain scores to a pre-agreed activity band.

What is claimed: Exercise is claimed by some advocates to fight cancer directly, boost the immune system enough to shrink tumours, and replace drug treatment.

This is an original Signature protocol design, not a tested medical program and not a substitute for a physiotherapist's or oncologist's prescription. Its value, if any, would have to be shown in a supervised pilot with safety and fatigue outcomes measured before any claim is made for it.

What studies actually show: Trials show the strongest, most consistent benefit is for fatigue, physical function, mood, and sleep during treatment. No randomised trial shows exercise alone shrinking tumours or replacing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, or other conventional treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Exercise is generally low risk when it is adapted to the person, but bone metastases, low blood counts, heart or lung problems, dizziness, neuropathy, and recent surgery all change what is safe. Stop and seek care for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, or new severe bone pain, and clear any new program with the oncology team first.

What is claimed: Acupuncture is sometimes claimed to unblock energy channels to destroy cancer, rebalance the body so tumours dissolve, and replace chemotherapy.

What studies actually show: The best-supported use is easing chemotherapy-related nausea and vomiting as an add-on to standard anti-nausea drugs. Evidence for pain relief is mixed and short-term. There is no credible evidence that acupuncture treats cancer itself.

Ginger for Chemotherapy Nausea JAH-HCCSG-000004 mixed

Signature design: the Signature Ginger Diary is an original tracking card that separates acute nausea (the first day after chemotherapy) from delayed nausea (days two to five), because trials suggest any ginger effect, if present, is more likely acute. The card also logs every other anti-nausea medicine taken, so a ginger effect is never mistaken for a drug effect.

What is claimed: Ginger is sometimes claimed to be a natural chemotherapy that kills cancer cells, cleanses the body of treatment toxins, and makes anti-nausea drugs unnecessary.

This is an original Signature tracking design, not a ginger product and not a claim that ginger works. It exists to stop patients and clinicians from being misled by a single good or bad day.

What studies actually show: Laboratory studies show ginger compounds affect cancer cells in dishes, which is not evidence of benefit in people. Human trials show mixed, modest results for nausea only, and no evidence that ginger treats cancer or replaces anti-nausea medicines.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Ginger can increase bleeding risk, interact with blood thinners, and worsen heartburn or mouth irritation. People on anticoagulants, with bleeding disorders, or close to surgery should discuss ginger supplements with their clinician, and ginger should never replace prescribed anti-nausea medicines.

High-Dose Vitamin C JAH-HCCSG-000008 unproven

Signature design: the Signature Ascorbate Evidence Card is an original one-page summary a patient can take to an appointment, listing exactly what is known (nutrient role, investigational infusion trials), what is unknown (survival benefit), and which interactions to ask about. Its original element is the trial-check box, which asks whether any proposed infusion is part of a registered trial with safety monitoring.

What is claimed: High-dose vitamin C is claimed to kill cancer cells selectively, replace chemotherapy, and cure advanced cancer, sometimes on the basis of laboratory studies or individual stories.

This is an original Signature information design, not a vitamin C protocol and not an endorsement of high-dose vitamin C. It exists to help patients ask hard questions before paying for an unproven infusion.

What studies actually show: Laboratory and animal findings have not translated into proven benefit in people. Randomised evidence does not support vitamin C as a cancer treatment, and no survival or tumour-shrinkage benefit is established.

Evidence grade: unproven.

Safety and interactions: High-dose vitamin C can interact with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, because antioxidants may protect tumour cells as well as healthy cells from treatments that work by damaging cancer cells. It can also cause kidney stones, interfere with some laboratory tests, and is dangerous for people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency or kidney disease. Never take high-dose vitamin C during treatment without oncology approval.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Because products are unstandardised, the dose of active constituents is unknown, and people should never delay or replace conventional treatment with Essiac.

Laetrile (Amygdalin) JAH-HCCSG-000010 harmful-interaction risk

Signature design: the Signature Cyanide Warning Card is an original plain-language card listing cyanide poisoning symptoms, the fact that vitamin C worsens the risk, and the instruction to seek emergency care and tell responders about laetrile or apricot kernel use. What is claimed: Laetrile is claimed to be a hidden natural vitamin treatment for cancer, suppressed by medicine, that selectively kills cancer cells while leaving healthy cells untouched.

This is an original Signature safety-information design, not a laetrile product and not an endorsement in any form. It exists to reduce harm among people who encounter laetrile promotion.

What studies actually show: Controlled human study found no anti-tumour benefit from laetrile, and the selective-killing theory is not supported by human pharmacology. Laetrile is disproven as a cancer treatment and carries a real poisoning risk.

Evidence grade: harmful-interaction risk.

Safety and interactions: Laetrile can cause cyanide poisoning, which can be fatal, and the risk rises with vitamin C supplements and with eating apricot kernels. Symptoms include headache, dizziness, confusion, fast breathing, and collapse; anyone with these symptoms after laetrile or kernel use needs emergency care immediately. Laetrile must never be used during or instead of conventional treatment.
