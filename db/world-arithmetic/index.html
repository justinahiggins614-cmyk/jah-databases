What Arithmetic Is

ID: JAH-ARIT-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Arithmetic is the branch of mathematics that deals with numbers and with the operations performed on them: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. It is the oldest and most widely used part of mathematics, and it is the foundation on which all other mathematics is built. Arithmetic answers questions such as how many, how much, how many more, and how many each, and it does so with rules that are exact and checkable. From counting change to computing the trajectory of a spacecraft, arithmetic is the first step, and every more advanced calculation depends on getting the arithmetic right.

History: Arithmetic began with counting, using fingers, tally marks, and knotted cords. Babylonian scribes, around 1800 BCE, kept multiplication tables on clay tablets, and Egyptian scribes multiplied by doubling. The abacus, used in China, Rome, and the Middle East, made fast calculation possible long before written methods. Indian mathematicians developed the decimal place-value system and the column methods for the four operations, and al-Khwarizmi's books of about 825 CE spread them through the Arabic-speaking world. Fibonacci's Liber Abaci of 1202 brought them to Europe, and printed arithmetic books of the 1500s standardised the methods still taught today. Mechanical calculators appeared in the 1600s, and electronic calculators in the 1970s, but the operations themselves have not changed in a thousand years. In the 1600s, John Napier's bones, rods marked with multiplication tables, made multiplication faster, and the slide rule, built on logarithms, was the engineer's calculator for three centuries. Charles Babbage designed a mechanical calculating engine in the 1830s, and the first electronic calculators arrived in the 1970s, putting the four operations in a pocket. Computers now do arithmetic at billions of operations a second, but they do the same four operations, and the methods they use, such as adding in binary with carries, are the column methods adapted to base two. The operations have not changed; only the speed and the notation have.

Key concepts: A number is an idea for counting and measuring. An operation is something done to numbers to get another number. Addition joins amounts: 4 + 3 = 7. Subtraction finds a difference or what remains: 7 - 3 = 4. Multiplication is repeated addition: 4 times 3 means 3 + 3 + 3 + 3. Division shares equally or counts groups: 12 divided by 3 is 4. An inverse operation undoes another: subtraction undoes addition, and division undoes multiplication. The equals sign means the two sides have the same value, and every calculation can be checked by its inverse. The sum is the result of addition, the difference is the result of subtraction, the product is the result of multiplication, and the quotient is the result of division. A fact family is a set of related facts: 3 + 4 = 7, 4 + 3 = 7, 7 - 3 = 4, and 7 - 4 = 3 belong to one family, and 3 times 4 = 12, 4 times 3 = 12, 12 divided by 3 = 4, and 12 divided by 4 = 3 belong to another. The minuend is the number subtracted from, and the subtrahend is the number subtracted. The dividend is the number divided, and the divisor is the number it is divided by.

How it works: Arithmetic works by rules that are applied step by step. Addition and multiplication can be done in any order, which is why 3 + 4 and 4 + 3 both give 7. Subtraction and division depend on order: 7 - 3 is not the same as 3 - 7. Column methods work from right to left, carrying tens in addition and multiplication, and borrowing tens in subtraction. Multiplication is broken into parts: 6 times 47 is 6 times 40 plus 6 times 7. Division asks how many times one number fits into another, and any remainder is what is left over. Every operation is checked by its inverse: a subtraction is checked by adding, and a division is checked by multiplying. Checking is part of arithmetic. An addition is checked by subtracting one addend from the sum, a subtraction by adding the difference to the subtrahend, a multiplication by dividing the product by one factor, and a division by multiplying the quotient by the divisor and adding the remainder. Estimation is the first check: before computing 47 times 38 exactly, note that 50 times 40 is 2,000, so the answer should be near 1,800, and an answer of 18,000 would be plainly wrong. A second check is to compute in a different order: 47 times 38 is the same as 38 times 47, so computing both ways and comparing catches errors.

Worked examples: Addition: 27 + 15 = 42, checked by 42 - 15 = 27. Subtraction: 83 - 29 = 54, checked by 54 + 29 = 83. Multiplication: 8 times 6 is 48, and 12 times 7 is 84. Division: 96 divided by 8 is 12, because 12 times 8 is 96, and 100 divided by 7 is 14 remainder 2, because 14 times 7 is 98 and 100 - 98 is 2. A mixed check: 12 plus 4 is 16, 16 minus 4 is 12, 12 times 4 is 48, and 48 divided by 4 is 12, showing each pair of inverse operations in action. A fact family: 3 + 4 = 7, 4 + 3 = 7, 7 - 3 = 4, and 7 - 4 = 3. Another: 3 times 4 = 12, 4 times 3 = 12, 12 divided by 3 = 4, and 12 divided by 4 = 3. Estimating 47 times 38: 50 times 40 is 2,000, so the exact answer, 1,786, is plausible, while 17,860 would not be.

Real applications: Arithmetic is used in shopping, cooking, travel, work, and managing money. A shopkeeper adds a bill and subtracts to give change; a cook multiplies a recipe and divides it between servings; a driver divides distance by speed to find travel time; a builder measures and cuts; a nurse calculates a dose. In business, arithmetic gives profit, tax, and interest. In science, it is the first step of every measurement and calculation. Computers do arithmetic at enormous speed, but they do the same four operations, and a person who understands them can check a machine's answer. In sport, arithmetic gives scores, averages, and league tables: a batsman's average is runs divided by times out, and a team's points are wins times three plus draws. In science, it combines measurements and counts, and it is the first step of every calculation. In computing, the processor's arithmetic unit does the four operations on binary numbers, and every program depends on it. In daily life, it is the mathematics of splitting a bill, of working out a discount, and of checking that a receipt is right.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is arithmetic the same as mathematics? No: arithmetic is the part of mathematics that works directly with numbers, while mathematics also studies shapes, patterns, chance, and change. Why learn arithmetic by hand when calculators exist? Because understanding the operations lets you estimate, spot errors, and decide what to calculate, which a calculator cannot do for you. What is the most common mistake in arithmetic? Misplacing digits in column work, and forgetting that subtraction and division depend on order. Does arithmetic ever change? The operations do not, but the notation and tools do: Roman numerals made arithmetic hard, while decimal place value made it easy, and the next change may be how machines and people share the work. Which operation should be done first? By convention, multiplication and division before addition and subtraction, and brackets first of all, which is the order of operations. Why are there four operations and not more? Because addition and multiplication, with their inverses subtraction and division, cover the basic ways of combining numbers, and all other operations are built from them. Is long division the only way to divide? No: short division, repeated subtraction, and estimation are all division, and the best method depends on the size of the numbers.

Counting and Natural Numbers

ID: JAH-ARIT-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Counting is the act of matching objects, one by one, to the number words in their fixed order: one, two, three, and so on. The natural numbers, 1, 2, 3, 4, and continuing without end, are the numbers used for counting. Each natural number has a next number, its successor, and every natural number except 1 has a previous number. Counting tells how many things there are, and the last number said is the total. Natural numbers are the first numbers a child learns and the foundation of all arithmetic: every other kind of number is built from them or defined by contrast with them.

History: Counting is older than writing. Tally marks on bone, some tens of thousands of years old, are the earliest records of counting, and finger counting is still used everywhere. Different cultures counted in different bases: the Babylonians used base 60, the Maya used base 20, and most of the world now uses base 10, probably because of ten fingers. The natural numbers were studied as objects in their own right by the Greeks, who asked which numbers are prime and which are perfect, and by Indian mathematicians, who gave them the decimal place-value system. In the 1800s, Giuseppe Peano set out axioms for the natural numbers, defining them by 1 and the successor operation, and in the 1870s Georg Cantor showed that the natural numbers form the smallest infinity, the countable infinity against which all others are measured. Roman numerals, used across Europe for a thousand years, made arithmetic hard: adding XXVII and XIV required converting to another system or using a counting board. The abacus, a frame of beads, was the standard calculating tool in China, Rome, and the Middle East, and it is still used in some places today. In the 1600s, John Napier invented logarithms and a calculating device of rods, and in the 1800s Giuseppe Peano set out axioms for the natural numbers, defining them by 1 and the successor operation. In the 1900s, computers adopted binary, a place-value system with only the digits 0 and 1, showing that the idea of place value works in any base, not just ten.

Key concepts: A natural number is a counting number: 1, 2, 3, and so on. Some conventions include 0; this record says so when it matters. The successor of a number is the next one: the successor of 7 is 8. Cardinality is how many a set has: a set with 5 elements has cardinality 5. Ordinality is position in an order: first, second, third. One-to-one correspondence is matching each object to exactly one number word, which is what makes a count correct. Even numbers are the natural numbers divisible by 2, and odd numbers are the rest. A multiple of a number is that number times a natural number. The successor of a number is the next one: the successor of 7 is 8, and every natural number has one. The predecessor is the one before: the predecessor of 8 is 7, and every natural number except 1 has one. Whole numbers are the natural numbers together with 0. Ordinal numbers say position, first, second, third, while cardinal numbers say how many. A counting system with a different base works the same way: in binary, 101 means 1 times 4 plus 0 times 2 plus 1, which is 5.

How it works: Counting works by order and by matching. Say the number words in order, touch each object once, and the last word is the total. If an object is missed or counted twice, the count is wrong, which is why counting in groups helps: count by twos, fives, or tens, and add the groups. To add natural numbers by counting, start at one number and count on by the other: 7 + 5 means start at 7 and count on five steps to 12. To compare two natural numbers, count them: the one reached later is bigger, and in decimal notation the longer number is bigger, or, if the lengths match, the one with the larger leftmost differing digit. Skip counting builds the multiplication tables: counting by 3s gives 3, 6, 9, 12, 15. Grouping makes counting fast and reliable. To count a large pile, make groups of ten, then groups of ten groups, and count the groups: 3 hundreds, 4 tens, and 7 ones is 347. To compare two counts, compare the largest groups first: more hundreds means a bigger number, whatever the smaller groups hold. To estimate a count, count a small part and scale up: if one row of seats holds 12 and there are 20 rows, the hall holds about 240. To check a count, count again in a different grouping: counting by twos and then by fives should give the same total.

Worked examples: Counting the numbers from 1 to 20 gives 20 numbers, of which 10 are even and 10 are odd. The sum of the numbers from 1 to 20 is 210: pair 1 with 20, 2 with 19, and so on to make ten pairs of 21, and 10 times 21 is 210. Counting by 5s from 0 gives 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, and 7 counted on by 5 reaches 12. The successor of 99 is 100, and the predecessor of 100 is 99. There are 31 days in January, and counting them gives 31. The multiples of 7 below 50 are 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, and 49, which is seven multiples. The binary number 101 is 5, since 1 times 4 plus 0 times 2 plus 1 is 5. A hall with 20 rows of 12 seats holds about 240 seats, since 20 times 12 is 240. Counting 1,000 dots by grouping them in tens gives 100 groups, and grouping those in tens gives 10 groups of 100.

Real applications: Counting is used wherever things must be tallied: a census counts people, a shop counts stock, a scoreboard counts points, and a ticket counts passengers. Natural numbers label things in order: house numbers, bus routes, chapters, and floors. They measure discrete quantities: how many eggs, how many days, how many votes. In computing, natural numbers count steps, index lists, and address memory. In science, they count atoms, cells, and species. Every measurement that comes in whole units, from the number of pages in a book to the number of goals in a match, is a count of natural numbers. Barcodes and product codes use counting with a check digit: the last digit is computed from the others, so a mistyped digit is caught. The ISBN of a book has a check digit computed from the other digits, which catches most errors in copying. In computing, counting in binary is how a machine counts memory, files, and steps. In science, counting is how samples are measured: the number of cells in a sample, or the number of stars in a survey, is a count, and its accuracy depends on the counting method.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is 0 a natural number? It depends on convention: many mathematicians include 0, while school counting starts at 1. Either is fine as long as it is stated. Is there a largest natural number? No: every natural number has a successor, so the natural numbers go on without end. Why count in tens? Because we have ten fingers, though other bases have been used, such as base 60 in Babylon and base 2 in computers. Can you count everything? Only things that come in separate items; continuous things such as water are measured, not counted. Are the natural numbers infinite? Yes, in the sense that there is no largest one, and they form the smallest infinity, called countable infinity. Why do different cultures count differently? Because counting grew from local needs and materials: fingers gave base ten, while the Babylonians used base sixty, perhaps from astronomy, and the Maya used base twenty, perhaps from fingers and toes. Is counting the same as measuring? No: counting tallies separate items, while measuring compares a continuous quantity with a unit, though the result of a measurement is often a count of units. Can a computer count? Yes, in binary, and it counts far faster than a person, but it follows the same idea: matching items to numbers in order.

Addition

Definition: Addition is the operation that joins two numbers into a total: 4 + 3 = 7. The numbers being added are called addends, and the result is called the sum. Addition answers questions such as how many altogether, how many more, and what is the total. It is commutative, meaning the order does not matter: 4 + 3 and 3 + 4 both give 7. It is associative, meaning the grouping does not matter: (2 + 3) + 4 and 2 + (3 + 4) both give 9. Adding zero changes nothing, so zero is the identity element for addition. Addition is the first operation learned, and it underlies the other three.

History: Addition is the oldest operation: joining piles of stones or tallies is addition, and every culture has done it. Babylonian and Egyptian scribes added in their own notations, and the abacus made addition fast by moving beads. The plus sign, +, appeared in German arithmetic books of the late 1400s, and the column method for adding large numbers, working right to left and carrying tens, was developed with the Hindu-Arabic numeral system and spread to Europe through Fibonacci's Liber Abaci of 1202. Subtraction

ID: JAH-ARIT-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Subtraction is the operation that finds a difference or what remains: 7 - 3 = 4. The number being subtracted from is called the minuend, the number subtracted is the subtrahend, and the result is the difference. Subtraction answers questions such as how many are left, how many more are needed, and how much bigger is one number than another. It is the inverse of addition: if 4 + 3 = 7, then 7 - 3 = 4. Unlike addition, subtraction depends on order: 7 - 3 is not the same as 3 - 7. Subtracting zero changes nothing, and subtracting a number from itself gives zero.

History: Subtraction is as old as addition: taking stones from a pile is subtraction. Egyptian and Babylonian scribes subtracted in their notations, and the abacus made it fast. The minus sign, -, appeared with the plus sign in German arithmetic books of the late 1400s. The column method for subtraction, working right to left and borrowing a ten when a digit is too small, developed with the Hindu-Arabic numeral system and was taught in European schools from the 1500s. Negative numbers, the result of subtracting a larger number from a smaller one, were used in China and India centuries before Europe accepted them, and they were fully accepted in the 1600s. Computers subtract by adding complements, a method that turns subtraction into addition of a transformed number. Roman numerals used subtractive notation, writing 4 as IV, that is, 5 minus 1, and 9 as IX, so subtraction was built into the way numbers were written, though arithmetic in Roman numerals was so awkward that most calculation was done on an abacus. Two methods for written subtraction competed for centuries: borrowing, where a ten is taken from the next column, and equal additions, the Austrian method, where the same amount is added to both numbers to avoid borrowing. In the 1900s, shopkeepers used counting up to make change: for a purchase of 3.40 paid with 5, they counted 3.40, then 3.50, then 4, then 5, giving change of 1.60. Mechanical calculators subtracted by adding complements, turning subtraction into addition of a transformed number, and computers still do this today.

Definition: A power is a number multiplied by itself a certain number of times: 2 to the power 3, written 2^3, means 2 times 2 times 2, which is 8. The number being multiplied is the base, and the number of times it is multiplied is the exponent, also called the index. Powers are a shorthand for repeated multiplication, and they grow very fast: 2 to the power 10 is 1,024, and 2 to the power 20 is 1,048,576. Powers appear in area, where a square of side 5 has area 5 squared, in volume, where a cube of side 3 has volume 3 cubed, and in science, where very large and very small numbers are written as powers of ten.

History: Powers were used by the Greeks, who spoke of a number squared and cubed, and by Archimedes, who in the 200s BCE worked out how to multiply powers of ten in his Sand Reckoner, counting the grains of sand that would fill the universe. Indian and Islamic mathematicians developed the rules for exponents, and the notation with a raised exponent was introduced by Descartes in 1637. Negative and fractional exponents were understood in the 1600s, with John Wallis and Isaac Newton extending the rules. In the 1700s, Euler studied the exponential function, and in the 1900s powers became central to computing, where binary powers describe memory, and to science, where scientific notation writes numbers such as the speed of light as 3 times 10 to the power 8. The caret notation 2^3, used in computing, comes from the days when a keyboard had no way to write a raised exponent. Logarithms, invented by John Napier in 1614, are exponents in disguise: a logarithm is the power to which a base must be raised, and the slide rule, invented by William Oughtred in 1622, computed by adding logarithms. In 1683, Jacob Bernoulli studied compound interest and found the number e, the limit of (1 + 1/n) to the power n as n grows, which is about 2.71828. In 1965, Gordon Moore observed that the number of transistors on a chip doubled about every two years, a pattern called Moore's law, which is exponential growth in the world of computing. Scientific notation, writing the speed of light as 3 times 10 to the power 8 metres per second, is the standard way of handling the very large and very small.

Definition: A root is the inverse of a power: the square root of 25 is 5, because 5 squared is 25, and the cube root of 27 is 3, because 3 cubed is 27. Every positive number has two square roots, a positive and a negative one, though the square root sign on its own means the positive one. Roots answer questions such as what length gives this area, and what number, raised to this power, gives that result. They are the inverse operation to powers, in the same way that subtraction is the inverse of addition.

Order of Operations

ID: JAH-ARIT-000013 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: The order of operations is the agreed sequence in which the parts of a calculation are carried out: brackets first, then powers, then multiplication and division, then addition and subtraction. Without it, an expression such as 2 + 3 times 4 would be ambiguous: it could be read as 2 + 12 = 14 or as 5 times 4 = 20. The convention settles it: multiplication comes before addition, so the answer is 14. The order is a convention, not a law of nature, but it is shared worldwide, so everyone reading the same expression gets the same answer. Brackets override the order, letting the writer say exactly what is meant.

History: The order of operations grew with algebraic notation. In the 1500s and 1600s, as symbols for powers, multiplication, and brackets were introduced, mathematicians needed rules for reading expressions, and the conventions settled gradually: powers before multiplication, multiplication before addition, and brackets to override. The mnemonic BODMAS, brackets, orders, division, multiplication, addition, subtraction, and its American cousin PEMDAS, parentheses, exponents, multiplication, division, addition, subtraction, became standard teaching aids in the 1900s, though both are sometimes misunderstood, since division and multiplication have equal priority and are done left to right, as do addition and subtraction. Calculators and computers follow the same rules, which is why a calculator gives 14 for 2 + 3 times 4. The symbols themselves have histories: the division sign, a line with a dot above and below, was introduced by Johann Rahn in 1659, and the obelus is still used in some countries where others use a slash. The vinculum, the bar of a fraction, is older still, and it acts as brackets for the numerator and denominator. Different countries teach the order with different mnemonics, BODMAS in Britain, PEMDAS in America, and BEDMAS in Canada, and the differences between them have caused arguments, since each mnemonic hides the fact that multiplication and division share a level. In the 1900s, the order became a matter for machines as well as people: programming languages encode it in their grammars, and a formula typed into a spreadsheet follows the same rules as one written on paper.

Key concepts: Brackets, also called parentheses, group parts of an expression and are always done first. Orders, or exponents, are powers and roots, done after brackets. Division and multiplication have equal priority and are done from left to right. Addition and subtraction have equal priority and are done from left to right. A mnemonic such as BODMAS or PEMDAS helps remember the order, but it must be understood as groups of equal priority, not as a strict sequence. The vinculum, the bar of a fraction, acts like brackets for the numerator and denominator. Nested brackets are brackets inside brackets, and they are worked from the innermost out: in 2 times [3 + (4 - 1)], first compute 4 - 1 = 3, then 3 + 3 = 6, then 2 times 6 = 12. Implied multiplication is multiplication shown by writing things next to each other, as in 2(3 + 4), and it is a source of ambiguity in expressions such as 6 divided by 2(1 + 2), which some readers take as 9 and others as 1. A unary minus is a minus sign applied to a single number, as in -3, and it binds differently in different systems, which is why -2 squared is -4 in some conventions and 4 in others. A well-formed expression is one whose meaning is clear from the rules alone, without guesswork.

Prime Numbers

ID: JAH-ARIT-000014 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A prime number is a natural number greater than 1 that has exactly two divisors: 1 and itself. The first primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, and 29. The number 2 is the only even prime, since every other even number is divisible by 2. A number greater than 1 that is not prime is called composite: 4, 6, 8, 9, and 10 are composite. The number 1 is neither prime nor composite, since it has only one divisor. Primes are the building blocks of the natural numbers: every natural number greater than 1 can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from order.

History: Primes were studied by the Greeks. Euclid, around 300 BCE, proved that there are infinitely many primes, and the sieve of Eratosthenes, from about the same time, finds primes by crossing out multiples. In the 1600s, Fermat studied primes and found results that are still used, and in the 1800s Gauss and others studied how primes are distributed. The prime number theorem, proved in 1896, says the number of primes below n is about n divided by the natural logarithm of n. In the 1970s, the RSA system made primes the basis of internet security, and the search for ever-larger primes became a public effort: the largest known prime, as of recent years, is a Mersenne prime with tens of millions of digits, found by a distributed computing project. In 1644, Marin Mersenne listed the primes of the form 2 to the power p minus 1, and the search for such primes continues today. In 1737, Euler proved that the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges, a second proof, after Euclid's, that there are infinitely many primes.
