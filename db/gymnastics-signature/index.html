Men's Artistic Gymnastics Rank 1 · simple · artistic gymnastics · advanced

Six apparatus, one all-around champion: men's artistic gymnastics has been Olympic since 1896 and remains the strength-and-flight showcase of the sport. Floor brings tumbling and balance, pommel horse brings unbroken swinging, rings bring still strength, vault brings one explosive effort, parallel bars bring swing and hold, and horizontal bar brings release moves above the bar. Scoring blends difficulty with execution, and judges deduct for every form break and landing step. The athletes who win are strong, flexible, and calm in the air, and they make skills that took years to learn look effortless.

History

The men's programme dates to 1896 and the first modern Games, with six apparatus as its enduring format.

Rules

Difficulty plus execution, minus deductions for form breaks, steps, and falls, decides scores. All-around totals combine six apparatus.

Techniques

Swing, hold, tumble, release, and land: each apparatus has its own vocabulary, and stuck landings protect scores.

Training

Apparatus sessions, strength work, flexibility, and landing practice fill the training week.

print("All-around example")

    print("%s: %.1f" % (a, s))
print("All-around total: %.1f" % sum(SCORES))
print("Average per apparatus: %.2f" % (sum(SCORES) / len(SCORES)))

Women's Artistic Gymnastics Rank 2 · simple · artistic gymnastics · advanced

Four apparatus, each with its own character: vault for power, uneven bars for swing and flight, beam for balance under pressure, and floor for tumbling joined to dance. Women's Olympic gymnastics dates to 1928, and its modern form blends athletic difficulty with performance. Beam is the nerve test: acrobatics on a ten-centimetre-wide surface, where a wobble costs marks and a fall costs far more. Floor asks for the same tumbling power plus artistry to music. Scoring rewards difficulty executed cleanly, and the all-around champion must be complete across all four.

History

The women's programme entered the Olympics in 1928 and grew into the four-apparatus format used today.

Rules

Difficulty plus execution, minus deductions, decides scores. All-around totals combine four apparatus.

Techniques

Power on vault, flight on bars, balance on beam, and tumbling with dance on floor define the programme.

Training

Apparatus work, dance, strength, flexibility, and landing practice fill the training week.

Rhythmic Gymnastics Rank 3 · simple · rhythmic gymnastics · advanced

Dance, flexibility, and apparatus handling set to music: rhythmic gymnastics became Olympic in 1984 and is the most expressive branch of the sport. Each routine weaves body elements, leaps, balances, pivots, and flexibility, together with continuous work with hoop, ball, clubs, ribbon, or rope. The apparatus must never rest: it rolls, bounces, juggles, draws, and flies while the gymnast moves. Judging rewards difficulty, artistry, and clean execution, and drops cost dearly. Group routines add synchronised exchanges between five gymnasts, where timing is everything.

History

The discipline grew from dance and expressive movement and reached the Olympics in 1984.

Rules

Difficulty, artistry, and execution are judged. Drops and control losses are deducted; music accompanies every routine.

Techniques

Body elements plus apparatus mastery define the sport. The apparatus must keep moving through the whole routine.

Training

Dance, flexibility, apparatus drills, and routine runs fill the training week.

print("After a 0.5 drop deduction: %.1f" % (DIFFICULTY + ARTISTRY + EXECUTION - 0.5))

Trampoline Rank 4 · simple · trampoline gymnastics · intermediate

Ten skills, one bouncing bed, and no pauses: trampoline gymnastics reached the Olympics in 2000 and rewards height, control, and centred flight. Routines string somersaults and twists in tucked, piked, and straight shapes, each rebounding straight into the next. Judging covers difficulty, execution, time of flight, and how well the gymnast stays centred; drifting across the bed or stopping costs marks. The height is the thrill and the test: skills happen metres above the bed, and air awareness decides whether a twist finishes cleanly or not. Other aerial sports use trampoline training for exactly this reason.

History

Trampoline grew from a training device into an Olympic sport in 2000, keeping its ten-skill routine format.

Rules

Ten skills per routine, judged on difficulty, execution, flight time, and centred displacement.

Techniques

Continuous rebounding, clean shapes, and centred landings define good routines. Pauses and drift cost marks.

Training

Bed work, shape drills, conditioning, and pit landings build the routine.

Acrobatic Gymnastics Rank 5 · simple · acrobatic gymnastics · advanced

Gymnastics as teamwork: acrobatic gymnastics builds human pyramids, throws, and catches from partnerships of two, three, or four. Pairs and groups perform balance routines of held structures, dynamic routines of throws and somersault catches, and combined routines mixing both. The sport runs on trust: a top commits to a throw because the base will be there, and a base holds a strength position while a partner balances above. Timing is everything, since a catch missed by a fraction fails the element. Judging rewards difficulty, execution, and artistry, and the best partnerships make extraordinary teamwork look calm.

History

The discipline grew from partner balancing and circus traditions into international competition.

Rules

Balance, dynamic, and combined routines are judged on difficulty, execution, and artistry.

Techniques

Holds, throws, catches, and synchronised tumbling define the sport. Partnerships train together for years.

Training

Partner drills, strength work, and routine runs with fixed partners build the trust the sport needs.

Working demo program

print("Average routine score: %.2f" % ((BALANCE + DYNAMIC + COMBINED) / 3.0))

Aerobic Gymnastics Rank 6 · compounded · aerobic gymnastics · intermediate

Eighty seconds of non-stop movement to music: aerobic gymnastics mixes dance patterns with strength holds, jumps, leaps, and flexibility, and never lets the performer rest. Individuals, pairs, trios, and groups all perform, and every routine must cover four element groups: dynamic strength, static strength, jumps and leaps, and balance with flexibility. Judging rewards artistry, execution, and difficulty, and punishes pauses, synchronisation losses, and form breaks. The fitness demand is extreme, because difficult elements must be performed while the athlete is already breathing hard. Timing to the music ties the whole routine together.

History

The discipline grew from fitness aerobics into international competition in the late twentieth century.

Rules

About eighty seconds, four element groups, and continuous movement define routines. Pauses are deducted.

Techniques

Dance patterns plus strength, jump, and flexibility elements, all timed to music, make up routines.

Training

Conditioning, dance, strength, and full routine runs build the fitness and timing the sport demands.

Tumbling Rank 7 · compounded · tumbling gymnastics · intermediate

A sprint down a sprung track, then eight linked somersaults and twists without a pause: tumbling is speed turned into flight. Passes build through a round-off and handsprings into twisting somersaults, finishing with a controlled landing at the end of the track. Judging rewards difficulty and execution, and above all the maintenance of speed and form through the whole pass; a pause or a stumble breaks the run. Tumbling underpins floor work, cheerleading, and acrobatic sports, and its athletes combine sprint speed with air awareness in a way no other gymnastics discipline quite matches.

History

The discipline grew from circus tumbling into organised competition, run alongside trampoline championships.

Rules

Eight-skill passes are judged on difficulty and execution. Pauses and landing errors are deducted.

Techniques

Speed into the pass, linked twisting somersaults, and a stuck landing define good tumbling.

Training

Track passes, shape drills, sprint work, and landing practice build the pass.

Double Mini-Trampoline Rank 8 · compounded · trampoline gymnastics · intermediate

Two contacts, two skills, one landing: double mini-trampoline uses a small two-part apparatus, a sloped mount and a flat dismount section, to launch athletes into somersaults and twists. Each pass pairs a mount skill with a dismount skill, and two passes make a competition score. The apparatus is quick and unforgiving; the mount must set the dismount perfectly, or the landing suffers. Judging covers difficulty and execution, with landing steps and losses of control deducted. It is the shortest, sharpest trampoline discipline, and it rewards precise timing above all.

History

The discipline developed with trampoline gymnastics as a compact two-contact apparatus event.

Rules

Two passes, each a mount plus a dismount, combine for the score. Difficulty and execution are judged.

Techniques

The mount sets the dismount; timing and clean mat landings decide passes.

Training

Apparatus passes, shape drills, and landing practice build the event.

Working demo program

print("Combined: %.1f" % (PASS1 + PASS2))
print("Average pass: %.2f" % ((PASS1 + PASS2) / 2.0))

Parkour Rank 9 · compounded · movement art · intermediate

Run, jump, vault, climb, roll: parkour is efficient movement through real environments, from city walls to natural rock. Practitioners train vaults, wall runs, precision jumps, and landing rolls, progressing to bigger obstacles only when technique is sound. Traditional parkour has no fixed competition; its measure is fluid, efficient passage and personal progress, though speed and style events now exist. The philosophy matters as much as the movement: respect the environment, train progressively, and know your limits. Safety is built into the method, because the obstacles are concrete, stone, and steel.

History

The discipline developed in France from obstacle training and movement practice, spreading through open training communities.

Rules

No fixed traditional competition exists; speed and style events add timed and judged formats.

Techniques

Vaults, precision jumps, wall runs, and rolls form the vocabulary. Progression is gradual and technique-led.

Training

Conditioning, drills, and progressive obstacle work build safe, efficient movement.

Freerunning Rank 10 · compounded · movement art · intermediate

Parkour's creative sibling: freerunning keeps the movement vocabulary but asks for expression, not just efficiency. Flips, spins, and stylish tricks join vaults and jumps into flowing runs, and competitions judge difficulty, execution, flow, and creativity. A freerunning run is a performance as much as a route, shaped by the athlete's style. Training blends gymnastics, trampoline, and parkour basics, with mats and spotting for new tricks. The line between parkour and freerunning is a matter of emphasis: efficiency on one side, creativity on the other, with most practitioners training both.

History

Freerunning grew from parkour as athletes added tricks and competition formats in the early twenty-first century.

Rules

Judged runs score difficulty, execution, flow, and creativity.

Techniques

Acrobatic tricks blended with parkour movement, performed fluently, define the discipline.

Training

Gymnastics, trampoline, and spotted trick practice build freerunning safely.

Cheerleading Rank 11 · compounded · team performance · intermediate

Two and a half minutes, one team, and a routine of stunts, pyramids, tumbling, and dance: competitive cheerleading has grown far beyond its sideline origins. Bases launch flyers into basket tosses and catch them; pyramids stack athletes into structures; tumbling and jumps add power; dance ties it together to music. Judging rewards difficulty, execution, synchronisation, and performance, and safety violations cost heavily. The sport runs on trust and timing: every stunt depends on bases, flyers, and spotters moving as one. It is gymnastics, acrobatics, and dance performed as a team.

History

The sport grew from school sidelines into international competition with its own championships.

Rules

About two and a half minutes, judged on difficulty, execution, synchronisation, and performance, with strict stunt safety rules.

Techniques

Stunts, pyramids, tosses, tumbling, and dance combine into routines. Timing and trust are essential.

Training

Stunt drills, tumbling, dance, and routine runs fill the training week.

Calisthenics Rank 12 · complex · bodyweight training · beginner

Strength using only your own body: calisthenics starts with pull-ups, dips, push-ups, and squats, and progresses to muscle-ups, levers, flags, and planches. Competitive street workout turns those elements into freestyle bar routines, mixing dynamic releases with static holds. Progression is the method: master the basics, then the next step, then the next, with form protected at every stage. No expensive equipment is needed, which is why parks worldwide have become training grounds. The sport rewards patience and consistency more than any single talent; the athletes who progress are the ones who train steadily for years.

History

Bodyweight training is ancient; modern street workout turned it into organised competition in parks worldwide.

Rules

Freestyle routines are judged on difficulty, execution, and flow. Repetition events count clean form reps.

Techniques

Basics first, then muscle-ups, levers, flags, and planches. Each step requires the one before it.

Training

Strength work, progressions, and routine practice, with recovery built in, drive progress.

Working demo program

Breaking Rank 13 · complex · dance sport · intermediate

Toprock, footwork, power moves, and freezes, traded round for round in battles: breaking is dance as a contest, judged on technique, vocabulary, execution, musicality, and originality. Power moves like windmills and flares need gymnastics strength; footwork needs speed and style; freezes need balance held in impossible shapes. Battles run one on one or crew against crew, with a DJ setting the music and judges deciding each round. Breaking reached the Olympic programme at Paris in 2024, carrying street dance culture onto the largest stage. It remains, at heart, a circle of dancers testing each other.

History

Breaking grew from 1970s New York street dance into a global culture and Olympic sport at Paris 2024.

Rules

Round-based battles are judged on technique, vocabulary, execution, musicality, and originality.

Techniques

Toprock opens, footwork builds, power moves peak, and freezes punctuate. Musicality ties them together.

Training

Dance practice, conditioning, power drills, and battles build the dancer.

Aerial Silks Rank 14 · complex · aerial arts · advanced

Two lengths of fabric, hanging from above, and a performer climbing, wrapping, and dropping through them: aerial silks blends strength, flexibility, and choreography in the air. Wraps and locks hold positions; controlled drops unwind them in falls that stop exactly on cue. Routines set to music link climbs, poses, and drops, and competition judging covers technique, artistry, and difficulty. Training starts low, building grip, core strength, and flexibility, and rises only when technique is secure. Rigging, mats, and spotting are not optional extras; they are the conditions that make the art possible.

History

Silks grew from circus aerial arts into a standalone performance and competition discipline.

Rules

Technique, artistry, and difficulty are judged over music routines.

Techniques

Wraps hold, locks secure, drops release, and poses display. Control at height is everything.

Training

Conditioning, flexibility, and low-height drills precede high work. Rigging is professionally inspected.

Aerial Hoop Rank 15 · complex · aerial arts · advanced

A steel circle hanging in the air, and a performer spinning, balancing, and posing within it: aerial hoop, or lyra, is sculpture in motion. Routines mix mounts, spins, balances, and drops, with the hoop's rotation adding both difficulty and beauty. Slacklining Rank 16 · complex · balance sport · beginner

A flat line between two trees, tensioned and trembling, and a person walking it: slacklining is balance training turned into a sport. The line bounces and sways, so every step is a correction, and tricks add jumps, turns, bounces, and yoga poses to the walk. Lines grow with skill, from short low lines to highlines rigged far above ground with harnesses. Training builds balance, core strength, and deep focus; progress is measured in steadier steps, not faster ones. Anchors, tension, tree protection, and highline harnesses are the safety basics, and they are never skipped.

History

The sport grew from climbers balancing on webbing into a worldwide balance discipline.

Rules

Traditional slacklining has no fixed rules; trickline and highline events add judged formats.

Techniques

Steady walking, controlled bouncing, turns, and poses define the sport. Focus matters as much as strength.

Training

Balance drills, core work, and gradual increases in length and height build skill safely.

Working demo program
