Build a Ladder-Back Chair

JAH-BUILDS-000001 · ladder rank 2 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. A ladder-back chair has two rear posts, horizontal slats, and a woven or wooden seat. Step 1: Cut two rear posts 42 inches long and two front legs 18 inches, all from 2x2 hardwood. Step 2: Cut three slats 18 inches long and two side stretchers. Step 3: Drill mortises in the posts and tenons on the slats. Step 4: Glue the back assembly first, then join the front legs with side stretchers. Step 5: Fit the seat — wood, rush, or woven cord. Step 6: Sand everything to 220 grit and finish with oil or polyurethane. Let glue cure 24 hours before sitting.

Build a Windsor Chair

JAH-BUILDS-000002 · ladder rank 3 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Windsor chairs use turned spindles socketed into a sculpted seat. Step 1: Cut and shape the seat from 2-inch thick pine, 20 inches wide. Step 2: Turn or buy 6 to 8 spindles, about 20 inches long. Step 3: Drill angled holes through the seat for the legs and spindles. Step 4: Drive the legs in with glue, wedging the tenons. Step 5: Fit spindles between seat and top rail. Step 6: Carve the seat saddle for comfort. Step 7: Paint — traditional Windsors are painted, which hides mixed woods.

Build a Folding Chair

JAH-BUILDS-000003 · ladder rank 4 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. A folding chair needs pivoting front and back frames. Step 1: Build two rectangular frames from 1x3 hardwood — front 18x17 inches, back 18x30. Step 2: Drill pivot holes at the crossing point of each side. Step 3: Join the frames with bolts, washers, and lock nuts — tight enough to hold, loose enough to pivot. Step 4: Add a plywood or slatted seat that hinges to the front frame. Step 5: Add a backrest slat to the rear frame. Step 6: Test the fold and sand all edges round.

Build a Farmhouse Table

JAH-BUILDS-000004 · ladder rank 5 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. A farmhouse table is a thick top on sturdy legs with a lower stretcher. Step 1: Glue up the top from 2x8 boards to 36x72 inches; alternate grain direction. Step 2: Cut four 4x4 legs to 29 inches. Step 3: Build two leg assemblies with aprons, then join them with a long stretcher. Step 4: Attach the top with figure-8 fasteners so it can expand. Step 5: Fill knots, sand to 150 grit, and finish with hardwax oil. Standard dining height is 30 inches including the top.

Build a Trestle Table

JAH-BUILDS-000005 · ladder rank 6 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Trestle tables rest the top on two A-frame trestles. Step 1: Cut the top boards to length — 72 inches for six seats. Step 2: Build two trestles: two splayed legs, a crossbar, and a center post each. Step 3: Join the trestles with a long stretcher beam. Step 4: Peg or bolt all joints — drawbore pegs pull joints tight. Step 5: Set the top on the trestles and fasten from below. The design knocks down flat for moving.

Build a Wooden Stool

JAH-BUILDS-000007 · ladder rank 1 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. A three-legged stool is the simplest seat to build and never wobbles. Step 1: Cut a round or square seat 12 inches across from 1.5-inch stock. Step 2: Cut three legs 18 inches long. Step 3: Drill three angled holes in the seat, splayed outward about 10 degrees. Step 4: Glue and wedge the legs in. Step 5: Add stretchers between the legs for strength. Step 6: Sand and finish. Three legs always find level; four legs need perfect length matching.

Build a Cutting Board

JAH-BUILDS-000011 · ladder rank 11 · signature-generated-completion

Build a Wooden Mallet

JAH-BUILDS-000012 · ladder rank 12 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. A joiner's mallet is head plus handle. Step 1: Cut the head from dense hardwood — 4x4x6 inches of beech or maple. Step 2: Cut the handle 12 inches long, shaped to fit the hand. Step 3: Cut a tapered mortise through the head. Step 4: Shape the handle tenon to match and drive it through with glue. Step 5: Wedge the tenon from the top. Step 6: Chamfer all edges so the mallet is comfortable. A wood mallet won't damage chisel handles like steel does.
