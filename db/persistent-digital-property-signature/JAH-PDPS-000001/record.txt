DESCRIPTIONS
In this design the property record is a deed kept in a chest: the chest holds the deed, the key is held by the owner, and the registry holds a copy of the lock. No deed can be changed without the key, and no key can be copied without the registry knowing. The design suits properties whose owners want their title to be as solid as paper. This is a Signature design-study: an invented proposal for how the Persistent Digital Property Ecosystem could be arranged, labeled as a design throughout and never presented as established fact. The design works through 3 parts: Deed chest; Owner's key; Registry lock. Each part is named so the design can be read, judged, and changed. The design is written for a reader who wants to imagine a different way the work could be done, and for a builder who wants a starting point, not a finished system. Nothing in this record claims the design is built, tested, or deployed; it is a design, and it says so wherever it appears.

MATH PROOF
The mathematical reasoning is plain arithmetic on the design's own figures. The figures are: chests = 240; deeds_changed = 0; keys_held = 240; locks_copied = 240. Adding them in one order and adding them again in a different order gives the same total, because addition does not depend on order — a fact the demo in this record checks by computing the total twice, in two different ways, and comparing the two fingerprints. The fingerprint is the SHA-256 digest, a standard fingerprint of a piece of text: the same text always gives the same fingerprint. The demo prints both fingerprints and whether they match; the program recomputes the same total and the same fingerprint from the same figures. The arithmetic is machine-checked by the demo; the design claim is stated, not machine-checked.
total = sum of all figures; digest = SHA-256 of the total as text
The equation says: add all of the design's figures together to get the total, then take the SHA-256 fingerprint of that total written as text. The demo computes the total twice, in two different orders, and the two fingerprints must be identical.

WORKFLOW
Problem: The design answers a question: A registry where every deed is kept in a chest with its key. The question matters because the usual arrangement of the work hides something — a cost, a risk, a pleasure, a fairness — that the design brings into the open. The design does not claim to be the only answer, or the best answer; it claims to be one answer, clearly drawn, so the reader can compare it with the usual way and decide what is worth keeping.
Solution: The design is built in 4 steps, in order. step 1: Put each deed in its chest; step 2: Give the key to the owner; step 3: Keep the lock's copy with the registry; step 4: Open the chest only with key and lock together. Each step is a choice the designer made, and each choice is written so the reader can disagree with it and change it. The parts of the design are: Deed chest; Owner's key; Registry lock. A reader who follows the steps will arrive at the same design, and a reader who changes a step will arrive at a different design — which is the point of a design-study: it is a place to start, not a place to stop.
Answer: What the design delivers is a clear picture: a The Deed Chest that can be imagined, discussed, and improved. The figures that describe this design are: chests = 240; deeds_changed = 0; keys_held = 240; locks_copied = 240. Those numbers are the design's own checkable figures, and the demo in this record recomputes them from the figures alone, so any reader can verify them without trusting the record's word. A design whose figures check out is a design whose arithmetic is honest, even though the design itself remains a proposal.
Applies: The Deed Chest applies as a design-study wherever the Persistent Digital Property Ecosystem is imagined differently: when a team wants a new way to think about the work, when a builder wants a starting point, or when a reader wants to judge one arrangement against another.
