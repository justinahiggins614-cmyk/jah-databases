Contentment Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Established: contentment is a documented emotion term in the published emotion literature; this record describes it as reported, with no claim about any person's health.

Contentment, the quiet pleasant state of being satisfied with what is present, without wanting it to be otherwise.

Contentment is easy to overlook because it is quiet: it makes no scene, raises no voice, and seeks no audience. The problem this record answers is how to tell contentment apart from its louder neighbours — joy, which is brighter and more active, and calm, which is restful without the sense of satisfaction. Contentment is pleasant and low in arousal: the person is glad of what is here, not reaching for what is not. The record sets out the definition, the situations reported to bring it on, the bodily signs as reported in the literature, the face and voice that accompany it, the behaviour that tends to follow, and the ways cultures describe it differently.

History

Contentment has a long history as a word and an idea. In ancient philosophy it appears as a goal: the Stoics praised freedom from wanting more, and Buddhist teaching described satisfaction with what is as a form of release. In the modern emotion literature it is treated as a distinct low-arousal pleasant state, placed by Russell's circumplex in the pleasant, calm quadrant. Fredrickson's broaden-and-build theory counts it among the positive emotions that widen a person's view of what is possible, quietly rather than dramatically.

An example

A gardener finishes weeding a small bed in the late afternoon, sits on the step with a cup of tea, and looks at the work without planning the next bed. Nothing dramatic has happened, and nothing more is wanted in that moment. The shoulders drop, the breathing slows, and the face is relaxed rather than lit up. If a neighbour stops to talk, the gardener is glad of the company and in no hurry to move on. The moment passes without ceremony, and the gardener later remembers it as a good afternoon rather than an exciting one.

Definition and triggers

Contentment is reported to follow the completion of a task done well, a meal shared without hurry, time in a familiar place, and the sense that what is present is enough. It is also reported after a worry resolves without drama, and in moments of rest following effort rather than during the effort itself.

Bodily signs as reported

The literature reports lowered muscle tension, slower and deeper breathing, and a relaxed posture. Heart rate is reported as steady rather than raised. These signs are modest and overlap with rest, and the record states them as reported, not as a fixed pattern that every person shows.

Facial and vocal expression

The face is described as relaxed: the mouth soft, the eyes unhurried, the brow smooth. The voice is reported as unhurried and even, without the lift that marks excitement. Neither the face nor the voice is dramatic, which is why contentment is often noticed by those nearby rather than announced.

Behavioral tendencies

Behaviour reported with contentment includes lingering where one is, savouring food or company without rushing, and a willingness to stay with the present task rather than start a new one. People are reported to be more open to conversation and less inclined to seek novelty in the moment.

Cultural variation

Some cultures praise contentment openly as a virtue, notably in Buddhist and Stoic traditions, while others treat ambition as the higher value and view contentment as settling. The English word has no exact match in every language, and the record notes where neighbouring words carry a different shade.

Related emotions

Contentment borders joy, which is brighter and more active; calm, which is restful without the sense of satisfaction; gratitude, which adds a sense of thanks toward a giver; and relief, which follows the end of a strain. Its opposite in the literature is longing, the sense that something missing matters.

Key figures

  • Barbara Fredrickson (born 1964) — psychologist whose broaden-and-build theory counts contentment among the positive emotions
  • Charles Darwin (1809-1882) — naturalist whose Expression of the Emotions described the quiet signs of satisfaction

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

BASICS = {'joy': (0.8, 0.6), 'trust': (0.6, 0.1), 'fear': (-0.6, 0.7), 'surprise': (0.1, 0.8), 'sadness': (-0.7, -0.3), 'disgust': (-0.6, 0.2), 'anger': (-0.7, 0.6), 'anticipation': (0.4, 0.5)}
title = 'Contentment'
valence = 0.55
arousal = -0.35
seed = 101
trials = 20
choices = 5
claimed_note = 'As reported in the literature: contentment is a low-arousal pleasant state, placed in the pleasant, calm quadrant of the circumplex.'

def compute():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pts = [(valence + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05),
            arousal + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05)) for _ in range(trials)]
    mv = sum(p[0] for p in pts) / trials
    ma = sum(p[1] for p in pts) / trials
    mag = math.hypot(mv, ma)
    nearest, dist = None, None
    for name, (bv, ba) in BASICS.items():
        d = math.hypot(mv - bv, ma - ba)
        if dist is None or d < dist:
            nearest, dist = name, d
    quadrant = ("pleasant" if mv >= 0 else "unpleasant") + ", " + ("activated" if ma >= 0 else "calm")
    levels = [round(mag * (k + 1) / choices, 3) for k in range(choices)]
    return {"title": title, "valence": round(mv, 3), "arousal": round(ma, 3),
            "magnitude": round(mag, 3), "nearest": nearest,
            "distance": round(dist, 3), "quadrant": quadrant, "levels": levels}

result_a = compute()
result_b = compute()
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result_a, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result_b, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()

print("Sampled mean (%d jittered points, fixed seed %d): valence %.3f, arousal %.3f" % (trials, seed, result_a["valence"], result_a["arousal"]))

print("Nearest basic emotion: %s (distance %.3f)" % (result_a["nearest"], result_a["distance"]))
print("Intensity scale (%d levels, mild to intense): %s" % (choices, ", ".join("%.3f" % v for v in result_a["levels"])))
print("Literature note: " + claimed_note)

print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)

Elation, the high, bright state of joy that lifts the whole body, stronger and more active than ordinary gladness.

Elation is joy at high intensity: the difference between being glad and being elated is one of degree and of bodily lift, and the problem this record answers is how to describe that difference plainly. History

In the modern literature it is treated as a high-arousal, high-valence state, placed in the bright corner of Russell's circumplex and in Plutchik's wheel as joy at its strongest intensity, above serenity and ecstasy's neighbour. The word comes from the Latin for lifting up, and the history of its use keeps that image of being raised.

An example

A student who has worked for months on an examination opens the results letter and reads a pass with distinction. She laughs aloud, reads the letter twice, and runs to tell her family, her voice higher and faster than usual. She cannot sit still; she paces the room, hugs her mother, and talks quickly about what comes next. Hours later the lift is still there, though quieter, and she remembers the day as one of the best of her life.

Affection Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established Established: affection is a documented emotion term in the published emotion literature; this record describes it as reported, with no claim about any person's health.

Affection, the warm feeling of fondness toward a person, animal, or place, shown in closeness and care.

Affection is often confused with love, and the problem this record answers is how to describe the difference plainly. Affection is the warm, fond feeling itself — milder than love, steadier than infatuation, and shown more in small acts of closeness than in grand statements. It is pleasant and mildly active: the person wants to be near, to touch, to look after. The record sets out the definition, the situations reported to bring it on, the bodily signs as reported in the literature, the face and voice that accompany it, the behaviour that tends to follow, and the ways cultures describe it differently.

History

Affection has been described since the earliest writing about human bonds as the ordinary warmth between people who belong together. Darwin described the gestures of affection — the touch, the leaning in, the soft voice — as among the most widely recognised expressions. In the modern literature it is treated as a distinct warm state, close to love on the circumplex but lower in intensity, and central to the study of attachment and of family bonds.

An example

A grandfather picks up his granddaughter from school and she runs to him, arms out. He lifts her, she rests her head on his shoulder, and they walk home slowly, talking about her day. He is in no hurry, and she keeps hold of his hand. At home he makes her a snack and sits beside her while she eats. Eagerness is often confused with excitement and with anticipation, and the problem this record answers is how to describe the difference plainly. Anticipation is the looking forward itself; excitement adds high energy and brightness; eagerness adds the wish to begin, the leaning forward into action. It is pleasant and moderately active: the person is ready, attentive, and impatient to start. The record sets out the definition, the situations reported to bring it on, the bodily signs as reported in the literature, the face and voice that accompany it, the behaviour that tends to follow, and the ways cultures describe it differently.

History

Eagerness has been studied in the literature on anticipation and on motivation, where it appears as the state that turns looking forward into readiness to act. In Plutchik's wheel it is close to anticipation at moderate intensity, and in Russell's circumplex it sits in the pleasant, activated quadrant. The modern literature treats it as a distinct state, studied beside excitement and interest, with its own research on the leaning forward and the readiness to begin.

An example

A young carpenter is given her first commission to build a chair of her own design. She reads the brief twice, lays out her tools in order, and measures the wood before the morning is out. She is not yet building, but she is already leaning into the work, and the waiting itself is full of energy. When the first cut is made, the eagerness turns at once into absorbed attention.

Most cultures have a word for eagerness, and most praise it in the young and in the willing worker. Some traditions prize restraint before a task, others prize open readiness, but the leaning forward and the wish to begin are described in much the same way everywhere the literature looks.

Related emotions

choices = 5

def compute():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pts = [(valence + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05),
            arousal + rng.uniform(-0.05, 0.05)) for _ in range(trials)]
    mv = sum(p[0] for p in pts) / trials
    ma = sum(p[1] for p in pts) / trials

        d = math.hypot(mv - bv, ma - ba)
        if dist is None or d < dist:
