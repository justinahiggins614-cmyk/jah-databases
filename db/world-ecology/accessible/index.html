What Ecology Is

Ecology is the science of the relations between living things and their surroundings: the organism and its environment, the population and its habitat, and the community and its ecosystem. The ecologist studies how living things live, grow, and die, and how they affect each other and the world around them. Ecology is the science of the whole: the individual, the population, the community, and the ecosystem, all together.

An ecosystem has 4 levels: the organism, the population, the community, and the ecosystem, and each level is made of the one below. A wood has 100 oak trees, and each oak has 1,000 leaves, so the wood has 100,000 leaves. A population of 50 deer in a wood of 10 hectares has a density of 5 deer per hectare. The word ecology was coined in 1866, and it is 160 years old in 2026. The ecologist measures the numbers: the population, the growth, and the change, and the measurement is the science.

Ecosystems

An ecosystem is a community of living things together with their non-living environment, interacting as a system: the plants, the animals, the microbes, and the air, the water, and the soil. The ecosystem is the unit of ecology: the forest, the lake, the grassland, and the desert are all ecosystems, and each has its producers, its consumers, and its decomposers. An ecosystem is the whole, and it is more than the sum of its parts.

A forest ecosystem has 3 feeding levels: the producers, the herbivores, and the carnivores, and the decomposers recycle the dead. If the producers capture 10,000 units of energy, the herbivores get about 1,000, and the carnivores about 100: only about 10 percent passes on, at each step. A lake ecosystem has the same levels: the algae, the small fish, and the large fish, and the decomposers at the bottom. The ecosystem was named in 1935, and it is 91 years old in 2026. The system is the unit: the forest, the lake, and the desert are studied as systems.

Habitats and Niches

A habitat is the place where a living thing lives: the wood, the pond, and the meadow, with its air, water, and soil. A niche is the role of the living thing in that place: what it eats, where it lives, and when it is active. The habitat is the address, and the niche is the profession: the owl lives in the wood, and it hunts at night, and that is its niche. No two species can occupy the same niche, for long, in the same place.

An owl and a kestrel both live in the wood, but the owl hunts at night, and the kestrel hunts by day: they share the habitat, but not the niche, and they coexist. Two species of warbler live in the same tree, but one feeds at the top, and one at the bottom: they share the habitat, but not the niche. The niche has 3 parts: what it eats, where it lives, and when it is active. Grinnell defined the niche in 1917, and Elton in 1927, 10 years later, and Hutchinson in the 1950s, about 30 years after Elton. The habitat is the address, and the niche is the profession.

Populations

A population is the group of one species in a place: the deer in the wood, the oak trees in the forest, and the fish in the lake. The population grows, shrinks, and changes, and the ecologist counts it, and measures its growth. Populations are the unit of the species: the species is made of populations, and the population is made of individuals, and the population changes by birth, death, immigration, and emigration.

A population of 100 deer grows by 20 births and shrinks by 10 deaths, so it grows by 10, to 110, in a year. If the carrying capacity is 200, the growth slows as the population nears 200, and it stops at 200. The lynx and the hare cycle over about 10 years: the hare rises, and the lynx follows, and the hare falls, and the lynx follows. The mark and recapture estimates the population: if 20 are marked, and 10 of the next 50 caught are marked, the population is about 100. The population changes by 4 things: birth, death, immigration, and emigration.

Communities

A community is all the populations in a place, living together: the trees, the birds, the insects, and the microbes of the wood, all together. The community is the unit above the population: the species interact, by eating, competing, and helping, and the community has a structure, a diversity, and a stability. The community is the living part of the ecosystem, and it is studied by the ecologist who counts the species and their relations.

A wood community has about 100 species of plants, 50 of birds, and 1,000 of insects, so about 1,150 species, in one wood. An island of 10 square kilometres has about 100 species, and an island of 100 square kilometres has about 200: the larger island has more species, but not 10 times as many. The community changes by succession: the bare ground becomes the grass, then the shrub, then the wood, over 100 years. The diversity is the richness and the evenness: 10 species with 10 individuals each is more diverse than 10 species with 91 and 1 each.

Food Webs and Food Chains

A food chain is the line of eating: the plant is eaten by the herbivore, which is eaten by the carnivore, and the chain is the flow of energy. A food web is the whole network: the many chains, joined together, because most species eat more than one thing, and are eaten by more than one thing. The food web is the structure of the community: it shows who eats whom, and how the energy flows, and what happens when one species is lost.

A food chain has 3 or 4 levels: the plant, the herbivore, the carnivore, and sometimes the top carnivore. If the plants capture 10,000 units of energy, the herbivores get about 1,000, the carnivores about 100, and the top carnivore about 10: only about 10 percent passes on, at each step. A food web has many chains: a fox eats the rabbit, the mouse, and the bird, and the rabbit eats the grass, the clover, and the leaf. The sea otter is a keystone species: it eats the sea urchin, and the urchin eats the kelp, and without the otter, the kelp forest is lost.

Energy Flow

Energy flow is the movement of energy through the ecosystem: from the sun, to the plant, to the herbivore, to the carnivore, and out as heat. The sun is the source, the plant captures it by photosynthesis, and the animal eats the plant, and the energy flows, and at each step most of it is lost as heat. Energy flow is the one-way flow: it enters as sunlight, and it leaves as heat, and it is not recycled, unlike the nutrients.

The sun gives 1,000 units of energy, and the plants capture about 1 percent, which is 10 units. The herbivores eat the plants, and they get about 10 percent of the 10, which is 1 unit. The carnivores eat the herbivores, and they get about 10 percent of the 1, which is 0.1 units. The chain is short, because the energy runs out: 3 or 4 levels, and then there is too little left. Lindeman measured this in 1942, and the 10 percent rule is 84 years old in 2026. The energy flows one way: it enters as sunlight, and it leaves as heat, and it is not recycled.

Nutrient Cycles

Nutrient cycles are the recycling of the materials of life: the carbon, the nitrogen, and the phosphorus, which move from the soil to the plant, to the animal, and back to the soil, again and again. The nutrients are recycled, unlike the energy: the same carbon atom is used many times, by many living things. Nutrient cycles are the circles of the ecosystem: the carbon cycle, the nitrogen cycle, and the water cycle, and the decomposers close them.

A tree takes carbon from the air, and it stores it in its wood: a large tree stores about 1 tonne of carbon, and a forest of 100 trees stores about 100 tonnes. The nitrogen cycle has 5 steps: the fixation, the nitrification, the assimilation, the ammonification, and the denitrification. The water cycle moves about 500,000 cubic kilometres of water a year, from the sea to the air. The carbon cycle is the climate: the burning of fossil fuels adds carbon to the air, and the air warms, and the climate changes. The cycles are circles: the same atom is used many times, by many living things.

Biodiversity

Biodiversity is the variety of life: the number of species, the variety within each species, and the variety of ecosystems. It is the richness of the living world: the millions of species, the genes, and the habitats, all together. Biodiversity is the measure of life's wealth, and it is falling: the species are being lost, and the ecosystems are being destroyed, and the loss is the greatest change of our time.

About 2 million species are known, and the total is thought to be about 8 million, so about 6 million are unknown, and 3 in 4 species are unknown. The loss is about 100 times the natural rate: if the natural rate is 1 species a year, the present rate is about 100 a year. A wood has about 1,150 species, and a tropical forest has about 10,000: the tropical forest has about 9 times as many. The Convention on Biological Diversity was signed in 1992, and it is 34 years old in 2026. Biodiversity is the wealth of life, and it is falling.

Succession

Succession is the change of a community over time: the bare ground becomes the grass, then the shrub, then the wood, as the species arrive, change the place, and are replaced. Succession is the development of the community: the pioneer species arrive first, and the climax community is the last, stable community. Succession is the history of a place, written in its species, and it is the ecologist's story of change.

Succession takes about 100 years: the bare ground becomes the grass in 10 years, the shrub in 30, and the wood in 100. The old fields of New Jersey were studied for 50 years, and the change was recorded: the grass, the shrub, and the young wood, in order. The pioneer is the lichen on the rock: it breaks the rock, and the soil forms, and the grass follows. The climax is the wood: it is stable, and it does not change, unless the fire, the storm, or the flood disturbs it, and the succession restarts.

Biomes

A biome is a large region of the Earth with a similar climate, and similar plants and animals: the tropical rainforest, the desert, the grassland, and the tundra are biomes. Biomes are the largest units of ecology on land: each covers millions of square kilometres, and each has its own community. The biome is the map of life on land, and the climate is what decides it.

There are about 10 major biomes on land: the rainforest, the savanna, the desert, the grassland, the temperate forest, the boreal forest, and the tundra, and 3 more. The rainforest covers about 6 percent of the land, and it holds about half of the species: 6 percent of the land, and 50 percent of the life. The desert gets less than 250 millimetres of rain a year, and the rainforest gets more than 2,000: the desert gets 8 times less. The tundra is the coldest biome: the summer is 2 months, and the winter is 10 months. The biomes are moving: the climate warms, and the biomes shift, by kilometres a year.

Forests

A forest is the community of trees: the woodland where the trees are the dominant plants, and the animals, the fungi, and the soil live beneath them. Forests cover about a third of the land, and they hold most of the land species, and they store the carbon. Forests are the lungs of the land: they take the carbon from the air, and they give the oxygen, and they are the home of most of the life on land.

Forests cover about a third of the land: 31 percent, and they hold most of the land species. A tropical forest has about 10,000 species, and a temperate forest has about 1,000: the tropical has 10 times as many. About 10 million hectares are lost a year, which is about 27,000 hectares a day. A large tree stores about 1 tonne of carbon, and a forest of 100 trees stores about 100 tonnes. The forest is the carbon store: the clearing releases it, and the planting stores it, and the protection is the climate work.

Grasslands

A grassland is the community of grasses: the open land where the grasses are the dominant plants, and the grazers, the predators, and the birds live among them. Grasslands cover about a quarter of the land, and they are the home of the great grazers: the bison, the zebra, and the antelope. Grasslands are the land of the grazer: the grass grows, the grazer eats it, and the grass grows again, and the fire renews them.

Grasslands cover about a quarter of the land: 25 percent, and they hold the great herds. The bison once numbered about 30 million on the prairie, and they fell to about 1,000, and they have recovered to about 500,000. A savanna has about 100 species of grasses, and 50 of grazers, and 10 of predators. The fire burns every 2 to 5 years, and the grass regrows in weeks: the fire renews the grassland, and without it, the wood takes over in 10 years. The grassland is the land of the grazer: the grass grows, and the grazer eats it, and the grass grows again.

Freshwater and Marine Ecosystems

Freshwater and marine ecosystems are the ecosystems of the water: the rivers, the lakes, and the ponds are the freshwater, and the seas and the oceans are the marine. The water covers about 70 percent of the Earth, and it holds most of the life: the plankton, the fish, and the whales. The freshwater is the water we drink, and the marine is the water that makes the climate: together they are the water of the planet.

The water covers about 70 percent of the Earth, and the ocean holds about 97 percent of the water. A coral reef holds about a quarter of the marine species, in less than 1 percent of the ocean: the reef is the rainforest of the sea. A lake has 3 zones: the shore, the open water, and the deep, and each has its life. The plankton make about half of the oxygen we breathe: the tiny algae, in the ocean, make the air. The ocean takes about a quarter of the carbon we emit, and it warms, and the coral bleaches.

Wetlands

A wetland is the land that is wet: the marsh, the swamp, and the bog, where the water covers the soil, for part or all of the year. Wetlands are the most productive ecosystems on Earth: they hold the water, clean it, and shelter the birds, the fish, and the plants. Wetlands are the kidneys of the land: they filter the water, and they store the flood, and they are the home of a quarter of the species.

Wetlands cover about 6 percent of the land, and they hold about a quarter of the species: 6 percent of the land, and 25 percent of the life. A wetland cleans the water: the reeds filter it, and the water leaves cleaner than it entered. The Ramsar Convention was signed in 1971, and it is 55 years old in 2026, and it lists about 2,500 sites. A bog stores the carbon for thousands of years: the peat grows 1 millimetre a year, and a bog of 10 metres is 10,000 years old. The wetland stores the flood: the marsh holds the water, and the town below is not flooded.

Deserts

A desert is the dry land: the place with less than 250 millimetres of rain a year, where the plants and animals are adapted to the dry. Deserts cover about a fifth of the land, and they are the home of the cactus, the camel, and the kangaroo rat. Deserts are the land of the dry: the rain is rare, and the life is adapted, and the desert blooms when the rain comes.

Deserts cover about a fifth of the land: 20 percent, and the Sahara is the largest hot desert, at about 9 million square kilometres. The rain is less than 250 millimetres a year, and in the driest desert, the Atacama, it is less than 1 millimetre a year: 250 times less. A camel drinks 100 litres at once, and it can go for weeks without drinking. The kangaroo rat never drinks: it makes its water from the seeds it eats. The desert blooms when the rain comes: the seeds wait for years, and the rain makes them flower, in days.

Climate and Ecology

Climate and ecology is the relation of the climate to the living world: the temperature and the rainfall decide where the species live, and the climate change moves them. The climate is warming, and the species are moving: the birds arrive earlier, the flowers bloom earlier, and the biomes shift. Climate and ecology is the science of the change: the warming, the moving, and the loss, and the ecologist measures them.

The Earth has warmed by about 1 degree since the 19th century, and the species are moving: the birds arrive about 2 weeks earlier, and the flowers bloom about 2 weeks earlier. The biomes are shifting by kilometres a year: the forest moves north, and the desert moves, and the tundra shrinks. A species that moves 10 kilometres a year will move 100 kilometres in 10 years, and 1,000 in 100. The species that cannot move are lost: the mountain species, and the island species, have nowhere to go, and the loss is the greatest change.

Invasive Species

An invasive species is a species that arrives in a new place, spreads, and harms the native species: the grey squirrel, the cane toad, and the Japanese knotweed are the famous invaders. The invasive species is the greatest threat to biodiversity after the habitat loss: it eats, competes, and spreads, and the natives have no defence. Invasive species are the accident of the global world: the ships, the planes, and the trade carry them, and they spread.

The rabbit arrived in Australia in 1859, with 24 rabbits, and they became millions: 24 became 600 million in 70 years. The grey squirrel arrived in Britain in the 1870s, and the red squirrel declined: the grey is now the common squirrel, and the red is rare. The cane toad was brought to Australia in 1935, with 101 toads, and they became millions, and they spread across the north. The Japanese knotweed costs millions to control, every year, in Britain. The invasive species is the greatest threat after the habitat loss: the ships carry them, and they spread.

Conservation Biology

Conservation biology is the science of saving species: the study of the threatened species, and the work of protecting them. The conservation biologist counts the species, finds the threat, and protects the habitat, the population, and the gene. Conservation biology is the science of the crisis: the species are being lost, and the biologist works to save them, before they are gone.

The red list has about 40,000 threatened species, of the 150,000 assessed: about 1 in 4 of the assessed species is threatened. The wolf returned to Yellowstone in 1995, with 31 wolves, and they became hundreds, and the elk, the beaver, and the willow returned too. The beaver returned to Scotland in 2009, with 16 beavers, and they spread. The panda has about 1,800 in the wild, and it was taken off the endangered list in 2016, because the numbers grew. Conservation biology is the science of the crisis: the species are counted, and the threat is found, and the protection is the work.

Restoration Ecology

Restoration ecology is the science of repairing ecosystems: the damaged place is restored, by planting, by rewetting, and by returning the species. The restoration ecologist takes the field, the mine, and the drained marsh, and they make it a living ecosystem again. Restoration ecology is the science of the repair: the damage is undone, and the life returns, and the ecosystem works again.

Restoration takes years: the meadow is restored in 5 years, the wood in 50, and the wetland in 10. The prairie restoration in Wisconsin began in the 1930s, and it is 90 years old in 2026, and it is still studied. The river re-meandered in the 1990s flows in bends again, and the fish return in years. The wetland rewetted in 10 years holds the water, and the birds return, and the carbon is stored. The restoration is the repair: the damage is undone, and the life returns, and the ecosystem works again.

Human Impacts

Human impacts are the effects of people on the living world: the habitat loss, the pollution, the hunting, and the climate change, which harm the species and the ecosystems. The human is the greatest force in ecology now: the population is 8 billion, and the footprint is the planet. Human impacts are the story of our time: the loss, and the change, and the choice to stop them.

The human population is about 8 billion, and it grows by about 70 million a year. The habitat loss is the greatest impact: about 10 million hectares of forest are lost a year. The plastic in the ocean is about 8 million tonnes a year, and the fish eat it. Silent Spring was published in 1962, and it is 64 years old in 2026, and the movement it began is still growing. The footprint is the demand: the average person uses the resources of about 1.7 Earths, and the planet has only 1.

Ecological Field Methods

Ecological field methods are the ways the ecologist works in the field: the quadrat, the transect, the mark and recapture, and the trap, which count the living things, and measure the place. The field method is the tool of ecology: the ecologist goes to the place, and they count, measure, and record, and the science is the numbers. Field methods are the hands of ecology, and they are the first skill the ecologist learns.

A quadrat is 1 square metre, and 10 quadrats cover 10 square metres: if 5 plants are in each, the density is 5 per square metre, and the field of 1,000 square metres has about 5,000 plants. A transect is 100 metres long, and the plants along it are counted every 10 metres, so 10 counts. The mark and recapture estimates the population: 20 marked, and 10 of the next 50 caught are marked, so the population is about 100. The field notebook is the record: the date, the place, and the weather, and the counts, written at the time.

A History of Ecology

The history of ecology is the history of the science of the relations: from the naturalists who described the world, to the scientists who measured it, to the movement that protects it. It is 160 years long, as a named science, and thousands of years long, as a watching. The history has five ages: the naturalists, the namers, the measurers, the systems, and the protectors, and each age changed what the ecologist could do.

Haeckel coined the word in 1866, and Tansley named the ecosystem in 1935, 69 years later. Lindeman measured the energy flow in 1942, 7 years after Tansley, and Carson wrote Silent Spring in 1962, 20 years after Lindeman. From Haeckel in 1866 to 2026 is 160 years, and from Carson in 1962 to 2026 is 64 years. The five ages are the naturalists, the namers, the measurers, the systems, and the protectors, and each lasted decades, and the last is still running. Ecology is 160 years old as a named science, and thousands of years old as a watching.

Ecology in the Modern World

Ecology in the modern world is the science of the planet in crisis: the climate warming, the species being lost, and the ecosystems being destroyed, and the ecologist measuring, explaining, and protecting. The modern ecologist works with the satellite, the DNA, and the computer, and the science is global: the planet is the study, and the protection is the work. Ecology in the modern world is the science that the planet needs, and it is the work of the century.

The satellite maps the whole planet in days, and the change is measured in years: the forest lost, and the desert grown, are seen from space. The DNA barcoding names 1,000 species from one sample, in a week, and the counting is faster than ever. The citizen science counts the birds: 100,000 people count, and 10 million birds are counted, in a year. The global ecology measures the planet: the climate, the biodiversity, and the human impact, together, and the protection is the work of the century. The modern ecologist works with the satellite, the DNA, and the computer, and the science is global.
