History of Paper

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000001 · ladder rank 1 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Paper was invented in China around 105 AD, traditionally credited to court official Cai Lun, who made sheets from bark, rags, and fishnets. Papermaking spread west along trade routes, reaching the Islamic world by the 8th century and Europe by the 12th. Before wood pulp, paper came from linen and cotton rags — which is why old books survive so well. The Fourdrinier machine, perfected in the early 1800s, made continuous rolls of paper and slashed prices. Wood pulp replaced rags in the mid-1800s, making paper cheap enough for mass newspapers.

Paper Fibers

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000002 · ladder rank 2 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Paper is a mat of plant fibers held together by hydrogen bonds. Long softwood fibers (pine, spruce) make strong paper; short hardwood fibers (birch, eucalyptus) make smooth, opaque paper. Cotton and linen fibers are longest of all — currency and fine stationery use them. Recycled fibers are shorter and weaker but save trees and energy. Fiber length directly predicts tear strength.

Pulping — Freeing the Fibers

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000003 · ladder rank 3 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Pulping separates plant fibers from wood. Mechanical pulping grinds logs against stones — high yield but weak, yellowing paper like newsprint. Chemical (kraft) pulping cooks chips in chemicals that dissolve lignin, leaving strong fibers for packaging and fine papers. Semi-chemical pulping splits the difference for corrugated board. Bleaching whitens chemical pulp; unbleached kraft is the brown of grocery bags. Pulping is the most energy- and chemical-intensive step of papermaking.

Sheet Forming — From Slurry to Sheet

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000004 · ladder rank 4 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Paper forms when a dilute fiber slurry drains through a screen, leaving a fiber mat. Hand papermakers use a mould and deckle: dip, shake to interlock fibers, and couch the sheet onto felt. The Fourdrinier machine does this continuously on a moving wire mesh. Cylinder machines build thick boards by layering. Formation — how evenly fibers distribute — determines paper quality more than any other factor.

Pressing and Drying

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000005 · ladder rank 5 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Fresh sheets are mostly water and must be pressed and dried. Pressing between felts squeezes out water mechanically — the cheapest water removal. Then heated dryer cans evaporate the rest, shrinking and strengthening the sheet. Calendering — pressing the dry sheet between polished rollers — sets smoothness and gloss. Sizing (starch or chemicals added to the sheet) controls how ink absorbs instead of feathering.

Paper Recycling

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000006 · ladder rank 6 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Recycling repulps used paper, screens out contaminants, de-inks it, and reforms new sheets. Each recycling shortens fibers, so paper can be recycled about 5 to 7 times before fibers are too short. De-inking uses flotation: air bubbles lift ink particles away from fibers. Recycled paper saves roughly 60 percent of the energy of virgin paper. Keep food-soiled paper and wax-coated cartons out of the recycling bin.

Handmade Paper at Home

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000007 · ladder rank 7 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. You can make paper with a blender, a picture frame with window screen, and a tub of water. Step 1: Tear scrap paper and soak it overnight. Step 2: Blend with water into pulp. Step 3: Pour pulp into the tub and stir. Step 4: Dip the screen frame, lift level, and let water drain. Step 5: Couch the sheet onto a cloth. Step 6: Press under boards, then dry flat. Add flower petals, seeds, or colored thread to the pulp for decorative sheets. Iron the dry sheet between cloths for smoothness.

Paper Types and Grades

JAH-PAPERMAKS-000008 · ladder rank 8 · signature-generated-completion

Signature-original. Paper grades serve different jobs. Newsprint is cheap, weak, and yellows — fine for newspapers. Uncoated fine papers take ink well for books and office printing. Coated papers have a clay layer for sharp magazine images. Kraft and linerboard are strong packaging papers. Tissue, towel, and specialty papers round out the family. Basis weight (pounds per ream) measures thickness and heft.
