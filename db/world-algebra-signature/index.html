What Algebra Is

ID: JAH-ALGBS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds an algebra ladder from arithmetic laws to symbolic equations, with the same laws checked on numbers first and on letters second.

Definition: Algebra is the branch of mathematics that uses symbols, usually letters, to stand for numbers, and studies the rules for working with them. Where arithmetic computes with particular numbers, such as 2 + 3 = 5, algebra states general facts, such as a + b = b + a for all numbers a and b, and proves them once for every case. An equation is a statement that two expressions are equal, and solving it means finding the values of the unknowns that make it true. Algebra is the language in which relationships are written down, from a phone bill to the orbit of a planet, and it is the gateway to all higher mathematics.

History: Algebra began as word problems. Babylonian scribes solved quadratic equations around 1800 BCE, and Egyptian problems in the Rhind papyrus, about 1650 BCE, asked for unknown quantities. The Greek Diophantus, around 250 CE, used symbols for unknowns. The word algebra comes from al-jabr, in the title of a book by the Persian scholar al-Khwarizmi, written around 825 CE, which solved linear and quadratic equations in words. In the 1500s, Italian mathematicians solved cubic and quartic equations, and in the 1600s Descartes joined algebra to geometry, writing equations for curves. In the 1800s, algebra became abstract: Galois studied the structure of equations, and mathematicians began to study operations themselves, leading to modern abstract algebra and to the algebra used in computing. The word algorithm comes from the name of al-Khwarizmi, Latinised as Algorismus, and his book was studied in the House of Wisdom in Baghdad, a centre of learning in the 800s. In 1145, Robert of Chester translated al-Khwarizmi's book into Latin, and through it algebra entered Europe, where Fibonacci's Liber Abaci of 1202 spread the new methods to merchants. In the 1500s, the Italian mathematicians solved the cubic and the quartic, and in the 1600s Descartes joined algebra to geometry. In the 1900s, algebra became the language of computing: a variable in a program is the same idea as a variable in an equation, and every spreadsheet formula is an algebraic expression.

Key concepts: A variable is a symbol for a number that can vary, usually a letter such as x. A constant is a number that does not change. An expression is a combination of variables, numbers, and operations, such as 3x + 5. An equation states that two expressions are equal. A solution is a value of the variable that makes the equation true. A formula is an equation that states a general rule, such as area equals length times width. An identity is an equation true for all values, such as (a + b) squared = a squared + 2ab + b squared. A term is a part of an expression separated by + or - signs. A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable: in 3x, the coefficient is 3. Like terms have the same variable parts and can be combined: 3x and 2x make 5x. An inverse operation undoes another: subtraction undoes addition, and division undoes multiplication. The distributive law says a times (b + c) is ab + ac. An unknown is a value to be found, while a parameter is a fixed number that can change between problems, such as the 2 in the formula for a taxi fare of 3 plus 2 per mile. An equation states equality, while an inequality states that one side is larger or smaller.

How it works: Algebra works by the same rules as arithmetic, applied to symbols. The commutative, associative, and distributive laws let expressions be rearranged: a + b is b + a, and a times (b + c) is a times b plus a times c. To solve an equation, undo what has been done to the unknown, keeping both sides equal at every step: for 3x + 5 = 20, subtract 5 from both sides to get 3x = 15, then divide by 3 to get x = 5. To check a solution, substitute it back into the original equation. To simplify an expression, collect like terms: 3x + 2x is 5x, and 4y - y is 3y. Algebra generalises arithmetic: one piece of algebra covers infinitely many arithmetic problems at once. To turn a word problem into algebra, name the unknown, write the equation, solve it, and check it. A number doubled and 5 added is 15: let the number be x, so 2x + 5 = 15, subtract 5 to get 2x = 10, divide by 2 to get x = 5, and check: 2 times 5 plus 5 is 15. The same steps solve a phone plan: a plan costs 10 plus 2 per gigabyte, and a bill of 24 means 10 + 2g = 24, so 2g = 14 and g = 7 gigabytes. Checking by substitution is part of the method, not an extra: it catches mistakes in the algebra.

Worked examples: Solve 3x + 5 = 20: subtract 5 to get 3x = 15, divide by 3 to get x = 5, and check: 3 times 5 plus 5 is 20. The distributive law: 3 times (x + 4) is 3x + 12, and with x = 2 that is 18, matching 3 times 6. Collecting terms: 3x + 2x - x is 4x. A phone plan costs 10 plus 2 per gigabyte: for 7 gigabytes the cost is 10 + 14 = 24, and in general the cost is 10 + 2g. The area of a rectangle of length x + 3 and width x is x(x + 3), which is x squared + 3x, and with x = 5 that is 40. 2(x - 3) + 4 = 10 gives x = 6, since 2x - 6 + 4 is 10, so 2x is 12.

Real applications: Algebra is used wherever a relationship must be worked out from given information. Engineers write equations for forces, currents, and heat; economists write them for supply and demand; doctors use them for drug doses. Computer programs are built from algebraic expressions, and spreadsheets are grids of formulas. In science, a law is usually an equation, such as distance equals speed times time, and solving it answers questions such as how long a journey takes. In everyday life, algebra is the mathematics of unknown quantities: how many hours of work pay for a purchase, how much paint covers a wall, or what speed reaches a destination on time. In spreadsheets, every formula is algebra: a cell containing =A1*2+B1 is an expression in the variables A1 and B1. In programming, variables hold values that change as the program runs, and a line such as total = price * quantity + tax is an algebraic formula. In budgeting, algebra turns a monthly income and a list of expenses into an equation for what is left. In sport, statistics such as a batting average or a points-per-game figure are algebraic formulas applied to data.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why use letters instead of numbers? Because letters stand for any number, so one piece of algebra covers infinitely many arithmetic problems at once. Is algebra just solving for x? No: solving is one skill, but algebra also studies the structure of operations, the behaviour of functions, and the patterns in equations. Is there always a solution? Not always: x + 1 = x has no solution, and some equations have many. Why do equations need checking? Because it is easy to make an error in a step, and a wrong solution usually fails the substitution check, which catches it. Is algebra hard? It is demanding, but it is built from arithmetic, and each rule can be learned and practised separately. Is algebra just arithmetic with letters? No: arithmetic computes with known numbers, while algebra works with unknowns and with general rules, so one piece of algebra covers infinitely many arithmetic problems. What is the difference between an expression and an equation? An expression, such as 3x + 5, has a value but no equals sign, while an equation, such as 3x + 5 = 20, states that two expressions are equal and can be solved. Why do we use letters? Because a letter can stand for any number, so a formula written with letters is a rule for all numbers at once. Does algebra have to be hard? No: it is a small set of rules applied carefully, and checking by substitution catches most mistakes.

Variables and Expressions

ID: JAH-ALGBS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A variable is a symbol, usually a letter such as x or n, that stands for a number whose value can change or is not yet known. An expression is a combination of variables, numbers, and operations, such as 3x + 5 or x squared - 2x + 1. An expression has a value once its variables are given values: 3x + 5 with x = 4 is 17. Expressions are the sentences of algebra: they describe quantities, relationships, and rules, and they are simplified, evaluated, and used to build equations. Learning to read and write expressions is the first step of algebra.

History: Variables developed slowly. Early algebra used words for unknowns: al-Khwarizmi, around 825 CE, wrote equations out in full sentences. Diophantus, around 250 CE, used symbols for unknowns, and Indian mathematicians used letters and colours for them. In the 1500s, Francois Vieta introduced letters for both unknowns and known quantities, and Descartes, in the 1630s, fixed the convention still used: letters from the end of the alphabet, x, y, z, for unknowns, and from the start, a, b, c, for knowns. The modern expression, with powers written as superscripts and operations shown by symbols, was complete by the late 1600s, and it is now the universal language of mathematics and science. Indian mathematicians used colours for unknowns: one unknown might be called black, another blue, a system that let them write equations with several unknowns. In 1557, Robert Recorde introduced the equals sign in The Whetstone of Witte, writing that no two things can be more equal than two parallel lines. Thomas Harriot, in the early 1600s, introduced the symbols for greater than and less than, and he wrote equations with a clarity that was new. In the 1700s, Euler introduced the notation f(x) for a function of x, which is now universal. In science, Greek letters are used for variables: theta for an angle, lambda for a wavelength, and pi for a ratio, and in computing a variable is a named place in memory that holds a value.

Key concepts: A variable stands for a number that can vary or is unknown. A constant is a fixed number. A term is a part of an expression separated by + or - signs: 3x + 5 has two terms, 3x and 5. A coefficient is the number multiplying a variable: in 3x, the coefficient is 3. Like terms have the same variable parts, such as 3x and 2x, and can be combined. The value of an expression is the number it gives when its variables are replaced by values. An algebraic expression has no equals sign; with one, it becomes an equation. A variable has a domain, the set of values it is allowed to take: in a problem about ages, the domain is positive numbers. A parameter is a fixed number in a formula that can change between problems, such as the interest rate in a savings formula. A placeholder is a symbol that marks a place where a value goes, as in a form with blanks to fill in. An index is a number written as a subscript to tell terms apart, as in a1, a2, a3. A subscript is a small number or letter written below and to the right of a symbol. A variable can stand for a number, a quantity, or a whole expression, depending on the problem.

How it works: Expressions work by substitution and by simplification. To evaluate an expression, replace each variable with its value and compute, following the order of operations: 3x squared + 2x - 5 with x = 4 is 3 times 16 plus 8 minus 5, which is 48 + 8 - 5 = 51. To simplify, collect like terms: 3x + 2x - x is 4x, and 5 + 2 - 1 is 6. To expand, use the distributive law: 3(x + 4) is 3x + 12. To factor, reverse the process: 3x + 12 is 3(x + 4). Expressions are built to describe situations: if a taxi charges 3 plus 2 per mile, the cost for m miles is 3 + 2m, and for 10 miles it is 23. To evaluate an expression with two variables, replace each with its value and follow the order of operations. For 2x + 3y with x = 4 and y = 2: 2 times 4 is 8, 3 times 2 is 6, and 8 + 6 is 14. To simplify 5a - 2a + 3b, combine the like terms 5a and 2a to get 3a + 3b, and note that 3b cannot be combined with 3a, since the variables differ. To expand 4(x - 2) + 3x, distribute the 4 to get 4x - 8 + 3x, then combine to get 7x - 8, and with x = 5 that is 27, checked by 4 times 3 plus 15, which is 27.

Worked examples: Evaluate 3x + 5 with x = 4: 3 times 4 plus 5 is 17. Evaluate 3x squared + 2x - 5 with x = 4: 3 times 16 is 48, 2 times 4 is 8, and 48 + 8 - 5 is 51. Collect like terms: 3x + 2x - x is 4x, and 2y + 5y is 7y. Expand: 3(x + 4) is 3x + 12, and 2(x - 3) is 2x - 6. Factor: 3x + 12 is 3(x + 4). Real applications: Expressions are used in every formula: the area of a rectangle, the cost of a service, the distance travelled, and the interest on a loan are all expressions. In programming, an expression is a calculation the computer evaluates, and variables hold the values. In science, expressions describe laws, such as the kinetic energy of a moving object, which is half the mass times the velocity squared. In business, expressions model costs, revenues, and profits. In everyday life, an expression is the mathematics of a rule: the cost of a taxi ride, the price of a meal with a tip, or the time of a journey at a given speed. In physics, formulas use variables for quantities: E = mc squared has variables for energy, mass, and the speed of light. In spreadsheets, a cell reference such as A1 is a variable, and a formula such as =A1*2 is an expression in it. In programming, a variable holds a value that the program changes: a counter goes up by 1 each time, and a total adds each new amount. In economics, a formula for profit uses variables for price, cost, and quantity sold.

Solving Linear Equations

ID: JAH-ALGBS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a solving ladder from one-step to two-step to unknowns-on-both-sides equations, with every solution verified by substitution.

Definition: A linear equation is an equation in which the unknown appears only to the first power, such as 5x - 7 = 18 or 2x + 3 = 11. Solving it means finding the value of the unknown that makes the equation true. A linear equation in one unknown has exactly one solution, unless it is an identity, true for all values, or a contradiction, true for none. Solving works by undoing what has been done to the unknown, keeping both sides equal at every step: whatever is done to one side must be done to the other. The solution is checked by substituting it back into the original equation.

History: Linear equations are ancient: Egyptian problems in the Rhind papyrus, about 1650 BCE, asked for unknown quantities, and Babylonian scribes solved them. Al-Khwarizmi, around 825 CE, set out the methods of al-jabr, restoring, and al-muqabala, balancing, which are the operations of moving terms between sides and combining them. European algebra of the 1500s and 1600s standardised the methods, and Descartes' notation made them easy to write. In the 1800s, linear equations were extended to systems of many equations, and to matrices, and in the 1900s they became the core of linear algebra, used in computing, engineering, and economics. Today, solving a linear equation is the first algebraic skill taught, and it is the basis of all equation solving. The Egyptians solved linear equations by false position: to solve x + x/7 = 24, they guessed a value, found how far the result was from 24, and scaled the guess. The Chinese used the method of excess and deficit, solving a problem by two guesses and combining the errors. In the 1600s, the balance scale became the standard picture of an equation: what is done to one side must be done to the other, or the scale tips. In the 1800s, linear equations were extended to systems, and in the 1900s computers began solving them by the thousand, using the same methods of elimination that Gauss had set out. The equation 3x + 5 = 20 is the same problem as the Egyptian one, in modern notation.

Key concepts: A linear equation has the unknown to the first power only. A solution is a value that makes the equation true. An identity is true for all values, such as 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2. A contradiction has no solution, such as x + 1 = x. The golden rule is that whatever is done to one side must be done to the other. Inverse operations undo each other: addition undoes subtraction, and multiplication undoes division. Collecting like terms simplifies each side before solving. A check substitutes the solution into the original equation. A one-step equation needs one operation to solve, such as x + 5 = 12. A two-step equation needs two, such as 3x + 5 = 20. A solution set is all the values that satisfy an equation: a linear equation in one variable has exactly one, unless it is an identity or a contradiction. An equivalent equation is one with the same solution, produced by doing the same operation to both sides. Transposition is moving a term to the other side and changing its sign, which is a shortcut for adding or subtracting the same amount from both sides. A check substitutes the solution into the original equation, not into a simplified version.

How it works: Solving works by isolating the unknown. For 5x - 7 = 18: add 7 to both sides to get 5x = 25, then divide both sides by 5 to get x = 5. For 3(x + 2) = 21: expand to 3x + 6 = 21, subtract 6 to get 3x = 15, divide by 3 to get x = 5. For an equation with the unknown on both sides, such as 4x + 3 = 2x + 11, subtract 2x from both sides to get 2x + 3 = 11, subtract 3 to get 2x = 8, divide by 2 to get x = 4. For an equation with fractions, multiply through by the denominator: x/3 + 1 = 5 becomes x + 3 = 15, so x = 12. To check, substitute the solution into the original equation and confirm both sides agree. To solve an equation with fractions, clear them first by multiplying by the common denominator. For (2x + 1)/3 = 5, multiply both sides by 3 to get 2x + 1 = 15, subtract 1 to get 2x = 14, divide by 2 to get x = 7, and check: (14 + 1)/3 is 5. For 7x - 4 = 3x + 12, subtract 3x to get 4x - 4 = 12, add 4 to get 4x = 16, divide by 4 to get x = 4, and check: 7 times 4 minus 4 is 24, and 3 times 4 plus 12 is 24. For 0.5x + 1.5 = 4, multiply by 2 to get x + 3 = 8, so x = 5.

Worked examples: Solve 5x - 7 = 18: add 7 to get 5x = 25, divide by 5 to get x = 5, and check: 5 times 5 minus 7 is 18. Solve 2x + 3 = 11: subtract 3 to get 2x = 8, divide by 2 to get x = 4, and check: 2 times 4 plus 3 is 11. Solve 4x + 3 = 2x + 11: subtract 2x to get 2x + 3 = 11, subtract 3 to get 2x = 8, divide by 2 to get x = 4. Solve 3(x + 2) = 21: x = 5, since 3 times 7 is 21. Solve x/3 + 1 = 5: multiply by 3 to get x + 3 = 15, so x = 12. A taxi charges 3 plus 2 per mile: for a fare of 23, 3 + 2m = 23 gives m = 10 miles. (2x + 1)/3 = 5 gives x = 7, since 2x + 1 is 15. 7x - 4 = 3x + 12 gives x = 4: 7 times 4 minus 4 is 24, and 3 times 4 plus 12 is 24.

Real applications: Linear equations are used to find unknown quantities in every field: the time needed at a given speed, the price that gives a target revenue, the dose that gives a target concentration, and the mixture that gives a target strength. In business, they solve break-even problems. In science, they solve for a missing measurement. In computing, they appear in graphics, in optimisation, and in the linear systems that underlie machine learning. In everyday life, they answer questions such as how many hours of work pay for a purchase, or how much paint is needed for a wall. In cooking, a recipe for 4 people is scaled to 6 by a linear equation: if the recipe uses 3 cups for 4, then for 6 it uses 3 times 6/4, which is 4.5 cups. In travel, converting Celsius to Fahrenheit is a linear equation: F = 1.8C + 32, so 20 degrees Celsius is 68 Fahrenheit. In business, a break-even point is where revenue equals cost, which is a linear equation in the number sold.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does a linear equation always have one solution? A linear equation in one unknown has exactly one solution, unless it is an identity, with infinitely many, or a contradiction, with none. Why must both sides be treated the same? Because an equation states that the two sides are equal, and doing different things to them breaks the equality. What if the unknown is on both sides? Collect the unknown terms on one side and the numbers on the other, then solve as usual. What if the equation has fractions? Multiply through by the denominators to clear them, then solve. How do you know the answer is right? Substitute it into the original equation: if both sides agree, the solution is correct. What is an equivalent equation? One with the same solution, produced by adding, subtracting, multiplying, or dividing both sides by the same non-zero number. Why must the same operation be done to both sides? Because an equation states that two things are equal, and changing only one side breaks the equality, like adding weight to one side of a balance. What if the equation has no solution? Then it is a contradiction, such as x + 1 = x, which cannot hold for any x. What if every value works? Then it is an identity, such as 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2, which is true for all x. Inequalities

ID: JAH-ALGBS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds an inequalities ladder from a single comparison to compound and absolute-value inequalities, with each solution set tested inside and outside.

Definition: An inequality is a statement that two expressions are not equal, but one is greater than or less than the other, such as 2x + 3 > 11 or x - 5 <= 0.
