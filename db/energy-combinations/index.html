Kinetic + Thermal: Friction's Heat Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Friction converting motion to heat is established textbook physics, measured since Joule, and confirmed every time a brake warms.

Collapse verdict: collapses — Dissipative collapse: ordered motion decays into disordered heat and the moving body stops unless driven. The energy is conserved; the usefulness is lost.

Members: kinetic energy, thermal energy

Combination rule: Kinetic energy meets a resisting surface and converts to thermal energy at the contact. The combination holds only while motion is supplied; the heat spreads away from the contact and the motion decays.

A moving body that rubs against a surface turns its motion into heat, and the motion runs down unless more energy is supplied.

When two surfaces slide against each other, the ordered kinetic energy of the moving body does not vanish: it is converted into the disordered thermal motion of the atoms at the contact. The body slows, the surfaces warm, and the combination of kinetic plus thermal energy collapses in the dissipative sense — the motion dies away into heat and the system comes to rest unless fresh energy is supplied. Friction is the most familiar energy combination in daily life: every brake, every sliding drawer, every footstep spends kinetic energy as heat. The verdict here is collapses, because without a drive the motion always decays; the heat itself is conserved, but the useful ordered motion is gone.

A cyclist squeezes the brakes on a long downhill. The brake pads press the rims, the bike slows, and the rims grow hot enough to feel. The kinetic energy the rider gained from the descent is now thermal energy in the pads, the rims, and the air. If the rider stops pedalling and keeps braking, the bike comes to a halt: the motion has collapsed into heat. The same accounting holds for a sliding box, a skidding tyre, and a rubbed match head.

History

People have made fire by rubbing wood together since prehistory, which is friction turning kinetic energy into thermal energy on purpose. Count Rumford showed in 1798 that boring a cannon kept producing heat without limit, arguing heat was motion, not a fluid. James Joule's paddle-wheel experiments in the 1840s measured the mechanical equivalent of heat and fixed the conversion between motion and warmth as a number.

Members: electrical energy, thermal energy

Combination rule: Electrical energy converts to thermal energy in a resistance. The combination holds steady at or below the rated power with designed cooling; it runs away when cooling fails and resistance rises with temperature.

A current through a resistor turns electrical energy into heat, steady at the rated power and runaway when cooling fails.

Resistive heating joins electrical energy with thermal energy: a current driven through a resistance warms the conductor, and almost all the electrical energy appears as heat. At the rated power, with designed cooling, the combination is conditionally stable — a toaster, a kettle element, an industrial heater. The condition is cooling: in most metals resistance rises as temperature rises, so a fixed voltage drives more heat as the element warms, and if cooling cannot keep up, the temperature climbs. The verdict is conditionally stable, with the rating and the cooling as the conditions.

A kettle element draws its rated current and boils a litre of water in minutes. The water carries the heat away as fast as the element makes it, so the element stays near the water temperature. James Joule showed in 1841 that the heat produced in a wire grows with the square of the current, giving the law P equals I squared R. Every modern heating element is rated for a power and a cooling arrangement, and every electrical code limits current by wire size for exactly this reason.

Fission joins nuclear energy with thermal energy: a nucleus splits and the fragments carry kinetic energy that becomes heat in the surrounding material. In a power reactor the chain reaction is controlled so that, on average, each fission causes exactly one further fission, and the heat output holds steady — conditionally stable. The condition is control: if the multiplication rises above one, power climbs; if it falls below one, power falls. for (ei, eo, el, es, exp) in cases:
    got = abs(ei - (eo + el + es)) <= 0.01
    if got == exp:
        correct += 1
accuracy = correct / total
