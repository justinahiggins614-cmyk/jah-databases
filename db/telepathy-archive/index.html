Rhine Card-Guessing Experiments Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: historical-claim Historical claim: the experiments are documented history, but the telepathy claim itself is unproven, and the record carries the skeptical analysis beside the reported results.

The card-guessing experiments run at Duke University in the 1930s, the founding experiments of modern experimental parapsychology.

In the early 1930s, researchers at Duke University asked a plain question: if a person tries to guess the symbol on a card they cannot see, do they score above chance? The Rhine card-guessing experiments were the first large, systematic attempt to answer that question with statistics rather than anecdote. Joseph Banks Rhine and his colleagues ran thousands of trials in which a sender looked at a card and a receiver, shielded from seeing it, tried to name its symbol. The problem the experiments posed was one of method as much as of mind: how do you test a claim of mind-to-mind communication without letting ordinary clues, memory, or sloppy recording produce a false positive? The experiments matter because they set the template for nearly everything that followed in the field, from the design of the card deck to the use of chance expectation as the baseline. They also matter because their results, once headline news, did not survive later scrutiny in the form their supporters claimed.

History

The work began at Duke University in Durham, North Carolina, in 1930, when J. B. Rhine arrived and began testing extrasensory perception with playing cards and then with a purpose-built deck of twenty-five cards carrying five symbols. Rhine published the results in his 1934 book Extra-Sensory Perception, which brought the work to wide public attention. The Duke Parapsychology Laboratory was formally established in 1935, and the Journal of Parapsychology began publication in 1937. Through the 1930s and 1940s the laboratory ran card-guessing series with many subjects, some of whom, like Hubert Pearce, posted strikingly high scores. By the 1950s the card-guessing program had largely given way to other designs, but the Rhine experiments remained the reference point for the field and the target of its critics.

The experiment or case

A typical Rhine run used a deck of twenty-five cards, five of each symbol, shuffled before the run. The sender sat with the deck and turned the cards one at a time; the receiver, in another room, called out a symbol for each card, and an experimenter recorded every call in order. With five symbols and random guessing, the chance expectation is five correct calls in twenty-five. Rhine reported that some subjects scored well above that level across long series — in his early reports, average scores for selected high-scoring subjects ran above chance over hundreds of trials, and the most famous series, the Pearce-Pratt experiment, is covered in its own record in this archive. The recording was done by hand, card by card, and the deck was reshuffled between runs. Later critics pointed out that hand recording, hand shuffling, and the absence of strict controls in the earliest series left room for ordinary errors to creep into the record, and that the highest scores came from a small number of subjects tested again and again.

Methods

A deck of twenty-five Zener cards is prepared, five cards carrying each of five symbols: circle, cross, wavy lines, square, and star; A sender in one place looks at each card in turn while a receiver in another place, unable to see the cards, calls out the symbol they believe is showing; Each call is written down before the next card is shown, so that later guesses cannot be revised to match earlier cards; The run is repeated for a fixed number of trials, and the total number of correct calls is counted against the chance expectation of one correct call in five; Control runs with no sender, or with shuffled cards the sender never sees, are run for comparison; Scores are analyzed with the binomial distribution to ask how often chance alone would produce a score that high.

Results as reported

Rhine reported that his subjects, taken together, scored above the chance expectation of one in five, and that a small number of subjects scored far above chance over long series. The headline results were the high scorers: subjects who, in Rhine's reports, averaged well above five hits in twenty-five across hundreds of trials, with odds against chance quoted in the millions to one. Rhine presented these figures as evidence of extrasensory perception, a term he preferred to telepathy, covering both mind-to-mind and mind-to-card effects. The reported results were published as tables of hits, trials, and computed odds, and they were widely reported in the press of the 1930s.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics raised three standing objections. First, the early series used hand shuffling and hand recording, and critics showed that imperfect shuffling can leave patterns in a deck that a receiver can exploit without any paranormal ability, while recording errors tend to favor hits. Second, the highest scores came from a small number of subjects tested repeatedly, which raises the problem of selection: test enough people and a few will score high by chance, and testing those few again and again inflates the apparent effect. Third, the effect did not replicate cleanly: when other laboratories ran card-guessing tests under tighter controls, the large effects shrank or vanished, a pattern later called the decline effect. None of this proves that nothing happened in the Rhine laboratory, but it means the reported results cannot be taken as established, and the burden of proof was never met by a clean, independently replicated series.

Science proof

The science of the card-guessing experiment is the science of the null hypothesis. With five symbols, a random guesser should be right one time in five, so twenty-five trials should give about five hits, with a standard deviation of two. A score of nine or ten in twenty-five is unusual but not extraordinary; a score far above that, sustained over hundreds of trials, would be hard for chance to explain. The experiment's scientific value lies in that clean baseline: unlike a seance or a dream report, a card call is either right or wrong, and the arithmetic is exact. The scientific weakness of the early Rhine work was not the arithmetic but the controls around it: hand-shuffled decks can be imperfectly shuffled, hand-recorded calls can be misrecorded, and a sender and receiver who know each other can pass information through ordinary channels without meaning to. Later designs in this archive — the ganzfeld, the pre-registered trial — were built to close exactly those gaps. In plain words, the demo guesses at random with a fixed seed: with 5 choices the chance hit rate is 1 in 5, so 25 trials should give about 5.0 hits by luck alone, and the demo's simulated run gave 1 hits, a z-score of -2.000. A claimed result is only interesting if it beats that chance baseline by a wide, repeatable margin — and the skeptical analysis in this record says whether it did.

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'Rhine Card-Guessing Experiments'
trials = 25
choices = 5
seed = 101
claimed_note = 'As reported: early Rhine series claimed average scores above the chance expectation of 5 hits in 25 trials, with a few subjects scoring far above chance; exact figures varied by series and are given in the record text, not as a single canonical number.'

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        sent = rng.randrange(choices)
        guessed = rng.randrange(choices)
        if sent == guessed:
            hits += 1
    return hits

p = 1.0 / choices
expected = trials * p
sd = math.sqrt(trials * p * (1 - p))

hits_b = run_once()

Ganzfeld Experiments Rank 2 · simple

Evidence: historical-claim Historical claim: the experiments are documented history, but the telepathy claim itself is unproven, and the record carries the skeptical analysis beside the reported hit rates.

The ganzfeld experiments of the 1970s and 1980s, in which a receiver in mild sensory deprivation tried to describe a target image a sender was viewing.

By the 1970s, card guessing had run its course as a research design, and parapsychologists wanted a test that looked more like the spontaneous cases people actually reported: a vivid impression of a distant scene, not a named symbol on a card. The ganzfeld experiment was the answer. A receiver sits in a state of mild, uniform sensory stimulation — halved ping-pong balls over the eyes, white noise in the ears, a soft red light — while a sender in another room looks at a randomly chosen picture or video clip. After a period of free description, the receiver is shown four possible targets and asked which one matches the impression they received. The problem the design tried to solve was noise: if telepathy is a weak signal, perhaps it is drowned out by ordinary sensory input, and quieting the senses might let it through. The problem it created was one of controls: with free description and human judging, small leaks and small biases can manufacture a hit rate above chance.

History

The ganzfeld technique was adapted from Gestalt psychology, where a ganzfeld — a uniform visual field — was used to study perception. Charles Honorton brought it into parapsychology in the 1970s at the Maimonides Medical Center in Brooklyn, and it became the field's flagship experiment through the 1980s. Honorton's 1985 meta-analysis pooled twenty-eight studies and reported a hit rate of about thirty-five percent against a twenty-five percent chance baseline. The design was then debated in public: psychologist Ray Hyman published a sharp critique, Honorton replied, and in 1986 the two issued a joint communique agreeing on the standards a convincing ganzfeld study would have to meet. New studies built to those standards followed in the 1990s, including the autoganzfeld series at Honorton's Psychophysical Research Laboratories, and meta-analyses continued to report hit rates around thirty to thirty-five percent. The debate has never been settled to both sides' satisfaction.

The experiment or case

In a standard ganzfeld session, the receiver lies back with the ping-pong ball halves taped over their eyes and white noise playing, while a sender in another room watches a short film clip of, say, a waterfall. The receiver talks freely — 'water, falling, white, loud' — and the experimenter records it all. When the sending period ends, the receiver is shown four clips: the waterfall, a desert road, a city street, and a forest. If the receiver picks the waterfall as the best match, that is a hit. Across many sessions, chance alone should produce a hit about one time in four. Honorton's 1985 meta-analysis reported about thirty-five percent, and later meta-analyses reported figures in the low thirties. The autoganzfeld studies of the 1990s automated the target selection and the judging to remove the experimenter's hands from the process, and they reported similar hit rates, which supporters present as the strongest evidence in the field and critics present as the product of subtle, persistent flaws.

Methods

A pool of four target images or short video clips is prepared, and one target is chosen at random for the session; The receiver relaxes in the ganzfeld state — halved ping-pong balls over the eyes, white noise through headphones, a soft red light — while the sender views the target in a separate, shielded room; The receiver speaks their impressions aloud for about twenty minutes, and the session is recorded; After the sending period, the receiver is shown all four targets and ranks them by how well each matches the impressions; A hit is scored when the actual target is ranked first; with four targets the chance hit rate is twenty-five percent; Sessions are repeated with different receivers and targets, and the overall hit rate is compared with chance.

Results as reported

The reported results are hit rates above the twenty-five percent chance baseline. Honorton's 1985 meta-analysis of twenty-eight studies reported about thirty-five percent. Later meta-analyses reported figures in the low thirties — Storm, Tressoldi, and Di Risio (2010) reported about thirty-two percent across their pooled studies, and later updates reported similar numbers. The autoganzfeld series of the 1990s reported a hit rate of about thirty-two percent. Supporters present these figures, sustained across decades and across laboratories, as the strongest quantitative case for telepathy in the published record.

Skeptical analysis

Critics reply that a hit rate a few points above chance is exactly what small, persistent flaws produce. Hyman's original critique listed flaws in the early studies: inadequate randomization, possible sensory leakage between sender and receiver, multiple analyses of the same data, and selective reporting of favorable sessions. Later critics added target non-independence — the four targets in a set are not equally matchable by a free description — and the file-drawer problem, in which unsuccessful studies go unpublished and the published pool overstates the effect. The joint Hyman-Honorton standards were meant to settle this, but the studies run to those standards are few, and reviewers disagree about whether they replicate the effect. The hit rate has also declined as controls tightened, which is the pattern expected if flaws, not telepathy, drove the early numbers.

Science proof

The science of the ganzfeld is the science of a four-choice test with a twenty-five percent baseline. A single hit means little; a hundred sessions at thirty-five percent would be hard for chance to explain, if the trials are truly independent and the judging truly blind. The design's scientific appeal is that it tests a vivid, naturalistic impression rather than a symbol call, and its scientific vulnerability is that vividness invites bias: a receiver who describes water will match a waterfall target better than a desert target even if the sender sent nothing, because the four targets are rarely equally likely to match a free description. This is the problem of target non-independence, and it is one of several mundane mechanisms — along with sensory leakage, judging bias, and selective reporting — that can push a hit rate above twenty-five percent without any telepathy. The joint Hyman-Honorton standards were an attempt to close those mechanisms one by one. The Society for Psychical Research, founded in London in 1882, and its archives: the oldest organized body of telepathy case records and investigation reports.

Before there were laboratories, there were case collections: letters from people who said they knew, at the moment it happened, that a distant loved one had died, or that a friend was in danger. The problem for anyone who wanted to study telepathy in the nineteenth century was that such stories were everywhere and evidence was nowhere — vivid, sincere, and impossible to check. The Society for Psychical Research was founded to do the checking: to collect cases systematically, to investigate them while the witnesses were still alive and the facts still verifiable, and to publish the results, including the failures. Its archives are the reason the early history of telepathy research can be written at all, and they are also the reason its limits are visible: a case collection, however careful, is not an experiment.

History

The Society for Psychical Research was founded in London in 1882, with the philosopher Henry Sidgwick as its first president. Its early members included the classicists Frederic Myers and Edmund Gurney, the physicist William Barrett, and the future prime minister Arthur Balfour. In 1886 Gurney, Myers, and Frank Podmore published Phantasms of the Living, a two-volume collection of over seven hundred cases of apparent telepathy, mostly crisis apparitions and deathbed impressions. The Society's Census of Hallucinations (1894) surveyed seventeen thousand people about hallucinatory experiences. Its Proceedings and Journal published investigations of mediums, haunted houses, and spontaneous cases for more than a century. The Society's archives, including its case files and correspondence, are now held at Cambridge University Library. The American Society for Psychical Research was founded in Boston in 1885 as its sister body.

The experiment or case

A typical case in Phantasms of the Living runs like this: a woman in London wakes at three in the morning with a vivid sense that her brother, serving abroad, is in danger; she notes the time; days later a letter arrives reporting that her brother was wounded in battle at that very hour. The Society's investigators would interview the woman, ask whether she had told anyone of the impression before the news arrived, check the letter's date and the military record of the wounding, and publish the case with their assessment. Of the hundreds of cases collected, the Society's editors judged a minority to be well attested — the rest lacked independent confirmation, or the timing was vague, or the impression was the kind of anxious dream that anyone might have. The archive's value is that it preserved both the strong cases and the weak ones, with the judgments attached.

Methods

A case is reported to the Society, usually by letter, describing an impression received at a distance and the event it seemed to match; Investigators interview the witness and, where possible, other people who were present, and record the testimony in writing; The investigators check the dates, times, and facts of the distant event against independent records such as letters, telegrams, or death certificates; Cases are graded by the strength of the corroboration: first-hand testimony with independent confirmation ranks highest; Cases that fail corroboration, or that turn out to have ordinary explanations, are recorded alongside the successes; Selected cases are published in the Society's Proceedings or Journal with the evidence and the investigators' judgment attached.

Results as reported

The Society reported that a minority of the cases it collected were well attested: impressions received at a distance that matched a distant event in time and detail, with first-hand testimony and independent confirmation. Phantasms of the Living presented over seven hundred cases and argued that chance coincidence could not account for the best of them, particularly the crisis apparitions in which a person seemed to see or hear a distant loved one at the hour of their death. The Census of Hallucinations reported that a small fraction of the population had experienced vivid hallucinations, and that a subset of those coincided with a distant death. The Society never claimed that its case collections proved telepathy; its own editors presented the evidence as suggestive and in need of experimental confirmation.

Skeptical analysis

Critics of the case method point out that the Society's archives are a selected sample: people write in when an impression matches an event, not when it matches nothing, so the files overstate the hit rate by construction. Memory is reconstructive, and a witness who learns of a death will often remember an earlier unease as sharper and better timed than it was. Frank Podmore, from inside the Society, argued that most crisis apparitions could be explained by coincidence, misremembered timing, and the sheer number of anxious impressions people have. Modern skeptics add that the Census of Hallucinations, for all its scale, relied on self-report and could not verify the timing of the experiences it counted. The archive's defenders reply that the best cases survived exactly these objections — but survival of objection is not proof, and the case method has never produced a result that an experiment could confirm.

In the West, the story of telepathy research is usually told as an Anglo-American story: Rhine at Duke, the SPR in London, the ganzfeld in Brooklyn. The Soviet story runs in parallel and is harder to verify, because much of it was published in Russian, some of it was classified, and some of it reached the West only through popular books that mixed reporting with romance. The problem this record addresses is that of sorting the documented Soviet work — the laboratory experiments on mental suggestion, the published monographs, the named researchers — from the Cold War mythology that grew around it, including the persistent claim that the Soviet Union had a secret, successful telepathy program that the West had to match. That mythology drove real American spending, which makes getting the Soviet record right a matter of history, not just of curiosity.

History

Soviet interest in mental suggestion began in the 1920s. The neurologist Vladimir Bekhterev studied thought transference at his institute in Leningrad, and the biophysicist Alexander Chizhevsky worked on related questions. In the 1930s Leonid Vasiliev, a physiologist at Leningrad University, ran experiments in which a hypnotized subject in one room was given mental suggestions by an experimenter in another, and reported effects on the subject's movements and physiology; his book Experiments in Mental Suggestion appeared in Russian in 1962 and in English in 1963. Named figures included the psychic Karl Nikolaiev, tested by Vasiliev, and the researcher Eduard Naumov, who organized conferences on parapsychology in Moscow in the 1960s before being arrested in 1974. After the Soviet collapse, Russian parapsychology continued on a smaller scale, but no declassified file has shown a successful state telepathy program.

The experiment or case

Vasiliev's mental-suggestion experiments are the best documented Soviet example. A subject, hypnotized and lying in one room, was to fall asleep, or to wake, or to move an arm, when the experimenter in another room mentally commanded it. Vasiliev reported that the subject responded at the commanded times more often than in control periods, and he published tables of successes and failures. The experiments were done with the limited shielding of the period, and the subject often knew the general schedule of the session. Western readers met the work through the 1963 English translation of Vasiliev's book, and through Ostrander and Schroeder's popular account, which added stories of long-distance telepathy tests between Moscow and Leningrad and of psychics who could read sealed envelopes. The documented core — Vasiliev's published experiments — is modest; the surrounding mythology is large.

A subject, often hypnotized, is placed in one room, and an experimenter in another room concentrates on a suggestion, such as raising an arm or falling asleep; The subject's movements and physiological responses are observed and timed, and the times are compared with the times of the suggestions; Control trials are run in which no suggestion is sent, to establish how often the subject moves or sleeps without one; In card and image tests, a sender views a target while a receiver in a shielded room attempts to identify it, with the results scored against chance; Results are reported in Soviet journals and monographs, and in some cases in the popular press; Later Western reviewers compare the published results with the methods described, and ask whether the controls rule out ordinary cues.

Results as reported

A hypnotized subject's compliance with an expected schedule is the ordinary outcome, not evidence of mental suggestion. The popular claims rest on a single popular book whose authors were enthusiasts, not investigators, and whose stories were not checked against primary sources. The Cold War framing — a secret Soviet program the West had to match — has no support in the declassified record on either side, and historians of the American program now treat it as a myth that drove funding rather than a fact that justified it. The Soviet work remains historically interesting; it is not evidence that telepathy works.

Methods

Begin with a simple, repeatable test — card guessing — and run it in large numbers so that chance expectation can be computed exactly; Select and retest the highest-scoring subjects, on the reasoning that ability, if it exists, will be unevenly distributed; Publish the results with computed odds against chance, so that readers can judge the size of the claimed effect; Found a laboratory, a journal, and a foundation to sustain the work beyond any single experiment; Respond to criticism by refining the design — machine shuffling, machine recording, distance tests — while keeping the same basic question; Extend the program from card guessing to dice, to dreams, and to spontaneous cases, building a literature rather than a single result.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics reply that Rhine's results were never independently replicated under conditions his critics accepted, and that the effects shrank as controls tightened — the pattern expected if the early results came from flawed procedure rather than from telepathy. The argument has never been settled, but the scientific consensus is that Rhine did not establish telepathy: his laboratory produced a literature, not a proof.

A Zener deck in use looks simple: twenty-five plain cards, each printed with one of five black symbols — a circle, a cross, three wavy lines, a square, a five-pointed star. Later work replaced the physical deck with machine shuffling and then with computer-generated symbols, and the large effects reported with the hand-shuffled deck did not survive the change. The deck is a good instrument; the early results obtained with it are not good evidence, because the instrument was not used under conditions that guaranteed its fairness.

By the 1970s, parapsychology had a problem: its founding experiments, the Rhine card tests, had lost credibility as controls tightened and effects shrank, and the field needed a new design that could convince both its own researchers and its critics. Charles Honorton set out to build it. The problem he addressed was one of design and of debate: could a telepathy experiment be built cleanly enough that a skeptic would accept a positive result, and could its results be pooled honestly enough that a meta-analysis would mean something? Honorton's answer was the ganzfeld, and his career is the story of that answer being tested in public — in the laboratory, in the journals, and in a famous joint statement with his leading critic. He matters because he did what few in the field did: he argued with his critics in the open and agreed, in writing, to the standards his own work would be judged by.

History

Charles Honorton was born in 1946 and studied parapsychology at the Maimonides Medical Center in Brooklyn, where he worked on dream telepathy in the 1960s and 1970s. In the 1970s he developed the ganzfeld design and ran the first series of ganzfeld studies. In 1979 he founded the Psychophysical Research Laboratories in Princeton, New Jersey, where he ran the autoganzfeld studies of the 1980s and early 1990s, using computers to select targets and record judgments. In 1985 he published the meta-analysis of twenty-eight ganzfeld studies that became the center of the field's debate, and in 1985-86 he debated Ray Hyman in the Journal of Parapsychology, ending with their joint communique of 1986. Honorton died in 1992, at forty-six, of a heart attack. His work remains the most cited body of ganzfeld research.

The experiment or case

Honorton's autoganzfeld laboratory in Princeton is the example. A receiver lay back in the ganzfeld state while a computer chose a target clip at random and showed it to a sender in another room; the receiver's spoken impressions were recorded; then the computer presented four clips and the receiver ranked them, with no experimenter handling the targets or the scores. The automation was the point: in the early ganzfeld studies, critics had argued that experimenters might unconsciously cue the receiver or mishandle the targets, and the autoganzfeld removed those hands from the process. The autoganzfeld series reported a hit rate of about thirty-two percent against twenty-five percent chance, a figure Honorton's supporters present as the cleanest result in the field and his critics present as still explicable by subtle flaws.

Methods

Adapt the ganzfeld technique from Gestalt psychology: uniform visual and auditory stimulation to quiet the receiver's ordinary senses; Select targets at random from a pool of four, and keep the target secret from the receiver and from the experimenter who interacts with them; Record the receiver's free impressions during the sending period, then ask the receiver to rank the four targets by match; Automate the target selection and the judging wherever possible, so that no person's choices can influence the result; Pool the results across studies in a meta-analysis, with stated inclusion criteria, so that the overall hit rate can be judged; Publish the methods in full and debate the critics in the open literature, agreeing in writing to the standards future studies must meet.

He presented these figures as evidence that telepathy could be demonstrated under laboratory conditions, and he argued that the autoganzfeld results, obtained with automated target selection and judging, answered the methodological objections to the earlier studies.

Daryl Bem's presentiment experiments and the 2011 'Feeling the Future' paper, the most publicly debated telepathy-adjacent claim of the twenty-first century.

In 2011, a paper in one of psychology's leading journals reported that people could feel the future: that their responses to a stimulus were influenced by events that had not yet happened. The paper was 'Feeling the Future: Experimental Evidence for Anomalous Retroactive Influences on Cognition and Affect' by Daryl Bem, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. The problem it posed was not only about telepathy — Bem's experiments were about precognition and presentiment, the sense of a future event before it occurs — but about science itself: if a respected researcher, using standard methods, could produce statistically significant evidence for something most scientists consider impossible, then either the impossible was real or the standard methods were broken. The paper set off a public argument about replication, statistics, and publication that reshaped psychology far beyond parapsychology.

History

Daryl Bem was born in 1938 and became a professor of psychology at Cornell University, known for his work on self-perception theory. In the 1990s he worked with Charles Honorton on ganzfeld research. In 2011 he published 'Feeling the Future' in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, reporting nine experiments in which participants' responses appeared to be influenced by future random events — for example, guessing the position of an erotic image before the computer had chosen it, or reacting to an emotional picture before it appeared. The paper was accepted after peer review, which made its claims harder to dismiss than the usual parapsychology paper. Replication attempts followed immediately: some reported success, most reported failure, and a 2015 meta-analysis by Bem and colleagues claimed an overall positive effect, while critics argued that the meta-analysis pooled flawed studies and that the effect vanished in pre-registered replications. The debate continues, and it is now cited as often in discussions of psychology's replication crisis as in discussions of telepathy.

The experiment or case

Bem's best-known experiment reversed a standard guessing task. A participant sat at a computer and was shown two curtains; behind one, the computer would later place an erotic image, chosen at random after the participant had guessed. The participant clicked the curtain they felt the image would be behind. With two curtains, chance is fifty percent. Bem reported that participants guessed correctly about fifty-three percent of the time across his experiments — a small edge, but one that, pooled across many participants and trials, reached statistical significance in his analysis. Critics replied that a three-point edge in a task with many possible analyses is exactly what selective reporting produces, and that pre-registered replications found no edge at all. The example shows the shape of the whole debate: a small effect, a large claim, and a disagreement about whether the methods that produced it can be trusted.

Methods

Take a standard psychology experiment — for example, a guessing task or a reaction-time task — and reverse its time order, so that the stimulus the participant responds to is chosen after the response; Run the task many times with many participants, and record the responses before the computer selects the stimulus; Compare the participants' accuracy or reaction times with the chance baseline for the task; Repeat the experiment in several forms, varying the stimuli and the measures, to see whether the effect appears across designs; Publish the full set of experiments, with the methods and the statistics, in a peer-reviewed journal; Invite independent replication, including pre-registered attempts in which the methods and analyses are fixed before the data are collected.

Results as reported

Bem reported that across nine experiments, participants' responses were influenced by future random events: guessing accuracy above fifty percent where chance was fifty percent, and faster reactions to emotional images that had not yet been shown. He reported the pooled result as statistically significant and presented it as experimental evidence for anomalous retroactive influences — effects running backward in time. A 2015 meta-analysis by Bem and colleagues pooled ninety replications and reported an overall positive effect. Supporters present the paper as peer-reviewed evidence that something anomalous is happening; critics present it as a demonstration of how flexible analysis can manufacture significance.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics reply that Bem's experiments gave the researcher many choices — which trials to count, which measures to report, when to stop collecting data — and that with enough choices, a significant result will appear by chance. The pre-registered replications, in which those choices were fixed in advance, found no effect, which is the decisive pattern: when the flexibility was removed, the effect disappeared. The 2015 meta-analysis is criticized for pooling studies of uneven quality and for counting the original experiments alongside their replications. The scientific consensus is that Bem's paper demonstrated a flaw in psychology's methods, not a flaw in the ordinary understanding of time: it helped trigger the replication crisis and the move toward pre-registration, which is its lasting contribution.

The meta-analyses that pooled decades of telepathy experiments — ganzfeld, card guessing, and dream studies — and the argument over what the pooled numbers mean.

A single telepathy experiment rarely settles anything: its effect, if it exists, is small, and a single study can be dismissed as a fluke or a flaw. A meta-analysis tries to settle the question by pooling many studies — combining their results with stated rules, weighting larger studies more heavily, and asking whether the whole body of work points in one direction. The problem this record addresses is whether that pooling works for telepathy. Meta-analyses of the ganzfeld report hit rates above chance; meta-analyses of card guessing report small positive effects; and yet the field's critics reply that pooling flawed studies does not produce a sound conclusion, it produces a precisely wrong one. The record's question is therefore methodological: what can a meta-analysis prove, and what can it only appear to prove?

History

Meta-analysis came to parapsychology in the 1980s. Charles Honorton's 1985 meta-analysis of twenty-eight ganzfeld studies was the first to draw wide attention, reporting a hit rate of about thirty-five percent against twenty-five percent chance. Ray Hyman's critique followed the same year, and the two debated the pooling in the Journal of Parapsychology. Later meta-analyses covered the ganzfeld again — Storm, Tressoldi, and Di Risio in 2010, and Storm and colleagues in 2017 — and extended the method to dream telepathy, presentiment, and card guessing. Bem's 2015 meta-analysis of presentiment experiments pooled ninety studies. Each new meta-analysis restated the same pattern: a small positive pooled effect, a dispute over which studies should have been included, and a disagreement over whether the file-drawer problem — unpublished failures — explained the whole result. The history of telepathy meta-analysis is therefore also a history of the method's limits.

The experiment or case

Honorton's 1985 ganzfeld meta-analysis is the example. He gathered twenty-eight ganzfeld studies published up to that point, extracted each study's hit rate — the fraction of sessions in which the receiver ranked the true target first — and pooled them, weighting by the number of sessions. The pooled hit rate came out at about thirty-five percent, against twenty-five percent chance, with odds against chance that Honorton computed as very large. Hyman's reply re-examined the same twenty-eight studies and argued that several had flaws — poor randomization, possible sensory leakage, multiple analyses of the same sessions — and that removing or correcting those studies reduced the pooled effect. Later meta-analyses repeated the pattern with larger pools: Storm, Tressoldi, and Di Risio (2010) pooled twenty-nine ganzfeld studies and reported about thirty-two percent. The numbers moved a little; the argument did not move at all.

Methods

Define the question and the inclusion rules: which experiments count, which designs, which outcome measures; Search the published literature and, where possible, the unpublished literature for every study that meets the rules; Extract the same number from each study — the hit rate, the effect size, or the significance level — and weight each study by its size; Combine the weighted numbers into a single pooled estimate, and compute how far it sits from the chance baseline; Test for publication bias by asking how many unpublished null studies would be needed to erase the pooled effect; Report the inclusion rules, the list of studies, and the sensitivity of the result to changing the rules.

Results as reported

The reported results are pooled hit rates above chance. Honorton (1985): about thirty-five percent across twenty-eight ganzfeld studies, against twenty-five percent chance. Storm, Tressoldi, and Di Risio (2010): about thirty-two percent across twenty-nine studies. Later updates reported similar figures in the low thirties. Bem and colleagues (2015) reported a positive pooled effect across ninety presentiment studies. Supporters present the stability of these figures across decades and across meta-analysts as evidence that something real is being measured; critics present the same stability as evidence that the same flaws are being pooled again and again.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics reply that a meta-analysis cannot fix the studies it pools. If the ganzfeld studies share a flaw — targets that are not equally matchable, judging that is not fully blind, sessions that are not fully independent — then pooling them produces a precise estimate of the flaw, not of telepathy. The file-drawer problem is the second objection: the published literature is a selected sample, and the unpublished failures, if they were counted, would pull the pooled rate toward chance. The third objection is that the pooled effect shrinks when stricter inclusion rules are applied, which is what a flaw-driven effect should do and what a real effect should not. Until a pre-registered, high-powered study run to agreed standards produces a positive result on its own, the meta-analytic case remains an argument about the literature, not evidence about the mind.

The skeptical case against telepathy research: methodological criticism, the file-drawer problem, and the argument that the field's positive results are artifacts of how studies are run and reported.

Every claim of telepathy has met the same reply: show me the flaw. The skeptical literature on telepathy is not a single argument but a toolkit of objections, built up over a century, about how experiments can go wrong and how results can mislead. The most important of those objections is the file-drawer problem, named by the psychologist Robert Rosenthal in 1979: studies that find nothing are filed away unpublished, while studies that find something are published, so the published literature overstates the effect even if every published study is honest. The problem this record addresses is what the skeptical case actually is — not a slogan, but a set of specific, checkable criticisms — and whether the field has answered it. A reader who understands the skeptical case understands why telepathy remains unproven despite a century of positive-looking results.

History

Skeptical criticism of telepathy research runs alongside the research itself. In the nineteenth century, Frank Podmore criticized the Society for Psychical Research's case collections from the inside. In the twentieth century, the magician and writer Martin Gardner attacked Rhine's card experiments in print, and the psychologist Ray Hyman became the field's most persistent methodological critic, debating Honorton over the ganzfeld in 1985-86. James Randi, a stage magician, offered a million-dollar prize from 1964 onward for a demonstration of paranormal ability under controlled conditions; the prize was never claimed and was retired in 2015. The file-drawer problem was named by Rosenthal in 1979 and became central to the debate in the 1980s. In the 2010s, Bem's presentiment paper turned the skeptical toolkit on psychology itself, and the replication crisis that followed made pre-registration — the skeptics' long-standing demand — standard practice across psychology.

The experiment or case

The file-drawer problem is the example. Suppose twenty laboratories run a ganzfeld study each. By chance alone, one of them will get a hit rate high enough to look significant, and that laboratory will publish its result; the nineteen that found nothing will file their data away, because journals prefer positive results and researchers prefer to move on. A reader of the published literature then sees one striking success and no failures, and concludes that the effect is real. Rosenthal's fail-safe number asks how many unpublished null studies would have to exist in file drawers to bring the pooled result down to chance; if that number is small, the published result is fragile. In telepathy research the fail-safe numbers have been disputed, because they depend on assumptions about how many studies were run and never published — a number nobody knows. The example shows why the skeptical case is hard to answer from the published literature alone: the literature is the evidence, and the literature is selected.

Methods

Read the published study and list every choice the researcher made: how subjects were selected, how targets were randomized, how results were scored; Ask whether each choice could have produced the result without telepathy: imperfect shuffling, sensory leakage, judging bias, selective reporting; Ask how many unpublished studies would be needed to erase the reported effect, and whether that number is plausible; Ask whether the effect replicates when the study is run again by independent researchers under tighter controls; Ask whether the effect shrinks as controls improve, which is the signature of an artifact rather than a real phenomenon; Publish the criticism in the open literature so that the researchers can answer it, and judge the exchange on its merits.

Results as reported

The skeptics report no positive result of their own; they report the failure of the field's positive results to survive scrutiny. Their reported findings are negative: effects that shrink as controls tighten, meta-analyses whose pooled rates fall when flawed studies are removed, replications that fail when pre-registered, and a million-dollar prize that went unclaimed for fifty years. The skeptical literature presents these as the pattern of a field measuring its own artifacts, and it presents the file-drawer problem as the mechanism that keeps the published literature looking positive while the underlying reality is null.

Skeptical analysis

This record is the skeptical analysis, so the analysis here is the reply to the reply: the field's defenders argue that skeptics set an impossible standard, that they dismiss positive results by inventing hypothetical flaws, and that the demand for perfect controls is applied to parapsychology and not to other sciences. The skeptics answer that the standard is the ordinary one — independent replication under agreed controls — and that parapsychology has had a century to meet it. The record presents both positions and notes the asymmetry: a skeptic needs only one surviving ordinary explanation to doubt a result, while a believer needs to exclude all of them to establish it. That asymmetry is why the burden of proof rests on the claim, and why the claim remains unproven.

S. G. Soal's card-guessing experiments of the 1930s and 1940s, once the strongest evidence for telepathy, later shown to rest on altered records.

For twenty years, the Soal experiments were the best evidence parapsychology had. S. G. Soal, a British mathematician, ran card-guessing tests in the 1930s and 1940s that reported hit rates far above chance, with careful controls and a reputable co-investigator, Kathleen Goldney. The results were published, praised, and cited as the answer to the critics of Rhine: here, at last, was a clean, well-controlled demonstration. The problem this record addresses is what happened next. In the 1970s, a re-examination of Soal's original score sheets found signs that the records had been altered — numbers changed, sequences reordered — in ways that manufactured the high scores. The Soal story is the field's cautionary tale: it shows how a result can be both carefully published and false, and why the survival of the original records matters more than the reputation of the researcher.

History

Samuel George Soal was a British mathematician who began card-guessing experiments in the 1930s. Working with the artist Basil Shackleton as his star subject, Soal reported strikingly high scores, including runs in which Shackleton appeared to guess cards before they were shown. Kathleen Goldney, a researcher with the Society for Psychical Research, collaborated on the later series and co-authored the reports. The results were published in the Proceedings of the Society for Psychical Research in the 1940s and were widely cited as the strongest evidence for telepathy. In 1974, the researcher Betty Markwick, working from Soal's own deposited score sheets, published an analysis showing that the sheets bore signs of alteration: digits changed, entries added, sequences reordered in ways that inflated the scores. The Society for Psychical Research accepted Markwick's findings, and Soal's results are now cited as a case of fraud, not of telepathy. Soal died in 1975, a year after Markwick's paper appeared.

The experiment or case

The Shackleton series is the example. Basil Shackleton, Soal's subject, sat in one room and called out the digits on cards that Soal turned over in another, and Soal reported scores far above the chance expectation of one in five. The published tables showed long runs of correct calls, and the odds against chance were quoted as astronomical. When Betty Markwick examined the original score sheets in the 1970s, she found something the published tables did not show: on some sheets, digits had been overwritten, entries had been inserted, and the sequence of calls did not match the sequence of the cards as recorded elsewhere. The alterations were small — a digit changed here, a call added there — but across hundreds of trials they were enough to manufacture a large apparent effect. Markwick's paper did not accuse Soal in anger; it laid out the sheets, the changes, and the arithmetic, and let the evidence speak.

Methods

Prepare a deck of cards with symbols or digits, and shuffle it before each run; Have the sender look at each card in turn while the receiver, shielded from the cards, calls out the symbol or digit; Record every call and every card in order on a score sheet, with the date and the conditions of the run; Score the run against the chance expectation for the number of choices, and compute the odds against chance; Publish the results with the methods, so that other researchers can judge the controls; Deposit the original score sheets, so that later researchers can check the published tables against the raw record.

The skeptical analysis of Soal is Markwick's: the score sheets show alteration, and alteration explains the scores. The deeper skeptical point is about the field's standards: Soal's results were accepted for twenty years because they came from a reputable researcher in a reputable journal, and they were overturned only because the raw score sheets had been preserved and someone thought to look at them. The case is therefore cited by skeptics as evidence that the field's published literature cannot be trusted at face value, and by the field's defenders as evidence that the field corrects itself — the fraud was found by a researcher working inside the field's own institutions, using its own archives. Long before anyone ran a card-guessing experiment, people told stories about knowing things at a distance: a mother who feels her son's death the moment it happens, a seer who sees a neighbor's funeral before it is announced, a twin who feels their sibling's pain across an ocean. Skeptics of folklore-as-evidence point out that a folk story is selected for survival by its tellability, not its truth, and that the stories that survive are the ones with the sharpest coincidence and the most dramatic timing. Twin telepathy claims: the persistent belief that twins, especially identical twins, share thoughts and feel each other's pain at a distance.

Of all telepathy claims, twin telepathy is the most believed and the most studied outside the laboratory. Ask a room of people whether twins can feel each other's pain, and many will say yes, often with a story: a twin who knew the moment their sibling was hurt, a twin who finished the other's sentence, a twin who woke in the night when the other was in danger. The problem this record addresses is what those stories amount to. Twins share genes, upbringing, habits, and often a private language; they also share a bond that makes each exquisitely sensitive to the other's moods and routines. The question is whether anything remains to explain once those ordinary facts are counted — whether twins, tested under conditions where they cannot see, hear, or anticipate each other, score above chance. The laboratory answer, so far, is no, and the record gives both the stories and that answer.

History

Twin lore is old: folklore in many countries holds that twins share a soul, or a fate, or a channel of feeling. In the twentieth century the claim entered the popular press and then the research literature. Guy Lyon Playfair's book Twin Telepathy (2002) collected case reports of twins who seemed to know each other's experiences at a distance. Rupert Sheldrake ran telephone experiments in which a person tried to guess which of several callers was on the line, including tests with twins, and reported above-chance results. Controlled laboratory tests of twins, in which one twin was shown images or given mild stimuli while the other, isolated, tried to report them, have been run in several countries; the published results show no effect beyond chance, and the positive claims come from anecdote and from tests without adequate controls. The claim persists in the press and in popular belief, and it is one of the most common telepathy stories in circulation.

The experiment or case

A typical controlled test runs like this: one twin sits in a room and is shown a series of pictures — a boat, a tree, a house, a dog — chosen at random; the other twin sits in a separate room, unable to see or hear the first, and is asked at intervals to say which picture they feel their sibling is looking at. With four pictures, chance is one in four. In the published controlled tests, twins score at chance, just like unrelated pairs. The stories that circulate outside the laboratory look different: a twin who phones the moment the other is hurt, a twin who feels a pain in the same place as an injured sibling. Those stories are real experiences, sincerely reported, but they are also the experiences that get remembered and retold, while the thousands of times a twin felt nothing, or felt something that matched nothing, are forgotten. The laboratory test and the folk story are measuring different things: one measures accuracy under control, the other measures the vividness of a bond.

Methods

Take a pair of twins, preferably identical, and separate them so that neither can see, hear, or signal the other; Show one twin a randomly chosen image, word, or mild stimulus, and record the time and the choice; Ask the other twin, at the same time, to report any impression, image, or feeling, and record the answer before revealing the stimulus; Repeat the test many times with different stimuli, and score the matches against the chance baseline for the number of choices; Run control tests with unrelated pairs, or with the same twins tested at different times, for comparison; Compare the twins' scores with the controls, and ask whether any difference survives when the sample is large enough.

Results as reported

The case literature reports striking stories: twins who knew of each other's injuries at a distance, twins who shared dreams, twins who finished each other's thoughts. Playfair's collection presents dozens of such cases. Sheldrake's telephone experiments reported above-chance guessing about who was calling. The controlled laboratory tests report no effect: twins separated and tested with random stimuli score at chance, indistinguishable from unrelated pairs. The record reports both, and marks which is which.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics reply that the case stories are selected: a twin who phones at the moment of an injury is remembered, while the many calls that matched nothing are forgotten, and a twin who feels a pain will check on the sibling and so learn of an injury that might otherwise have gone unnoticed — a self-fulfilling loop. Sheldrake's telephone tests have been criticized for inadequate controls and for the file-drawer problem. The controlled laboratory tests, which remove the ordinary explanations, find nothing, and that is the pattern a null claim should show. The bond between twins is real; the evidence that it operates without the senses is not.

The Stargate program: the United States government's remote viewing research from the 1970s to 1995, and the official evaluation that ended it.

In the 1970s, the United States government spent millions of dollars on a question that sounds like science fiction: can a trained person describe a distant place they have never seen — a Soviet submarine base, a hostage location, a hidden facility — by mental means alone? The program was called Stargate, and its history is a matter of public record, because the government declassified it in 1995 and published its own evaluation. The problem this record addresses is what the program actually did, what it claimed, and what the government's own reviewers concluded. Remote viewing is not telepathy — it is clairvoyance, the claimed perception of a distant place rather than a distant mind — but it belongs in this archive because it was the largest government program ever run on a telepathy-adjacent claim, because its story is entangled with the Soviet mythology, and because its ending is the clearest official verdict the field has ever received.

History

The program began in the 1970s at the Stanford Research Institute (SRI) in California, where physicists Hal Puthoff and Russell Targ ran remote viewing experiments: a viewer in a laboratory tried to describe a target location that a second person was visiting, with the descriptions scored against the target and against control locations. The Defense Intelligence Agency and then the CIA funded the work, and in 1978 it was consolidated as the Stargate Project, run by the Army with a small team of viewers, including the former intelligence officer Joe McMoneagle. The program continued through the 1980s, with viewers tasked on operational questions, and it was reviewed several times. In 1995 the CIA commissioned the American Institutes for Research to evaluate it; the review, by Ray Hyman and Jessica Utts, concluded that remote viewing had not been shown to produce actionable intelligence and that the program's successes could be explained by ordinary means. The program was closed the same year, and its files were declassified. The SRI experiments and the Stargate files are now public.

The experiment or case

A standard SRI remote viewing trial ran like this: a target — say, a swimming pool complex — was chosen at random from a list of locations in the San Francisco area, and an experimenter drove out to visit it at an agreed time. In the laboratory, the viewer, who had been told nothing about the target, spent fifteen minutes describing their impressions: water, concrete, a large open space, the sound of splashing. The descriptions were recorded and later shown to a judge alongside photographs of the target and of several control locations; the judge ranked the photographs by how well they matched the description. In the published SRI experiments, the judges ranked the true target first more often than chance would predict, and Puthoff and Targ presented the results as evidence of remote viewing. In operational Stargate use, viewers described facilities and locations for intelligence questions, and the program's managers reported occasional striking matches — and many misses that were not reported with the same enthusiasm.

Methods

Choose a target location at random, and send a person to visit it while the viewer waits in the laboratory; Ask the viewer to describe the location — its shapes, its features, its feeling — and record the description word for word; Show the viewer a set of possible locations, including the true target and several controls, and ask them to rank the descriptions against the locations; Score the match blind, so that the judge does not know which location is the target; Repeat the procedure over many sessions, and compare the hit rate with the chance baseline for the number of choices; In operational use, task the viewer with a real question — a location, a facility, a person — and judge the description against what is later learned.

Results as reported

The SRI experiments reported that judges ranked the true target first more often than chance would predict, and Puthoff and Targ presented the results as evidence of remote viewing. The Stargate program's managers reported operational successes — viewers describing facilities and locations that later checked out. The 1995 evaluation reported the opposite conclusion: the American Institutes for Research found that remote viewing had not been shown to produce actionable intelligence, that the laboratory results had not been independently replicated, and that the operational successes were anecdotal. The program was closed in 1995.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics, including Hyman in the 1995 evaluation, reply that the SRI results came from a small number of trials with generous matching, that the viewers were selected for past success, and that the operational record was never scored completely, so the successes are a selected sample. Utts, the evaluation's other assessor, argued that the laboratory results were statistically significant and deserved further study — a disagreement the evaluation published in full, with both assessments side by side. The scientific consensus follows Hyman and the program's closure: a statistically interesting result in a small, unreplicated set of trials is not evidence of a real ability, and a program that cannot produce actionable intelligence after twenty years and millions of dollars has failed its own test. The Stargate files remain a valuable public record of what a government will fund when it fears the other side knows something it does not.

The Ganzfeld Debate: Hyman versus Honorton Rank 20 · complex

Evidence: historical-claim Historical claim: the debate and the joint communique are documented history, but the telepathy claim debated is unproven, and the record carries the skeptical analysis beside the reported hit rates.

The 1985-86 debate between Ray Hyman and Charles Honorton over the ganzfeld meta-analysis, and their joint communique — the field's most famous adversarial collaboration.

In 1985, the ganzfeld experiment — the field's best design — was put on trial in public, and the trial was conducted by its two ablest advocates: Charles Honorton, who had built the design and pooled its results, and Ray Hyman, a psychologist and skeptic who had spent twenty years criticizing parapsychology. The problem this record addresses is what they argued, what they agreed on, and why their argument matters more than the experiments themselves. The Hyman-Honorton debate is the most cited exchange in the history of telepathy research, not because it settled anything, but because it showed what a scientific argument looks like when both sides are competent, honest, and willing to state their standards in writing. It is also the record of a failure: the two men agreed on what a good test would look like, and the field then spent decades arguing about whether the tests run to those standards had succeeded.

History

The debate opened in 1985, when Honorton published his meta-analysis of twenty-eight ganzfeld studies in the Journal of Parapsychology, reporting a hit rate of about thirty-five percent against twenty-five percent chance. Hyman replied in the same journal with a detailed critique: he argued that the studies had flaws — inadequate randomization, possible sensory leakage, multiple analyses of the same data — and that the meta-analysis overstated the effect. Honorton answered, Hyman answered the answer, and in 1986 the two published a joint communique in the Journal of Parapsychology, setting out the standards they agreed a convincing ganzfeld study would have to meet: adequate randomization, automated target selection, blind judging, and pre-stated analyses. The communique was widely praised as a model of adversarial collaboration. Honorton then ran the autoganzfeld studies to those standards; Hyman and other critics argued that the autoganzfeld results did not replicate the effect cleanly; and the argument continued after Honorton's death in 1992, with later reviewers disagreeing about whether the agreed standards had been met and whether they had mattered.

The experiment or case

The joint communique of 1986 is the example. In it, Hyman and Honorton listed, point by point, what a ganzfeld study would have to do to be convincing: the target must be chosen by a random process the experimenter cannot influence; the sender and receiver must be shielded so that no ordinary cue can pass between them; the judging must be blind, so that the judge does not know which target is the true one; and the analysis must be stated before the data are examined, so that the researcher cannot choose the analysis that gives the best result. The communique did not say the existing studies had met these standards — Hyman said they had not, and Honorton said they had come close — but it said what meeting them would look like. It is the closest the field has ever come to a shared definition of proof, and it is still cited whenever a new ganzfeld study is judged.

Methods

Publish a meta-analysis with stated inclusion rules, pooling the studies and reporting the pooled hit rate; Publish a critique that re-examines the same studies, lists their flaws, and recomputes the pooled result with the flawed studies corrected or removed; Publish a reply that defends the original pooling and answers each criticism on its merits; Meet — in print, and in person where possible — to agree on the standards a future study would have to meet to convince both sides; Publish the agreed standards as a joint communique, so that later researchers and later critics can judge new work against them; Run new studies to the agreed standards, and let the results, not the argument, decide the question.

Results as reported

Honorton reported a pooled ganzfeld hit rate of about thirty-five percent across twenty-eight studies, against twenty-five percent chance. Hyman reported, after re-examination, that the studies had flaws that inflated the pooled rate, and that the corrected figure was lower and less secure. The joint communique reported no result; it reported an agreement on standards. The autoganzfeld studies that followed reported a hit rate of about thirty-two percent. The record reports all four figures and marks which is a result and which is an agreement.

Skeptical analysis

Skeptics reply that the debate, for all its quality, did not settle the question, because the standards the two men agreed on were then disputed in their application: every new study could be read as meeting them or as failing them, depending on who read it. Hyman himself later argued that the autoganzfeld studies did not cleanly replicate the effect, while Honorton's defenders argued that they did. The skeptical point is that an agreement on standards is not an agreement on results, and that a field which cannot agree on whether its own agreed standards have been met has a deeper problem than any single experiment can solve. The debate remains the field's best example of how to argue honestly — and of how honest argument can still end without a verdict.

Methods of testing telepathy: the experimental design — blinding, randomization, shielding, pre-registration, and replication — that separates a test from a story.

A century of telepathy research has produced one durable achievement: a clear account of what a good test looks like, learned by making every mistake first. The problem this record addresses is that account. Testing telepathy is hard, not because the mind is mysterious, but because the ordinary explanations are numerous and subtle: a glance, a sound, a pattern in a shuffled deck, a judge who knows the answer, a researcher who chooses the analysis after seeing the data. The record sets out the methods that close those gaps one by one — double blinding, true randomization, sensory shielding, fixed target pools, blind judging, pre-registration, and independent replication — and it explains why each one matters by showing what happened when it was missing. It is the most practical record in the archive, and the one that makes the rest of the archive readable: once you know what a good test looks like, you can judge every experiment in the archive for yourself.

History

The methods were learned in sequence. Rhine's card tests of the 1930s taught the field that hand shuffling and hand recording are not random and not reliable. The Soal exposure of the 1970s taught it that raw data must be preserved and shared. The ganzfeld debate of 1985-86 taught it that judging must be blind and analyses must be stated in advance, and the joint Hyman-Honorton communique wrote those lessons down as agreed standards. Bem's 2011 paper taught psychology as a whole that flexible analysis can manufacture significance, and the replication crisis that followed made pre-registration — fixing the methods and analyses before the data are collected — standard practice. The methods record is therefore a history of corrections, each one paid for by a failed experiment, and the current standard is the sum of those corrections: a pre-registered, double-blind, randomized, shielded, independently replicated test, with the raw data shared.

The experiment or case

The autoganzfeld is the example. In Honorton's Princeton laboratory, a computer chose the target clip at random, showed it to the sender, and recorded the receiver's rankings, with no experimenter handling the targets or the scores. The randomization was done by the computer, the judging was done by the receiver ranking clips the computer presented, and the analysis was the simple hit rate against twenty-five percent chance. The design closed the gaps the critics had found in the earlier ganzfeld studies: no hand shuffling, no experimenter cueing, no post-hoc choice of analysis. It did not close them all — the critics still questioned the target pools and the file drawer — but it showed what a test built to the agreed standards looks like, and it is the model this record holds up for judging every other experiment in the archive.

Methods

State the hypothesis and the analysis before collecting any data, and register them publicly so that they cannot be changed after the results are known; Choose the targets by a true random process — a tested random-number generator, not a hand shuffle — and keep the target secret from everyone who interacts with the receiver; Shield the sender and the receiver so that no ordinary cue — sound, sight, vibration, timing — can pass between them; Blind the judging: the person who scores the results must not know which target is the true one; Fix the target pool in advance, and make sure its members are equally matchable, so that no target is easier to guess than the others; Run the test with enough trials and enough participants for the result to mean something, share the raw data, and invite independent replication.

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'Statistics of Chance Expectation in Card Guessing'
trials = 25
choices = 5
seed = 2323
claimed_note = 'As reported and computed: 25 trials at 1 in 5 gives 5 hits expected (SD 2); Pearce-Pratt at 1,850 trials gave 558 hits against 370 expected, z about 11 — the arithmetic is exact, the data are disputed.'

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    hits = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        sent = rng.randrange(choices)
        guessed = rng.randrange(choices)
        if sent == guessed:
            hits += 1
    return hits

p = 1.0 / choices
expected = trials * p
sd = math.sqrt(trials * p * (1 - p))
hits_a = run_once()
hits_b = run_once()
z_a = (hits_a - expected) / sd
result = {"title": title, "trials": trials, "choices": choices,
          "seed": seed, "hits": hits_a, "expected": expected, "z": round(z_a, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Experiment: " + title)
print("Trials: %d  Choices per trial: %d  Chance probability: 1/%d" % (trials, choices, choices))
print("Chance expectation: %.1f hits in %d trials" % (expected, trials))
print("Simulated hits (random guessing, fixed seed %d): %d" % (seed, hits_a))
print("z-score: %.3f (hits minus expectation, divided by the chance standard deviation %.2f)" % (z_a, sd))
print("Claimed result as reported: " + claimed_note)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:
    print("Deterministic: MATCH - both runs produced the same simulated result.")
else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")
print("Note: this demo simulates random guessing only. It demonstrates the statistics of chance expectation; it cannot detect telepathy, and a high simulated hit count would be chance, not evidence.")

Math proof

Worked arithmetic: chance probability p = 1/5 = 0.2000. Chance expectation = 25 x 0.2000 = 5.0 hits. Chance standard deviation = sqrt(25 x 0.2000 x 0.8000) = 2.00. The demo's simulated run gave 7 hits, so z = (7 - 5.0) / 2.00 = 1.000.
