2. Math proof

A franchise is arithmetic before it is anything else. The upfront money is the initial fee multiplied by the number of locations: with a 40,000 fee and 10 locations, that is 40,000 times 10, which is 400,000. The ongoing money is the royalty rate multiplied by each location's sales: with a 6 percent royalty and 50,000 of monthly sales at one location, that is 0.06 times 50,000, which is 3,000 per month, or 36,000 per year from that location. With 10 locations at the same sales, the yearly royalty is 10 times 36,000, which is 360,000. A location breaks even for its owner when its sales cover its costs plus the royalty: if monthly costs are 30,000 and the royalty is 6 percent, the sales needed are 30,000 divided by 0.94, which is about 31,915 per month.

3. Science proof

A franchise works because a proven way of working can be copied: the same steps, the same standards, and the same checks, repeated in every location. People learn a routine faster than they invent one, customers trust a place that behaves the same way every time, and a written manual plus training plus regular checks keeps the copy faithful to the original. That is why franchising grows a business by teaching others to repeat what already works, instead of building every new location from scratch.

From One Online Shop to a Franchise Model

JAH-OFRCS-000001 — A franchise model starts with one online business that already works: steady sales, a clear product, and a way of working that can be written down and taught. The creator's first job is to prove the single shop works before asking anyone else to copy it. Signature edition: this is the creator's own version of the record, written fresh from the public record.

Math proof: The model is proven with one location first: if the original shop earns 50,000 a month in sales and keeps 10,000 after costs, its margin is 10,000 divided by 50,000, which is 0.20, or 20 percent. A second location copying the same model should earn near the same margin, and if it earns far less, the model is not yet ready to copy.

Science proof: A single working shop is the experiment that proves the model: one shop, measured honestly, shows whether the idea earns money before it is repeated. Copying an unproven shop multiplies its problems instead of its profits, so the proof must come first.

If a step depends on one person's talent or one local connection, it cannot be copied, and it does not belong in a franchise. Signature edition: this is the creator's own version of the record, written fresh from the public record.

Math proof: Teachability is counted in steps: if a routine has 12 steps and 11 can be written down plainly, it is 11 divided by 12, which is about 92 percent teachable. A routine below about 80 percent teachable needs simplifying before it can be franchised.

Science proof: People copy what they can see and follow: a written step, a picture, and a short practice run teach faster than a long lecture. A business whose work can be shown step by step can be taught to a stranger, and a business whose work lives only in one person's head cannot.

Royalties: A Worked Example

JAH-OFRCS-000004 — A royalty is the ongoing payment a franchisee makes, usually a small percentage of sales, in exchange for the brand, the system, and continuing support. A 6 percent royalty on 50,000 of monthly sales is 3,000 a month, and the same percentage keeps the payment fair when sales rise or fall. Signature edition: this is the creator's own version of the record, written fresh from the public record.

Math proof: The royalty is a percentage of sales: 0.06 times 50,000 is 3,000 per month, and 3,000 times 12 is 36,000 per year from one location. With 10 locations at the same sales, the yearly royalty is 10 times 36,000, which is 360,000. If sales double to 100,000, the royalty doubles to 6,000 a month, so both sides share the gain.

Science proof: A percentage royalty aligns both sides: when a location sells more, the franchisor earns more, and when it sells less, the payment falls, so the franchisor has a reason to keep helping locations sell. A flat monthly fee does not do this, because it stays the same whether the location thrives or struggles.

Designing a Franchise Territory

JAH-OFRCS-000005 — A territory is the area one franchisee serves, drawn so each location has enough customers without stealing sales from its neighbours. A good territory is drawn from real numbers: how many people live there, how far they will travel, and how much they spend. Signature edition: this is the creator's own version of the record, written fresh from the public record.

Math proof: A territory is sized by people and spending: if an area has 40,000 people and each spends 25 a year on this kind of product, the area's spending is 40,000 times 25, which is 1,000,000 a year. If one location needs 400,000 a year in sales to thrive, the area can support 1,000,000 divided by 400,000, which is 2.5, so two locations fit and a third would starve.

Science proof: People shop close to home: a shop that is a short trip away gets visited often, and one that is far away gets visited rarely. Drawing territories around how far customers will actually travel keeps each location busy without crowding its neighbours.
