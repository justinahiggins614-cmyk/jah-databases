Mecca, also spelled Makkah, is a city in western Saudi Arabia in the Makkah Province, in the Hejaz region along the Red Sea coast's inland side. Its published coordinates are approximately 21.42 degrees north latitude and 39.82 degrees east longitude, and it lies roughly 70 to 80 kilometres inland from the Red Sea, with published figures varying slightly by the reference point chosen. The city sits in a valley among hills, which shapes its street pattern, its drainage, and the way its built-up area has spread along valley floors rather than in a regular grid. Mecca is the capital of the Makkah Province, and it is the holiest city in Islam, a statement of religious significance recorded as a fact about the city's status in Islamic tradition and public reference works. The Hejaz is the western region of Saudi Arabia along the Red Sea, and Mecca stands within it as its best-known inland city. The region runs along the coastal plain and the mountain range behind it, and its historic towns include Jeddah on the coast, Mecca inland, and Medina to the north. Hejaz has long been a corridor for trade and for pilgrimage, because its ports receive travellers who then travel inland to Mecca, and its roads and later railways follow that same coast-to-interior pattern. The region's geography is therefore best read as a system: a port, an inland holy city, and the routes between them, rather than as isolated points on a map. Mecca is ringed by hills and mountains of the Hijaz range, and the city itself occupies valleys between them, which is why its neighbourhoods climb slopes and its main roads follow valley floors. Published topographic descriptions name three valleys in the immediate area, Taneem, Bakkah, and Abqar, and describe city elevations varying from roughly 300 to 900 metres above sea level across the wider area, with the central city at about 277 metres. The surrounding heights include Jabal al-Nour to the northeast and Jabal Thawr to the south, both named in published geographic and historical references. These mountains matter geographically because they channel rainfall runoff into the valleys, which is the physical reason flash flooding has been a recurring hazard despite low annual rainfall. This record is a fact record: the landforms and elevations are measurable geography, while any religious associations of particular mountains are tradition and are labelled as such in the tradition pair, not here.

Climate: A Hot Desert Climate Rank 4 · simple

Mecca has a hot desert climate, classified BWh in the Koppen-Geiger system, with high temperatures throughout the year and very low rainfall. Published climate tables give a mean daily maximum of about 38.1 degrees Celsius across the year, a daily mean of about 30.8 degrees, and a mean daily minimum of about 24.7 degrees, with summer daytime temperatures commonly above 40 degrees and winter days around 30 degrees with nights near 18 to 19 degrees. Annual precipitation in the published tables totals about 111.8 millimetres, falling mostly between November and January, while other published summaries give roughly 110 to 130 millimetres; the range is stated here because sources differ by station and period. The combination of intense heat, low rainfall, and occasional heavy downpours explains both the city's water history and its flood history. This record is a fact record based on published climate tables, and where two published totals differ, the record gives the range rather than choosing one figure silently.

Central Mecca stands at about 277 metres above sea level, while the wider built-up area climbs across hillsides to elevations reported between roughly 300 and 900 metres, so a single elevation figure describes only the centre and not the whole city. Drainage follows the valleys toward the Red Sea side, and the same slopes that give the city its dramatic setting also speed runoff during storms. Watercourses and Wadis Rank 6 · simple

This Signature edition record is an original Signature companion study for the same subject, written for this database and never presented as an authentic public record. It follows the public record's facts where they are stated, and it adds a Signature framing: how a reader can study, map, and compare the subject using only this database's own tools, demos, and cross-links. Mecca's natural drainage is by wadis, the dry or intermittently flowing valleys of arid Arabia, of which Wadi Ibrahim is the most important for the city because the Zamzam well draws groundwater from its alluvium and bedrock. For most of the year a wadi carries little or no surface water, but after rain it can carry a sudden, powerful flow, which is why wadi floors are both natural routes and natural flood paths. The groundwater beneath the wadi gravels is the city's historic water store, replenished by rainfall on the surrounding hills that drains into the valley floor and infiltrates through sand and gravel. Modern Mecca also manages storm water with dams and channels built after severe floods, including the serious flood of the early 1940s recorded in published flood histories. This record is a fact record about physical hydrology; the religious history of the well itself is covered in the Zamzam pair, where fact and tradition are separated.

Mecca is reached by road from Jeddah on the Red Sea coast, by the Haramain High Speed Railway linking Mecca with Jeddah, King Abdulaziz International Airport, and Medina, and by road corridors north to Medina and east toward Taif and Riyadh. Mina, Muzdalifah, and Arafat lie to the east of Mecca in a chain along the valley route out of the city, and their geography is the geography of the Hajj itself, since pilgrims move between them in a fixed sequence. Mina is the closest, a valley settlement a few kilometres east of Mecca, used as a tent city during Hajj; Muzdalifah lies further east between Mina and Arafat; Arafat is the furthest east, an open plain where pilgrims gather on the Day of Arafat. The ground rises gently eastward out of Mecca, the sites sit in open or semi-open terrain rather than in the tight central valley, and that openness is what allows very large temporary gatherings. The geology around Mecca is dominated by ancient crystalline basement rocks of the Arabian Shield, the Precambrian core of western Arabia, overlain in the valleys by younger alluvium of sand and gravel washed down from the hills. The Hijaz mountains are the eroded edge of that shield, uplifted along the Red Sea rift margin, which is why the terrain is steep, rocky, and cut by narrow valleys rather than broad plains. The valley alluvium matters far beyond its thickness, because it stores the groundwater that feeds wells including Zamzam, and its permeability lets rainfall infiltrate rather than running off entirely. Faults and fractures in the basement rock also guide groundwater movement at depth. This record is a fact record drawn from published geological summaries, and where a depth or thickness is given in the Zamzam pair it is given as a published range, not as a single exact figure.

Mecca's principal natural hazards are flash floods, extreme heat, and dust storms, in that order of documented impact, and all three follow directly from its desert valley geography. Flash floods are the most destructive: despite annual rainfall of only about 110 millimetres, rain falling on rocky hills runs quickly into the valleys, and published flood histories record 89 historic floods by 1965, a severe flood in the early 1940s, and destructive flooding in the wider Makkah Province in November 2009. Extreme heat is a chronic hazard, with summer days commonly above 40 degrees Celsius, and dust and sand storms, including shamal winds, reduce visibility and affect breathing, especially in summer. Dams and drainage works have been built to reduce flood risk, but the record does not claim the risk is removed.
