Skeleton clocks are open-framework clocks showing the movement: open plates, visible gear trains, and a visible escapement — not skeleton watches. JAH-SKELS-000001 — The Signature Meridian Open-Frame Timepiece (simple, Signature Designs)

Record

Specifications. The Signature Meridian is an original Signature open-frame timepiece, designed as the first study in this Signature series: a slim brass frame of two arched plates on turned pillars, a going barrel, a three-wheel train, and a deadbeat escapement, all visible beneath a glass dome on a walnut base. It stands 32 centimetres tall, runs eight days on one winding, and carries a chapter ring on four scroll feet. This is a Signature original design, not a copy of any public clock.

Mechanism. Power flows from the barrel through the centre wheel and third wheel to the escape wheel, where the deadbeat pallets release it in clean steps while the seconds pendulum below beats once per second. The Meridian's frame is pierced with a meridian-line motif, a thin engraved line running down the centre of each plate, so the eye follows the train from barrel to pendulum without interruption.

Maintenance. Wind the Meridian weekly, keep it under its dome, and level it with the base screws until the beats sound even. Oil only at the pivots at service, and dust the open wheels with a soft brush between services. The Signature Meridian is a Signature design, and its service record travels with it.

Build and restoration guidance. Building the Meridian starts with the frame: cut the arched plates, turn the pillars, and verify that every pivot hole lines up before any wheel is cut. Cut the train wheels to the stated counts, fit the deadbeat anchor, and test the escapement on a test stand before final assembly, so faults are found while the frame is still open.

Tools. Winding key, soft brush, spirit level, loupe, service logbook.

JAH-SKELS-000002 — The Signature Lattice Pillar Frame (simple, Signature Designs)

Record

Specifications. The Signature Lattice frame replaces solid plates with an open lattice of slim brass bars, joined at turned nodes, so the movement is visible from every angle and not only from the front. The lattice carries the same pivot positions as a conventional frame, but the bars are arranged as a repeating diamond pattern that stiffens the structure while removing most of its metal. This is an original Signature frame design.

Mechanism. The lattice works because triangulation is stiff: each diamond of bars braces its neighbours, so the frame resists flexing even though it is mostly air. Wheels run in pivot holes set into the lattice nodes, and the pendulum hangs from a bridge across the top nodes, keeping the escapement's geometry exact while the whole train stays visible from front, back and sides.

Maintenance. A lattice frame needs its nodes checked yearly for looseness, since a single loose joint lets a wheel go out of depth. Lift the clock by its base only, and never press on the lattice bars. Dust with a soft brush from several angles, since dust settles on bars that a front-only cleaning misses.

Build and restoration guidance. Building a lattice frame means jigging every node position before joining, and verifying the pivot alignment with a test arbour through each pair of holes. A bar that is even slightly out of line throws its wheel out of true, so the frame is proved on the bench before the train is fitted.

Tools. Jig, pivot test arbours, loupe, spirit level, soft brush.

JAH-SKELS-000003 — The Signature Three-Train Open Chimer (simple, Signature Designs)

Record

Specifications. The Signature Three-Train Open Chimer is an original Signature design with three visible trains side by side: going, hour strike, and quarter chime, each on its own barrel, each with its own wheels exposed. Mechanism. At the quarter, the going train releases the chime train, which plays its quarter pattern on the rods, and at the hour the strike train adds the hour count on the bell. Maintenance. Wind all three trains weekly and never let one run down while the others run, since a released train with no power jams its warning. If the chime and strike fall out of step, let both finish their cycles before correcting, and check the release levers through the open frame.

Build and restoration guidance. Building the chimer means timing the three trains together on the bench: the chime barrel must complete its pattern before the strike rack drops, and both must lock up cleanly. Test with the frame open and the dome off, watching every lever, before the clock is cased.

Tools. Winding key, loupe, strike-test logbook, oiler, soft brush.

JAH-SKELS-000005 — The Signature Twin-Barrel Long-Duration Skeleton (simple, Signature Designs)

Record

Specifications. The Signature Twin-Barrel skeleton is an original Signature design that runs thirty days on one winding by dividing the power between two going barrels, wound together through a single square. The twin barrels sit side by side in the open frame, their great wheels meshing with a shared centre wheel, and the clock stands 36 centimetres tall under a dome.

Mechanism. Two barrels halve the load on each spring and halve the torque variation each barrel sees, so the train receives steadier power across the month. The barrels turn in step, and the viewer can watch them creep together while the escape wheel steps on, a slow duet that lasts thirty days between windings.

Maintenance. Wind the twin barrels monthly on a fixed date, turning the key steadily until both clicks hold. Never wind one barrel alone: the shared centre wheel needs both springs pulling evenly, and a single wound barrel overdrives its side of the train.

Build and restoration guidance. Building the twin-barrel movement means matching the two springs for strength and the two barrels for tooth count, then proving that both barrels deliver equal torque before the train is fitted. A mismatch shows up as uneven barrel rotation through the open frame.

Tools. Winding key, mainspring winder, loupe, service logbook, soft brush.

JAH-SKELS-000007 — The Signature Rotating Cage Escapement Study (compounded, Signature Designs)

Record

Specifications. The Signature Rotating Cage study is an original Signature experimental design: the escapement sits in a small rotating cage that turns once a minute, carrying the balance of errors around with it so that positional differences average out over each rotation. It is a study piece, built to explore the idea in an open frame where the cage's rotation is fully visible, not a claim about any public clock.

Mechanism. The cage carries the escape wheel and pallets around a fixed axis while the train drives the cage itself through a subsidiary wheel. As the cage turns, the escapement's orientation cycles through every position, and the viewer watches the cage complete its slow rotation while the escapement ticks inside it.

Maintenance. A rotating-cage clock needs scrupulous balance: the cage must be poised so its rotation is even, and the driving wheel must not load the escapement. Service intervals are shorter than for a fixed escapement, and the cage pivots are oiled only by a specialist, since excess oil unbalances the cage.

Build and restoration guidance. Building the cage means machining it light and true, poising it on a balance, and proving its rotation period on the bench before it is fitted to the train. Any wobble in the cage shows immediately through the open frame and must be corrected before rating begins.

Tools. Poising tool, loupe, cage jig, oiler, rating logbook.

JAH-SKELS-000012 — The Signature Visible Remontoire Study (complex, Signature Designs)

Record

Specifications. The Signature Visible Remontoire study is an original Signature experimental design that adds a small remontoire, a secondary spring rewound at short intervals by the main train, between the train and the escapement, so the escapement receives power in equal small doses rather than the main train's varying pull. The remontoire spring and its fly are mounted in the open frame where their rewinding cycle is visible.

Mechanism. Every thirty seconds, the main train rewinds the remontoire spring, and the spring alone drives the escape wheel between rewinds. The viewer watches the remontoire fly spin briefly at each rewind and the escape wheel tick steadily between them, a two-rhythm display that shows constant-force timekeeping in action.

Maintenance. A remontoire clock needs its rewind interval checked at service, since a remontoire that rewinds late starves the escapement and one that rewinds early wastes power. Oil the remontoire pivots only at service, and never adjust the remontoire spring's tension by hand.

Build and restoration guidance. Building the remontoire means proving the rewind cycle on the bench: the main train must rewind the small spring fully within its interval without disturbing the escapement, and the fly must govern the rewind speed. Test over many cycles before the escapement is rated.

Tools. Remontoire jig, loupe, oiler, rating logbook, fly gauge.
