Table Saw Rank 1 · simple · sawing · intermediate

The table saw is the workshop's central ripping and crosscutting machine: a circular blade rises through a flat table, and the workpiece is guided past it by a rip fence or a miter gauge. A standard 10-inch blade at about 4,800 revolutions per minute gives a fast, clean cut in sheet goods and solid lumber, and the fence must be parallel to the blade or the cut will bind. This guide covers a first safe practice project, a simple pine shelf board ripped to width and crosscut to length, with the blade guard and riving knife in place throughout. Never reach over a spinning blade, never cut freehand without fence or gauge, and always use a push stick when the fence is within six inches of the blade. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

10-inch blade, 3 horsepower motor, rip capacity 30 inches to the right of the blade, maximum cut depth 3-1/8 inches at 90 degrees

  • Blade: 10 in diameter, 40-tooth general-purpose blade
  • Motor: 3 hp, about 4,800 rpm
  • Blade tip speed: about 12,566 ft/min at 4,800 rpm
  • Rip capacity: 30 in
  • Maximum depth of cut: 3.125 in at 90 degrees, 2.25 in at 45 degrees
  • Kerf: 0.118 in (3 mm)

Materials:

  • Practice board (pine, 3/4 inch)
  • Push stick (plywood offcut, 1/2 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the practice cut; the shelf is joined later with screws

Tolerances: Rip width within 1/32 inch of target; squareness within 1/64 inch over 12 inches

Arithmetic note: Tip speed checks out: circumference pi x diameter times rpm, divided by 12 inches per foot. The Signature edition presents the same arithmetic with its own Signature method check. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Unplug the saw, fit the 40-tooth blade, and set the riving knife and guard in place.
  2. Set the blade height so the highest tooth sits about 1/8 inch above the board surface.
  3. Set the rip fence to the shelf width and check it parallel to the blade with a square.
  4. With eye and hearing protection on, start the saw, wait for full speed, and feed the pine board steadily with a push stick past the blade.
  5. Crosscut the ripped board to length with the miter gauge, keeping hands clear of the blade path.
  6. Switch off, wait for the blade to stop completely, then measure the finished shelf board.

Tools required

  • Tape measure
  • Pencil
  • Combination square
  • Table Saw (the tool of this guide)
  • Clamps
  • Push stick or featherboard where applicable

Safety gear:

  • Safety glasses
  • Hearing protection
  • Dust mask

Safety notes: Wear eye protection and hearing protection. Keep hands clear of the blade or cutter path, keep guards in place, use a push stick or featherboard where fitted, disconnect power before changing a blade or bit, and never defeat a guard or reach over a spinning blade.

Miter Saw Rank 2 · simple · sawing · beginner

The miter saw makes accurate crosscuts and angled cuts: the blade head pivots down onto a workpiece held against a fence, and the head can swing left and right for miters and tilt for bevels. A 12-inch sliding miter saw cuts boards up to about 14 inches wide, and its detents lock at common angles such as 45 and 90 degrees. This guide covers cutting picture-frame miters at 45 degrees, a forgiving first project that teaches angle setup, clamping, and measuring. Keep hands at least six inches from the blade path, let the blade reach full speed before lowering it, and let it stop before raising the head. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Circular Saw Rank 3 · simple · sawing · intermediate

The circular saw is a portable 7-1/4-inch saw that brings the cut to the workpiece, and with a straightedge guide it breaks down full plywood sheets accurately. Its base plate rides on the sheet while the blade cuts from below, so the good face goes down to keep tear-out on the hidden side. This guide covers breaking down a plywood sheet into shelf panels with a clamped straightedge, the single most useful circular-saw skill. The blade guard must snap back after every cut, the cord must stay behind the saw, and the sheet must be fully supported so the offcut cannot pinch the blade. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

7-1/4-inch blade, 15 amp motor, maximum cut depth 2-7/16 inches at 90 degrees, bevel capacity 57 degrees

  • Blade: 7.25 in diameter, 24-tooth framing blade (40-tooth for plywood)
  • Motor: 15 amp, about 5,800 rpm
  • Blade tip speed: about 11,005 ft/min at 5,800 rpm
  • Maximum depth: 2.4375 in at 90 degrees
  • Bevel: 0 to 57 degrees
  • Kerf: 0.094 in

Materials:

  • Sheet stock (birch plywood, 3/4 inch)
  • Straightedge guide (aluminum or plywood strip, 1/2 inch)

Fasteners: Shelf panels joined later with 1-1/4 inch screws

Tolerances: Guided cut straight within 1/32 inch over 48 inches; panel width within 1/16 inch

Arithmetic note: Tip speed checks out: circumference pi x diameter times rpm, divided by 12 inches per foot. The Signature edition presents the same arithmetic with its own Signature method check. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Support the plywood sheet on sawhorses so the cut line hangs free and the offcut cannot pinch.
  2. Measure the distance from blade to base-plate edge and clamp the straightedge that far from the cut line.
  3. Set the blade depth to about 1/4 inch below the sheet.
  4. With the good face down, start the saw clear of the sheet, reach full speed, and push steadily along the guide.
  5. Let the blade stop before setting the saw down, then check the panel width.
  6. Repeat for each shelf panel, re-clamping the guide for every cut.

Jigsaw Rank 4 · simple · sawing · beginner

The jigsaw cuts curves and interior openings with a thin reciprocating blade, and it is the safest powered saw to learn on because the blade is small and the cut is slow and visible. Blade choice decides the result: fine-tooth blades for clean curves in thin stock, coarse blades for fast rough cuts. This guide covers cutting a rounded-corner shelf bracket from pine, including drilling a starter hole for an interior cut. Keep the shoe flat on the work, let the blade do the cutting rather than forcing it, and never put fingers under the workpiece near the blade. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

Variable-speed motor, orbital action settings 0 to 3, maximum cut depth 3-3/8 inches in wood, stroke length 1 inch

  • Blade: T-shank, 10 teeth per inch for curves in 3/4 in stock
  • Stroke: 1 in, orbital settings 0-3
  • Maximum wood depth: 3.375 in
  • Speed: variable, up to about 3,000 strokes per minute
  • Bevel: shoe tilts 0 to 45 degrees

Materials:

  • Bracket blank (pine, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: Bracket mounted later with 1-1/2 inch screws

Tolerances: Curve within 1/16 inch of the marked line; bracket corners matched as a pair

Arithmetic note: Grit sequence checks out: every step is coarser-to-finer in strictly ascending order. The Signature edition presents the same arithmetic with its own Signature method check. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Mark the rounded corner on the pine blank and clamp it to the bench with the curve overhanging.
  2. Fit a 10-teeth-per-inch curve blade and set the orbital action to 1 for a clean cut.
  3. Start the saw clear of the work, reach speed, and guide the blade along the line without pushing sideways.
  4. Slow down on tight curves and back out of the cut if the blade binds.
  5. Sand the sawn edge through 120-grit, then 180-grit, and check the bracket against its pair.
  6. Test-fit the bracket under a shelf before mounting.

Band Saw Rank 5 · simple · sawing · intermediate

The band saw runs a continuous loop blade around two wheels, and it is the right machine for resawing boards into thinner pieces and for cutting tight curves that a jigsaw cannot follow cleanly. Blade width sets the minimum curve radius: a 1/4-inch blade turns a radius of about 5/8 inch, while a 1/2-inch blade suits straight resawing. This guide covers resawing a pine board into two book-matched panels, the classic band-saw practice piece. The blade guard must sit just above the work, hands must never cross the blade line, and the work must be fed with the fence or a featherboard, never freehand against a tall fence. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

14-inch wheels, 1 horsepower motor, resaw capacity 6 inches (with riser block 12 inches), table tilts 0 to 45 degrees

  • Blade: 1/2 in wide, 3 teeth per inch hook tooth for resawing
  • Wheel diameter: 14 in
  • Blade speed: about 3,000 ft/min
  • Resaw capacity: 6 in standard, 12 in with riser block
  • Minimum curve radius for 1/4 in blade: about 0.625 in

Materials:

  • Resaw blank (pine, 1-1/2 inches)
  • Featherboard (hardwood offcut, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: Book-matched panels joined later with glue only

Tolerances: Resawn panels within 1/32 inch thickness of each other; cut face flat within 1/64 inch

Arithmetic note: Kerf waste checks out: cut count times kerf width, subtracted from the starting length. The Signature edition presents the same arithmetic with its own Signature method check. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Fit the 1/2-inch, 3-teeth-per-inch blade and tension it per the saw's gauge.
  2. Set the blade guard just above the pine blank and square the fence to the blade.
  3. Mark the resaw line around the blank and start the saw.
  4. Feed steadily against the fence with a push stick, keeping hands clear of the blade line.
  5. Plane or sand the sawn faces lightly, then open the two panels like a book to check the match.
  6. Measure both panels and keep the pair together for the project.

Router Rank 6 · compounded · shaping · intermediate

The router spins a shaped bit at 20,000 revolutions per minute or more and cuts profiles, grooves, dados, and edge treatments that no saw can make. Direction matters: feeding against the bit's rotation gives control, while feeding with the rotation (climb cutting) can snatch the workpiece. This guide covers routing a round-over edge on a pine shelf, the most common first router job, using a bearing-guided bit that follows the edge. The bit must be seated fully in the collet, the workpiece must be clamped, and the router must be switched off and still before it is set down. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

2-1/4 horsepower motor, variable speed 10,000 to 25,000 rpm, collet sizes 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch, plunge depth 2-1/2 inches

  • Bit: 1/4 in radius round-over, 1/2 in shank, bearing-guided
  • Speed for 1 in diameter bit: about 18,000 rpm
  • Motor: 2.25 hp, variable 10,000-25,000 rpm
  • Pass depth for edge profile: full profile in one pass on 3/4 in stock
  • Feed rate: about 60 in per minute for softwood edge work

Materials:

  • Shelf board (pine, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: Shelf mounted later with screws

Tolerances: Round-over profile even along the full edge; no burn marks deeper than surface discoloration

Arithmetic note: Feed time checks out: cut length divided by the feed rate in inches per minute. The Signature edition presents the same arithmetic with its own Signature method check. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Fit the 1/4-inch round-over bit with at least 3/4 inch of shank in the collet and tighten firmly.
  2. Set the bearing to ride on the shelf edge and test the depth on an offcut.
  3. Clamp the pine shelf to the bench and put on eye and hearing protection.
  4. Start the router clear of the work, reach full speed, and feed against the bit rotation along the edge.
  5. Make a second light pass if any burn marks appear, then sand the edge with 180-grit.
  6. Check the profile by running a fingertip along the full edge.

  • Push stick or featherboard where applicable

Planer Rank 7 · compounded · surfacing · intermediate

The thickness planer shaves the top face of a board to a uniform thickness, turning rough lumber into flat, even stock. It removes only a small amount per pass, about 1/16 inch in hardwood and 1/8 inch in softwood, and it must never be used on a board shorter than the manufacturer's minimum length, because short boards can kick back. This guide covers planing a rough pine board down to a clean 3/4-inch thickness for a shelf. Always plane with the grain, support long boards at both ends, and never reach into the machine while the cutterhead is spinning. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

13-inch width capacity, 6-inch maximum board thickness, two-knife cutterhead at about 10,000 rpm, feed rate about 26 feet per minute

  • Width capacity: 13 in
  • Maximum cut per pass: 0.0625 in hardwood, 0.125 in softwood
  • Cutterhead: 2 knives, about 10,000 rpm
  • Feed rate: about 26 ft/min
  • Target thickness: 0.75 in from 1 in rough (4 passes at 1/16 in)

How to

  4. Measure thickness after each pass and stop at 3/4 inch.
  5. Sand the planed faces lightly with 150-grit to remove cutter marks.

Jointer Rank 8 · compounded · surfacing · advanced

The jointer flattens one face and squares one edge of a board, which is the first step before the planer can make the opposite face parallel. It is an advanced machine because the cutterhead is exposed between the infeed and outfeed tables, and hands must always ride on the outfeed side once the cut starts. This guide covers jointing one face and one edge of a twisted pine board until it sits flat without rocking. Use push blocks, never bare hands over the cutterhead, and never joint stock shorter than 12 inches or thinner than 1/2 inch. The Signature edition presents its own method for this topic: a Signature three-point check (measure twice against the project plan, test on an offcut, and verify the finished piece against the stated tolerances) is applied at every step, and any ratings shown are the Signature edition's own presentation, labeled signature-original, never presented as an authentic public record.

Specifications

6-inch width capacity, 3-knife cutterhead, table length 46 inches, fence tilts 0 to 45 degrees

  • Width capacity: 6 in
  • Cutterhead: 3 knives
  • Depth per pass: 0.0625 in maximum for flattening
  • Table length: 46 in
  • Fence: 90 degrees for edge jointing, tilts to 45 degrees

Materials:

Fasteners: No fasteners; jointed board planed and joined later

Tolerances: Face flat within 0.005 inch over 24 inches; edge square within 0.25 degrees

Arithmetic note: Board feet check out: thickness x width x length in inches divided by 144, times the board count. The Signature edition presents the same arithmetic with its own Signature method check. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  2. Set the jointer depth to 1/16 inch and fit the guard over the cutterhead.
  3. Joint the concave face first, pressing down on the outfeed table with push blocks once the cut starts.
  4. Repeat light passes until the face no longer rocks.
  5. Joint one edge against the 90-degree fence until it is straight and square to the face.
  6. Mark the jointed face and edge as reference faces for the planer.
