The Standard Model JAH-FPT-000001 · simple

The Standard Model is the theory of the known particles and forces: six quarks, six leptons, and the force carriers — photon, W and Z bosons, gluons — plus the Higgs boson, found in 2012.

The Standard Model is the theory of the known particles and forces: six quarks, six leptons, and the force carriers — photon, W and Z bosons, gluons — plus the Higgs boson, found in 2012. It predicts experimental results to extraordinary precision, yet it does not explain dark matter, dark energy, gravity, or why the particles have the masses they do. It is the most successful theory in physics and, by its own admission, incomplete.

Quarks JAH-FPT-000002 · intermediate

Quarks are the building blocks of protons and neutrons: up and down quarks make ordinary matter, while the heavier strange, charm, bottom and top quarks appear only in high-energy collisions.

Quarks are the building blocks of protons and neutrons: up and down quarks make ordinary matter, while the heavier strange, charm, bottom and top quarks appear only in high-energy collisions. Quarks carry fractional electric charge and colour charge, and they are never found alone — confinement binds them into hadrons. The top quark, the heaviest known particle at about 173 GeV, was found at Fermilab in 1995.

Gauge bosons carry the forces: photons carry electromagnetism, W and Z bosons carry the weak force, and gluons carry the strong force. The Higgs boson, found at CERN in 2012, is the quantum of the Higgs field, which gives mass to the W and Z bosons and to the fermions. Without it, the Standard Model's equations would require massless particles, contradicting experiment. Its mass is 125 GeV, and its discovery completed the Standard Model while opening new questions: the Higgs mass is unnaturally light, which suggests new physics may stabilize it.

Neutrinos JAH-FPT-000006 · advanced

Neutrinos come in three flavours — electron, muon and tau — and oscillate between them as they travel, which proves they have mass.

Neutrinos come in three flavours — electron, muon and tau — and oscillate between them as they travel, which proves they have mass. They are produced in the Sun, in supernovae, in reactors and in cosmic-ray showers, and they are detected in huge underground tanks such as Super-Kamiokande in Japan and IceCube at the South Pole. The remaining questions are their mass ordering, whether they are their own antiparticles, and whether they violate CP symmetry.

Particle accelerators push charged particles to near light speed with electric fields and steer them with magnets. The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN collides protons at 13-14 TeV, recreating energies last seen a fraction of a second after the Big Bang. A cross section measures how likely a collision outcome is, in units of area — the barn, where one barn is 10^-28 square metres, and particle physics works in femtobarns, a millionth of a billionth of a barn. The Higgs production cross section at the LHC is about 50 picobarns, so with the LHC's luminosity it is made about once a second. Cross sections are the bridge between theory and counted events.

Feynman diagrams, introduced by Richard Feynman in 1948, are pictures of particle interactions: lines for particles, vertices where they meet, and each diagram corresponding to a term in a calculation. They turned quantum field theory from pages of algebra into drawings a physicist can read. Every collision at the LHC is understood as a sum of such diagrams, and the diagrams themselves are the calculation.

QCD and Confinement JAH-FPT-000018 · advanced

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the theory of the strong force: quarks carry colour charge and exchange gluons, which themselves carry colour, so the force grows stronger with distance.

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the theory of the strong force: quarks carry colour charge and exchange gluons, which themselves carry colour, so the force grows stronger with distance. That is confinement — quarks can never be pulled free, and trying only creates new quark pairs. At the extreme temperatures of the early universe, or of a heavy-ion collision, confinement melts into a quark-gluon plasma.

Electroweak theory unifies electromagnetism and the weak force: at high energies they are one force, and at low energies the Higgs field breaks the symmetry, giving mass to the W and Z while the photon stays massless. CP Violation JAH-FPT-000024 · advanced

CP violation is the asymmetry between matter and antimatter: some particle decays happen at slightly different rates for particles and antiparticles.

CP violation is the asymmetry between matter and antimatter: some particle decays happen at slightly different rates for particles and antiparticles. It was found in kaons in 1964 (Nobel Prize 1980) and in B mesons at BaBar and Belle, and it is part of the Standard Model — but far too small to explain why the universe is made of matter. A larger source of CP violation, perhaps in neutrinos, is one of the great open questions.
