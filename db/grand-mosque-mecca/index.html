Mosque Overview Rank 1 · simple

The Grand Mosque, Masjid al-Haram, is the mosque that encloses the Kaaba in the centre of Mecca, and it is the largest mosque in the world by published area and capacity. Its historic core covers about 356,000 square metres, while the total built-up area after the Third Saudi Expansion is published as 1.47 million square metres including courtyards, bridges, terraces, and service buildings. Published capacity figures run from about 820,000 after the King Fahd expansion, through about 1.5 million in many summaries and about 1.85 million after the Third Saudi Expansion according to Saudipedia, up to 3.0 million in the widest infobox count. The mosque operates around the clock, and its main elements are the Mataf around the Kaaba, the prayer halls and courtyards, the Masa'a gallery, the minarets, and the gates. Entry to Mecca is restricted to Muslims, and that restriction applies to the mosque. This pair describes each element in its own record, so this overview can stay short and point onward.

Courtyards and Prayer Halls Rank 2 · simple

The prayer space of the Grand Mosque is not one hall but a system of halls, roofed areas, and open courtyards arranged around the central Mataf. The Third Saudi Expansion breakdown published by Saudipedia gives a sense of the scale: the expansion building itself covers 320,000 square metres for 300,000 worshippers, the courtyard covers 175,000 square metres for 280,000 worshippers, and the eastern terraces cover 263,000 square metres for more than 150,000 worshippers, with service buildings adding room for more than 310,000. Older parts of the mosque, including the Ottoman arcades, sit inside this larger system, and the King Fahd wing added a further large prayer area reached through the King Fahd Gate. Floors are largely marble and artificial stone, and the halls are arranged so that rows face the Kaaba from every direction. This record describes spaces and capacities only, and it contains no depiction of any person.

Minarets Rank 3 · simple

The Grand Mosque's minarets are its vertical markers, visible across the city and used for the call to prayer. Published counts differ by era: after the King Fahd expansion the mosque had nine minarets, seven older ones plus two added, while current infobox listings give thirteen minarets with the tallest at 139 metres. Both figures are reported here with their eras, because a single count without a date would mislead. The minarets were raised in stages across the Saudi expansions, and the tallest belong to the most recent works. Their architectural role is orientation and sound: they mark the mosque from a distance and carry loudspeakers for the call to prayer across the complex. This record gives counts and heights only from published summaries, and where a height is given for a single tallest minaret, it is labeled as the published tallest, not as the height of every minaret.

Domes and Roof Rank 4 · simple

The roof of the Grand Mosque tells its building history in layers. Early roofing was flat and wooden, and the Ottoman works of the early 1570s replaced it with domes carried on additional columns, with a stone arcade added around the courtyard. That Ottoman layer is described in published histories as the oldest remaining part of the modern structure. Later Saudi works added further roofing, including the King Fahd expansion's three domes positioned at gates, and the modern complex mixes domed sections, flat roofed halls, retractable and fixed canopies over courtyards, and open sky over the Mataf. The roof must do several jobs at once: shade worshippers from intense sun, shed rare heavy rain, carry lighting and sound equipment, and allow air to move through a building that holds hundreds of thousands of people. This record reports the dated works and the roof types, and it does not claim a complete structural survey, which is not in the public record used here.

Mataf and Tawaf Area Rank 5 · simple

The Mataf is the open area around the Kaaba where pilgrims perform tawaf, circling the Kaaba seven times counterclockwise. It is the centre of the whole complex, and every other space, hall, courtyard, and gallery, is arranged around it. Modern works have widened and leveled the Mataf, added multiple levels and ramps so that circling can happen on more than one floor, and separated flows so that entering and leaving streams do not collide head-on. Published throughput figures for the complex as a whole, with Umrah hourly capacity rising from about 44,000 to about 118,000 after the Third Saudi Expansion, show why the Mataf is engineered as a flow system rather than as a simple open floor. The rite itself, seven circuits, is a fact about practice, and the meanings attached to it are reported as tradition in the pilgrimage records.

Safa and Marwa Rank 6 · simple

Expansions Through History Rank 7 · compounded

The Grand Mosque has been enlarged in stages for more than a thousand years, and the published sequence is as follows. Early enlargements under the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphs added ground to the north and east, and the Abbasid caliph al-Mahdi's expansion around 783 added about 12,512 square metres after neighbouring houses were acquired. For the next eight centuries the mosque remained largely unchanged apart from restoration, until the Ottoman works of the early 1570s replaced the flat wooden roof with domes and a stone arcade, bringing the area to about 28,003 square metres. The first Saudi enlargement began in 1955 and completed in 1973, growing the area from about 27,000 to about 152,000 square metres and raising capacity to about 500,000. The King Fahd expansion, foundation stone laid on 14 September 1988, brought the area to about 356,000 square metres for up to 1.5 million worshippers in published summaries of that era. The King Abdullah expansion, launched in 2011 and inaugurated in 2015, brought the total built-up area to 1.47 million square metres and capacity to about 1.85 million by Saudipedia's count. The history pair gives the wider city timeline, and this record gives the mosque sequence only.

MECCA_LAT = 21.3891
MECCA_LON = 39.8579
def qibla(lat, lon):
    p1 = math.radians(lat); p2 = math.radians(MECCA_LAT)
    d = math.radians(MECCA_LON - lon)
    y = math.sin(d) * math.cos(p2)
    x = math.cos(p1) * math.sin(p2) - math.sin(p1) * math.cos(p2) * math.cos(d)
    b = (math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x)) + 360.0) % 360.0
    a = math.sin((p2 - p1) / 2.0) ** 2 + math.cos(p1) * math.cos(p2) * math.sin(d / 2.0) ** 2
    dist = 6371.0 * 2.0 * math.asin(math.sqrt(a))
    return b, dist
