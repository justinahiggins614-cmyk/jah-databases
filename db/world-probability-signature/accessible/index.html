What Probability Is

Probability is the mathematics of chance: it assigns numbers between 0 and 1 to events, measuring how likely they are. A probability of 0 means the event cannot happen, 1 means it certainly happens, and 1/2 means it is as likely as not. Probability turns uncertainty into something that can be calculated, compared, and used to make decisions in gambling, insurance, science, and everyday life.

Roll a fair die 60 times: each face is expected about 10 times, because 60 times 1/6 = 10. In one real run the counts might be 9, 11, 8, 12, 10, 10, which add to 60 and hover near 10 each. The probability of rolling an even number is 3/6 = 1/2, because three of the six faces are even. The probability of rolling a 7 is 0, because no face shows 7. The probabilities of all six faces add to 6 times 1/6 = 1, as they must.

Sample Spaces and Events

A sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment, and an event is a subset of the sample space — a collection of outcomes we care about. Tossing two coins has the sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}, and the event of exactly one head is {HT, TH}. Every probability question starts by writing down the sample space and the event.

Toss two coins: the sample space is {HH, HT, TH, TT}, four equally likely outcomes. The event of exactly one head is {HT, TH}, with probability 2/4 = 1/2. The event of at least one head is {HH, HT, TH}, with probability 3/4. The complement of at least one head is {TT}, with probability 1/4, and 3/4 + 1/4 = 1, as it must. Roll two dice: the sample space has 36 pairs, and the event of a total of 7 is {(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)}, six outcomes, so its probability is 6/36 = 1/6.

The Rules of Probability

The rules of probability are the axioms and their consequences: probabilities lie between 0 and 1, the probability of the whole sample space is 1, the probability of the complement is 1 minus the probability of the event, and for mutually exclusive events the probability of either is the sum of the probabilities. The addition rule for overlapping events subtracts the intersection, so nothing is counted twice.

Roll a die: P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2 and P(greater than 4) = 2/6 = 1/3. The events overlap at {6}, so P(even or greater than 4) = 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3, and indeed the outcomes are {2, 4, 5, 6}, four of six. The complement rule gives P(not a 6) = 1 - 1/6 = 5/6. For three events the inclusion-exclusion principle adds three singles, subtracts three pairs, and adds back the triple: with P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 1/2 and pairwise intersections 1/4 and triple intersection 1/8, the union is 3/2 - 3/4 + 1/8 = 7/8.

Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another event has happened, written P(A|B) and read as the probability of A given B. It is the probability of A among the outcomes where B holds: P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B). Conditioning narrows the sample space to B and renormalises, which is how new information changes probabilities.

Draw two cards from a deck without replacement. The probability the first is an ace is 4/52. Given the first is an ace, the probability the second is an ace is 3/51, because three aces remain among 51 cards. So P(both aces) = 4/52 times 3/51 = 12/2652 = 1/221, about 0.0045. A medical test example: if 1 in 1,000 people has a disease and the test is 99 percent accurate, the probability of having the disease given a positive test is not 99 percent — it is about 9 percent, because false positives from the 999 healthy people outnumber the true positives. Conditioning on the positive test changes everything, and the base rate matters.

Bayes' Theorem

Bayes' theorem reverses conditional probability: it computes P(A|B) from P(B|A), P(A), and P(B). In its common form, P(A|B) = P(B|A) times P(A) / P(B). It tells us how to update a belief when new evidence arrives: the prior probability P(A) is multiplied by the likelihood P(B|A) and divided by the total probability of the evidence. It is the foundation of Bayesian statistics.

A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test is 99 percent sensitive (it catches 99 percent of cases) and has a 1 percent false positive rate. The prior is 0.001. The likelihood of a positive test given the disease is 0.99. The total probability of a positive test is 0.001 times 0.99 + 0.999 times 0.01 = 0.00099 + 0.00999 = 0.01098. So the posterior is 0.00099 / 0.01098 = 0.0902, about 9 percent. A positive result raises the probability from 0.1 percent to 9 percent — a large increase, but still far from certainty, because the disease is rare and false positives are common.

Independence

Two events are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other: P(A and B) = P(A) times P(B), or equivalently P(A|B) = P(A). Coin tosses are independent: the result of one toss tells you nothing about the next. Independence is the assumption behind most elementary probability, and also the assumption most often violated in real life.

Toss a coin twice: P(heads then heads) = 1/2 times 1/2 = 1/4, because the tosses are independent. Roll two dice: P(double six) = 1/6 times 1/6 = 1/36. But draw two cards without replacement: P(two aces) = 4/52 times 3/51 = 1/221, not (4/52)^2 = 1/169, because the first draw changes the deck. The gambler's fallacy says that after five heads in a row, tails is due — but the sixth toss is still 1/2 heads, because the tosses are independent and the coin has no memory.

Random Variables

A random variable is a number assigned to each outcome of a random experiment: the number rolled on a die, the number of heads in three tosses, the height of a randomly chosen person. It turns outcomes into numbers, so that probability can use arithmetic. A discrete random variable takes separate values with probabilities; a continuous one takes values in a range, with probabilities given by a density.

Let X be the number of heads in three coin tosses. The sample space has 8 outcomes: HHH has X = 3, HHT, HTH, THH have X = 2, HTT, THT, TTH have X = 1, and TTT has X = 0. So P(X = 0) = 1/8, P(X = 1) = 3/8, P(X = 2) = 3/8, and P(X = 3) = 1/8, and these add to 1. The expected value is 0 times 1/8 + 1 times 3/8 + 2 times 3/8 + 3 times 1/8 = 12/8 = 1.5, which is 3 times 1/2, as it should be for three tosses.

Expectation and Variance

The expectation, or mean, of a random variable is its long-run average: the sum of each value times its probability. The variance measures the spread around the mean: the expected squared distance from the mean, and its square root is the standard deviation. Expectation tells you where the values centre; variance tells you how far they typically stray, and together they summarise any random variable in two numbers.

For a fair die, the expectation is (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5. The variance is the average of (1-3.5)^2, (2-3.5)^2, (3-3.5)^2, (4-3.5)^2, (5-3.5)^2, (6-3.5)^2, which are 6.25, 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25, 6.25, summing to 17.5, divided by 6 to give 35/12, about 2.92. The standard deviation is the square root of 35/12, about 1.71. For the number of heads in three tosses, the expectation is 1.5 and the variance is 3/4, because each toss contributes 1/4 and variances add for independent tosses.

The Binomial Distribution

The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials, each with the same probability p of success. If X is the number of successes in n trials, then P(X = k) = C(n, k) times p^k times (1-p)^(n-k), where C(n, k) is the number of ways to choose k successes from n trials. It is the most used distribution in statistics.

Toss a fair coin 10 times. The probability of exactly 5 heads is C(10, 5) times (1/2)^10 = 252/1024, about 0.246. The probability of at least 8 heads is the sum for k = 8, 9, 10: (45 + 10 + 1)/1024 = 56/1024, about 0.0547. The mean is 10 times 1/2 = 5 and the variance is 10 times 1/2 times 1/2 = 2.5. In quality control, if 5 percent of items are defective, the probability that a batch of 20 has no defectives is 0.95^20, about 0.358.

The Normal Distribution

The normal distribution, or bell curve, is the most important continuous distribution: it is symmetric about its mean, with most values near the mean and fewer far away. Its density is a bell-shaped curve fixed by two numbers, the mean mu and the standard deviation sigma. About 68 percent of values lie within one standard deviation of the mean, 95 percent within two, and 99.7 percent within three.

Adult male heights are approximately normal with mean 175 cm and standard deviation 7 cm. A height of 182 cm has z = (182 - 175)/7 = 1, so about 16 percent of men are taller than 182 cm, because about 68 percent lie within one standard deviation, leaving 16 percent above and 16 percent below. A height of 189 cm has z = 2, so about 2.5 percent are taller. A height of 196 cm has z = 3, so about 0.15 percent are taller. The probability of a height between 168 and 182 cm, within one standard deviation, is about 68 percent.

The Poisson Distribution

The Poisson distribution counts events that happen randomly over time or space, when they are rare and independent: the number of calls to a call centre in an hour, the number of typos on a page, the number of radioactive decays in a second. If the average rate is lambda, then P(X = k) = e^(-lambda) times lambda^k / k!. Its mean and variance are both lambda.

A call centre averages 6 calls an hour, so lambda = 6. The probability of exactly 4 calls is e^(-6) times 6^4 / 4! = 0.002479 times 1296 / 24 = 0.1339, about 13 percent. The probability of no calls is e^(-6) = 0.00248, about 0.25 percent. The probability of more than 10 calls is 1 minus the sum for k = 0 to 10, which is about 0.084. The mean is 6 and the variance is 6, so the standard deviation is about 2.45. If the rate doubles to 12, the probability of exactly 12 calls is e^(-12) times 12^12 / 12!, about 0.114.

The Law of Large Numbers

The law of large numbers says that the average of many independent trials tends to the expected value as the number of trials grows. Flip a coin 10 times and the proportion of heads may be 0.7; flip it 10,000 times and the proportion will be very close to 0.5. The weak law says the proportion converges in probability; the strong law says it converges almost surely.

Simulate 10,000 fair coin tosses: after 10 tosses the proportion of heads might be 0.7, after 100 it might be 0.54, after 1,000 it might be 0.512, and after 10,000 it is typically within 0.01 of 0.5. The standard deviation of the proportion after n tosses is sqrt(0.25/n): after 100 tosses it is 0.05, after 10,000 it is 0.005, so the average is ten times more precise with a hundred times more data. A casino with a 1 percent edge on a bet expects to win 1 percent of the total wagered over many bets, and the law of large numbers makes that expectation a near-certainty.

The Central Limit Theorem

The central limit theorem says that the average of many independent random variables is approximately normally distributed, whatever the original distribution. Roll one die and the result is uniform; average 30 dice and the average is bell-shaped, centred on 3.5 with a standard deviation of about 0.31. It is the reason the normal distribution appears everywhere, and the foundation of statistical inference.

Roll one die: the mean is 3.5 and the standard deviation is about 1.71. Average 30 dice: the mean of the average is still 3.5, but its standard deviation is 1.71/sqrt(30) = 0.312, so the average is almost always between 2.9 and 4.1. The probability that the average of 30 dice exceeds 4 is about the probability that a standard normal exceeds (4 - 3.5)/0.312 = 1.60, which is about 0.055. For 100 dice the standard deviation of the average is 0.171, and the probability of exceeding 4 is the probability that a standard normal exceeds 2.92, about 0.0018. Averages concentrate fast.

Markov Chains

A Markov chain is a sequence of random states in which the next state depends only on the current state, not on the whole history. The weather tomorrow depends on the weather today, but given today, yesterday adds nothing: that is the Markov property. A chain is described by its transition probabilities, and its long-run behaviour is described by its stationary distribution.

A simple weather chain: if today is sunny, tomorrow is sunny with probability 0.8 and rainy with 0.2; if today is rainy, tomorrow is sunny with 0.4 and rainy with 0.6. Starting sunny, the chance of sun two days later is 0.8 times 0.8 + 0.2 times 0.4 = 0.64 + 0.08 = 0.72. The stationary distribution solves pi_sunny = 0.8 pi_sunny + 0.4 pi_rainy with pi_sunny + pi_rainy = 1, giving pi_sunny = 2/3 and pi_rainy = 1/3: in the long run, two days in three are sunny. A gambler's ruin chain has absorbing states at 0 and at the target, and the chain ends when it is absorbed.

The Monty Hall Problem

The Monty Hall problem is a probability puzzle: three doors hide one car and two goats. You pick a door. The host, who knows where the car is, opens a different door to reveal a goat, and offers you the chance to switch. Switching wins the car with probability 2/3, while staying wins with probability 1/3 — so you should always switch. The answer surprises most people, including many mathematicians.

Suppose you pick door 1. If the car is behind door 1 (probability 1/3), the host opens door 2 or 3, and switching loses. If the car is behind door 2 (probability 1/3), the host must open door 3, and switching wins. If the car is behind door 3 (probability 1/3), the host must open door 2, and switching wins. So switching wins in two of the three cases, with probability 2/3, and staying wins with probability 1/3. In a simulation of 10,000 games, switching wins about 6,667 times and staying about 3,333 times.

The Birthday Paradox

The birthday paradox says that in a group of just 23 people, the probability that two share a birthday is already over 50 percent. It feels paradoxical because 23 seems far too small, but the number of pairs is what matters: 23 people make 253 pairs, and each pair has a 1-in-365 chance of matching. With 70 people the probability is over 99.9 percent.

For 23 people, the probability of no shared birthday is (365/365) times (364/365) times ... times (343/365), which equals about 0.4927, so the probability of a match is 1 - 0.4927 = 0.5073, just over half. For 30 people the match probability is about 0.706. For 50 people it is about 0.970, and for 70 it is about 0.99916. The approximation 1 - e^(-23^2/730) = 1 - e^(-0.7247) = 0.5155, close to the true 0.5073. By contrast, the chance that someone shares your birthday needs about 253 people for 50 percent.

Gambler's Ruin

The gambler's ruin problem asks for the probability that a gambler, starting with i dollars and betting 1 dollar at a time with win probability p per bet, reaches N dollars before losing everything. If p is 1/2, the answer is i/N. If p is less than 1/2, the answer is much smaller: (1 - ((1-p)/p)^i) / (1 - ((1-p)/p)^N). Even a small disadvantage compounds into near-certain ruin over a long run.

A gambler starts with 10 dollars, aims for 20, and bets 1 dollar at a time on a fair coin. The chance of reaching 20 before 0 is 10/20 = 1/2. But if the coin is slightly biased, with p = 0.49, then r = 0.51/0.49 = 1.0408, and the chance is (1 - 1.0408^10)/(1 - 1.0408^20) = (1 - 1.490)/(1 - 2.221) = 0.490/1.221 = 0.401, already below half. With a target of 100 and the same p, the chance falls to about 0.017: a 1 percent disadvantage against a tenfold target means ruin 98.3 percent of the time.

Probability in Games of Chance

Games of chance are probability made visible: dice, cards, roulette, and lotteries are all random experiments with known sample spaces and computable odds. The house edge is the expected loss per bet, and it is built into the payout: a roulette bet on red pays even money but wins with probability 18/38, so the expected return is -2/38, about -5.26 cents per dollar. Understanding the odds does not beat the game, but it explains why the house always wins.

American roulette has 38 pockets: 18 red, 18 black, 0, and 00. A bet of 1 dollar on red wins 1 dollar with probability 18/38 and loses 1 dollar with probability 20/38, so the expected return is 18/38 - 20/38 = -2/38, about -0.0526 dollars. A lottery with 6 numbers from 49 has C(49, 6) = 13,983,816 possible tickets, so the chance of the jackpot with one ticket is about 1 in 14 million. A poker hand of five cards has C(52, 5) = 2,598,960 possibilities, and a royal flush has 4 of them, so its probability is 4/2,598,960, about 1 in 649,740.

Risk and Decision Making

Risk is probability times consequence: a small chance of a large loss can matter more than a large chance of a small one. Decision making under uncertainty weighs the expected value of each option, and also the variance, the worst case, and the decision maker's attitude to risk. Expected value alone is not enough: most people prefer a certain 450 dollars to a 50 percent chance of 1,000 dollars, even though the gamble has the higher expectation.

A choice: take 450 dollars for certain, or a 50 percent chance of 1,000 dollars and a 50 percent chance of nothing. The gamble's expected value is 500 dollars, higher than 450, yet most people take the certain 450: they are risk averse, and the utility of the extra 50 dollars is worth less than the risk of getting nothing. A flood defence example: a defence costing 10 million dollars that prevents a flood with probability 0.01 per year and damage of 2 billion dollars has expected annual benefit 0.01 times 2 billion = 20 million dollars, so it pays for itself in expectation in six months — but the decision also depends on the variance and on who bears the risk.

Probability in Science

Science runs on probability: measurement error is random, experiments are designed with randomisation, and theories are tested with statistical tests. Quantum mechanics is probabilistic at its core: it predicts probabilities, not certainties. Statistical mechanics explains temperature and pressure as averages over countless random molecular motions. Probability is not a sideline of science; it is the language in which scientific uncertainty is stated.

The Higgs boson discovery required a significance of five sigma, meaning the probability that the signal was a fluke was about 1 in 3.5 million. A clinical trial with 1,000 patients per arm and a true effect of 10 percent has a standard error of about sqrt(0.1 times 0.9 / 1000) = 0.0095, so the effect is measured to about plus or minus 2 percent. A measurement of the gravitational constant with a standard deviation of 0.0001 gives a 95 percent confidence interval of plus or minus 0.0002. In quantum mechanics, an electron passing through two slits lands with probabilities given by the interference pattern, and no single landing can be predicted.

Simulation and Monte Carlo Methods

Monte Carlo methods solve problems by random sampling: instead of computing an integral or a probability exactly, simulate the random process many times and average the results. The law of large numbers guarantees the average converges to the answer, and the central limit theorem says the error falls as 1/sqrt(n). It is how pi is estimated by throwing darts, how integrals are computed in high dimensions, and how financial risk is measured.

Estimate pi by throwing darts: a unit square contains a quarter circle of radius 1, whose area is pi/4, so the fraction of darts landing inside estimates pi/4. With 10,000 darts, about 7,854 land inside, giving pi about 3.1416, with a standard error of about sqrt(0.785 times 0.215 / 10000) times 4, about 0.016. With 1,000,000 darts the error falls to about 0.0016. A financial example: the value at risk of a portfolio is estimated by simulating thousands of market scenarios and taking the 5th percentile of the losses.

The Prosecutor's Fallacy

The prosecutor's fallacy is the mistake of confusing P(evidence|innocence) with P(innocence|evidence): the probability of seeing this evidence if the defendant is innocent is not the probability that the defendant is innocent given the evidence. A DNA match with a random-match probability of 1 in a million does not mean the probability of innocence is 1 in a million — the base rate matters, and Bayes' theorem is what connects the two.

A DNA profile matches 1 in a million random people. In a city of 1 million people, about one innocent person is expected to match, besides the true source. If the police found the suspect through the DNA database alone, with no other evidence, then there are about two matching people — the culprit and one innocent — so the probability that this suspect is the culprit is about 1/2, not 1 - 1/1,000,000. With a prior of 1 in 1,000,000 and a match, Bayes' theorem gives a posterior of about 1/2. The prosecutor's fallacy would claim the probability of innocence is 1 in a million; the correct figure, with no other evidence, is about 1 in 2.

A History of Probability

The history of probability runs from dice games in the 1650s to the mathematics of risk, science, and machine learning today. It began as the theory of games of chance, became the theory of measurement error and statistics in the 1700s and 1800s, and was given its modern axiomatic form by Kolmogorov in 1933. It is a young subject by mathematical standards, and it is still growing fast.

The timeline is short: 1654 for the Pascal-Fermat letters, 1657 for Huygens's book, 1713 for Bernoulli's Ars Conjectandi, 1733 for de Moivre's normal curve, 1809 for Gauss's theory of errors, 1812 for Laplace's Théorie analytique, 1933 for Kolmogorov's axioms, and 1949 for the first Monte Carlo paper. From the first letters to the axioms is 279 years. Bayes's essay was published in 1763, two years after his death. The word probability comes from the Latin probabilis, meaning worthy of approval — a reminder that probability began as a judgement, not a calculation.

Probability in Everyday Life

Probability is part of everyday life: weather forecasts, medical tests, insurance, lotteries, and games all run on it. A 70 percent chance of rain, a 1-in-14-million lottery, a 99 percent accurate test — these are probability statements, and understanding them means understanding base rates, expected value, and the difference between a single event and a long run. Probability does not remove uncertainty; it measures it.

A lottery ticket costs 2 dollars and the jackpot is 10 million dollars with a chance of 1 in 14 million. The expected value of the jackpot alone is 10,000,000/14,000,000, about 0.71 dollars — less than the ticket price, before smaller prizes are counted. A weather forecast of 70 percent rain means that in 100 days with the same forecast, it rained on about 70. A car insurance premium of 800 dollars a year reflects an expected claim of perhaps 600 dollars plus costs: the insurer's expected profit is the difference. A medical test with 99 percent accuracy for a disease affecting 1 in 1,000 people gives a positive result that means only about a 9 percent chance of disease.
