What Probability Is

ID: JAH-PRBBS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-probability-signature#JAH-PRBBS-000001 k=64f3c37e

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a counting ladder from one die roll to the axioms, with the 60-roll example as the running case.

Definition: Probability is the mathematics of chance: it assigns numbers between 0 and 1 to events, measuring how likely they are. A probability of 0 means the event cannot happen, 1 means it certainly happens, and 1/2 means it is as likely as not. Probability turns uncertainty into something that can be calculated, compared, and used to make decisions in gambling, insurance, science, and everyday life.

History: Probability began with games of chance. In the 1650s Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat exchanged letters about how to divide the stakes in an interrupted game of dice, and in doing so they founded the theory of probability. Christiaan Huygens wrote the first book on the subject in 1657. Jacob Bernoulli's Ars Conjectandi, published in 1713, proved the first law of large numbers. Abraham de Moivre connected probability to the normal curve in the 1730s. Pierre-Simon Laplace's Théorie analytique des probabilités of 1812 organised the subject, and in 1933 Andrey Kolmogorov gave probability its modern axiomatic foundation, defining it in terms of measure theory. Today probability underlies statistics, finance, physics, and machine learning.

Key concepts: The key ideas are probability, event, sample space, outcome, fair. A probability is a number between 0 and 1 assigned to an event. The probability of rolling a 6 on a fair die is 1/6, about 0.167. An event is a set of outcomes: rolling an even number is the event {2, 4, 6} on a die. The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. For a coin toss it is {heads, tails}; for a die it is {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. An outcome is one possible result of a random experiment, like rolling a 4 or drawing the ace of spades. A die or coin is fair when every outcome is equally likely. A fair die gives each face probability 1/6. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, describe the experiment and list every possible outcome in the sample space. Second, identify the event as the set of outcomes that count as success. Third, count the outcomes, or use symmetry, to assign probabilities. Fourth, check that all probabilities are between 0 and 1 and add up to 1. Fifth, test the assignment against real trials or a simulation. Worked examples: Roll a fair die 60 times: each face is expected about 10 times, because 60 times 1/6 = 10. In one real run the counts might be 9, 11, 8, 12, 10, 10, which add to 60 and hover near 10 each. The probability of rolling an even number is 3/6 = 1/2, because three of the six faces are even. The probability of rolling a 7 is 0, because no face shows 7. The probabilities of all six faces add to 6 times 1/6 = 1, as they must.

Real applications: Weather forecasts are probabilities: a 70 percent chance of rain means that in similar conditions it rained 70 times out of 100. Insurance is priced with probability: premiums are set from the probability of claims, estimated from large amounts of past data. Medicine uses probability to weigh treatments: a drug's benefit and its side effects are both stated as probabilities. Games, lotteries, and casinos are designed with probability: the house edge is a small probability advantage built into every bet.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is probability just guessing? No: probability is exact mathematics about uncertainty. It does not predict a single toss, but it predicts the pattern over many trials with great precision. What does a 50 percent chance mean? It means the event is as likely as not — like a fair coin landing heads. It does not mean it will happen every other time. Can probability be 0 or 1? Yes: 0 means impossible in the model, and 1 means certain. Most real events sit in between. Why do probabilities add to 1? Because something in the sample space must happen: the probabilities of all mutually exclusive outcomes cover every possibility, so they sum to 1.

Sample Spaces and Events

ID: JAH-PRBBS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-probability-signature#JAH-PRBBS-000002 k=b0dba96e

Definition: A sample space is the set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment, and an event is a subset of the sample space — a collection of outcomes we care about. Tossing two coins has the sample space {HH, HT, TH, TT}, and the event of exactly one head is {HT, TH}. Every probability question starts by writing down the sample space and the event.

History: Sample spaces were implicit in the earliest work on dice: Pascal and Fermat in the 1650s counted outcomes to solve the problem of points. Huygens in 1657 wrote about expectations over equally likely cases. Jacob Bernoulli in 1713 made the sample space explicit in his Ars Conjectandi, and Laplace in 1812 defined probability as the ratio of favourable cases to all possible cases, assuming them equally likely. Kolmogorov's axioms of 1933 turned the sample space into a mathematical set with a measure on it, and the event into a measurable subset. Today every probability model begins with the same two objects: a sample space and the events defined on it.

Key concepts: The key ideas are sample space, event, complement, union, intersection. The sample space, usually written Omega, is the set of all possible outcomes. For two coin tosses it is {HH, HT, TH, TT}. An event is a subset of the sample space. The event of exactly one head in two tosses is {HT, TH}. The complement of an event is everything in the sample space that is not in the event. The complement of rolling a 6 is rolling 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5. The union of two events is the set of outcomes in either event. The union of even and greater than 4 on a die is {2, 4, 5, 6}. The intersection of two events is the set of outcomes in both. The intersection of even and greater than 4 on a die is {6}. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, describe the random experiment precisely. Second, list every possible outcome, without missing any or double-counting. Third, write the event as the subset of outcomes that satisfy the condition. Fourth, check the sample space is complete and the event is inside it. Fifth, use set operations — union, intersection, complement — to build complex events. Worked examples: Toss two coins: the sample space is {HH, HT, TH, TT}, four equally likely outcomes. The event of exactly one head is {HT, TH}, with probability 2/4 = 1/2. The event of at least one head is {HH, HT, TH}, with probability 3/4. The complement of at least one head is {TT}, with probability 1/4, and 3/4 + 1/4 = 1, as it must. Roll two dice: the sample space has 36 pairs, and the event of a total of 7 is {(1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1)}, six outcomes, so its probability is 6/36 = 1/6.

Real applications: Quality control defines a sample space of inspected items and an event of defective items, then estimates the defect probability. Genetics uses sample spaces of offspring genotypes: a Punnett square is a sample space of four equally likely combinations. Card games are sample spaces: the 52 cards, the 1,326 two-card hands, and the events like pairs and flushes are all sets of outcomes. Software testing treats program paths as a sample space and bugs as events, then measures how often each path is exercised.

Common questions and misconceptions: Must the sample space list everything? Yes: if an outcome is missing, probabilities will not add to 1, and the model is wrong. Completeness is the first check. Can an event be empty? Yes: the empty event has probability 0, and the whole sample space has probability 1. They are the extremes. What if outcomes are not equally likely? Then counting does not work directly: each outcome needs its own probability, and the event's probability is the sum of its outcomes' probabilities. Are HT and TH different outcomes? Yes, when the coins are distinguishable: HT means the first coin is heads and the second tails, which is a different outcome from TH.

The Rules of Probability

ID: JAH-PRBBS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-probability-signature#JAH-PRBBS-000003 k=2860a5c0

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a rules ladder from the complement to inclusion-exclusion, with the even-or-greater-than-4 example as the running case.

Definition: The rules of probability are the axioms and their consequences: probabilities lie between 0 and 1, the probability of the whole sample space is 1, the probability of the complement is 1 minus the probability of the event, and for mutually exclusive events the probability of either is the sum of the probabilities. The addition rule for overlapping events subtracts the intersection, so nothing is counted twice.

History: The rules were used implicitly by Pascal, Fermat, and Huygens in the 1600s, and stated as principles by Laplace in 1812. Boole's algebra of logic in the 1850s gave set operations their modern form. Kolmogorov's Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung of 1933 set out three axioms: non-negativity, normalisation, and countable additivity. Everything else — the complement rule, the addition rule, the inclusion-exclusion principle — follows from those three. The axioms are deliberately minimal: they say nothing about what probability means, only how it must behave, which is why they serve every interpretation, from frequencies to degrees of belief.

Key concepts: The key ideas are axiom, complement rule, addition rule, mutually exclusive, inclusion-exclusion. An axiom is a starting assumption. Kolmogorov's three axioms are: probabilities are non-negative, the whole sample space has probability 1, and the probability of a union of disjoint events is the sum. The complement rule says P(not A) = 1 - P(A). If the chance of rain is 0.3, the chance of no rain is 0.7. The addition rule says P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B). The subtraction corrects for outcomes counted twice. Two events are mutually exclusive when they cannot both happen: they have no outcomes in common, so their intersection is empty. Inclusion-exclusion generalises the addition rule to many events: add the singles, subtract the pairs, add back the triples, and so on. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, check that every probability is between 0 and 1. Second, check that the probabilities of all outcomes add to 1. Third, for a union, add the probabilities and subtract the overlap. Fourth, for a complement, subtract from 1. Fifth, verify the answer by counting outcomes in the sample space. Worked examples: Roll a die: P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2 and P(greater than 4) = 2/6 = 1/3. The events overlap at {6}, so P(even or greater than 4) = 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3, and indeed the outcomes are {2, 4, 5, 6}, four of six. The complement rule gives P(not a 6) = 1 - 1/6 = 5/6. For three events the inclusion-exclusion principle adds three singles, subtracts three pairs, and adds back the triple: with P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 1/2 and pairwise intersections 1/4 and triple intersection 1/8, the union is 3/2 - 3/4 + 1/8 = 7/8.

Real applications: Risk assessment adds the probabilities of distinct failure modes, then corrects for overlaps where one cause triggers another. Medical screening uses the complement rule: the probability of a false alarm is 1 minus the probability of a correct result. Polling combines the probabilities of mutually exclusive voter groups to get the total support for a candidate. Engineering reliability adds the probabilities of component failures, with inclusion-exclusion for shared causes.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why subtract the intersection? Because outcomes in both events are counted in P(A) and in P(B). Without subtracting, they are counted twice and the total can exceed 1. Can two probabilities add to more than 1? Only if the events overlap: P(A) + P(B) can exceed 1, but P(A or B) can never exceed 1, because it is a probability. What if events are not mutually exclusive? Use the full addition rule with the subtraction term. Mutual exclusivity is the special case where the intersection is empty and the subtraction is zero. Do the axioms say what probability means? No: they say how probabilities must behave. Frequency, symmetry, and belief are interpretations that all obey the same axioms.

Conditional Probability

ID: JAH-PRBBS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-probability-signature#JAH-PRBBS-000004 k=a8294b37

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a conditioning ladder from cards to the medical test, with the 1-in-1,000 example as the running case.

Definition: Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another event has happened, written P(A|B) and read as the probability of A given B. It is the probability of A among the outcomes where B holds: P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B). Conditioning narrows the sample space to B and renormalises, which is how new information changes probabilities.

History: Conditional probability was used by Bayes in the 1760s and by Laplace, who made it central to his theory. The definition as a ratio is due to the early 20th century formalisation, and Kolmogorov's axioms of 1933 made it rigorous. The idea is older than the formula: gamblers knew that the odds change when some cards are already on the table. In the 20th century conditional probability became the language of statistics, machine learning, and medical testing, where every result is a probability given the evidence. The Monty Hall problem and the prosecutor's fallacy both turn on getting conditioning right.

Key concepts: The key ideas are conditional probability, conditioning, multiplication rule, independent events, Bayes' theorem. P(A|B) is the probability of A given that B has happened. It equals P(A and B) divided by P(B). Conditioning means restricting attention to the outcomes where the given event holds, and rescaling probabilities to add to 1 there. The multiplication rule says P(A and B) = P(A|B) times P(B). It is the definition of conditional probability rearranged. Two events are independent when conditioning does nothing: P(A|B) = P(A). The result of one coin toss does not affect the next. Bayes' theorem reverses conditioning: it computes P(A|B) from P(B|A) and the base rates. It is the subject of the next record but one. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, identify the conditioning event B and restrict the sample space to it. Second, find the probability of A and B together. Third, divide by the probability of B. Fourth, check the answer is between 0 and 1 and agrees with counting inside B. Fifth, ask whether the conditioning genuinely changes the probability, or the events are independent. Worked examples: Draw two cards from a deck without replacement. The probability the first is an ace is 4/52. Given the first is an ace, the probability the second is an ace is 3/51, because three aces remain among 51 cards. So P(both aces) = 4/52 times 3/51 = 12/2652 = 1/221, about 0.0045. A medical test example: if 1 in 1,000 people has a disease and the test is 99 percent accurate, the probability of having the disease given a positive test is not 99 percent — it is about 9 percent, because false positives from the 999 healthy people outnumber the true positives. Conditioning on the positive test changes everything, and the base rate matters.

Real applications: Medical testing is conditional probability: a positive result means the probability of disease given the test, not the accuracy of the test. Spam filters compute the probability a message is spam given its words, updating as each word arrives. Insurance pricing conditions on risk factors: the probability of a claim given age, history, and location. Card games and sports betting are conditioning in action: the odds change with every card dealt and every goal scored.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is P(A|B) the same as P(B|A)? No, and confusing them is the prosecutor's fallacy. The probability of a positive test given the disease is not the probability of the disease given a positive test. What if P(B) is zero? Then P(A|B) is undefined: you cannot condition on an impossible event, because there is nothing to restrict to. Does conditioning always change the probability? No: if A and B are independent, P(A|B) = P(A), and the information is irrelevant. Why divide by P(B)? Because the probabilities inside B must add to 1. Dividing by P(B) rescales them so the restricted sample space has total probability 1.

Bayes' Theorem

ID: JAH-PRBBS-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-probability-signature#JAH-PRBBS-000005 k=7c8e00c3

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds an updating ladder from prior to posterior, with the 1-in-1,000 test as the running example.

Definition: Bayes' theorem reverses conditional probability: it computes P(A|B) from P(B|A), P(A), and P(B). In its common form, P(A|B) = P(B|A) times P(A) / P(B). It tells us how to update a belief when new evidence arrives: the prior probability P(A) is multiplied by the likelihood P(B|A) and divided by the total probability of the evidence. It is the foundation of Bayesian statistics.

History: Thomas Bayes, an 18th-century English minister, wrote an essay on the problem in the 1760s, published after his death in 1763 by Richard Price. Pierre-Simon Laplace developed the idea independently and used it widely, calling it the principle of inverse probability. For much of the 20th century Bayesian methods were controversial, because they require a prior probability, and frequentists objected to its subjectivity. The rise of computing changed that: Markov chain Monte Carlo methods made Bayesian computation practical, and today Bayes' theorem powers spam filters, medical diagnosis, machine learning, and the search for lost aircraft and spacecraft. The theorem itself is a one-line consequence of the definition of conditional probability.

Key concepts: The key ideas are prior, likelihood, posterior, evidence, base rate. The prior P(A) is the probability before seeing the evidence. It is the starting belief that the evidence updates. The likelihood P(B|A) is the probability of the evidence if the hypothesis is true. It measures how well the hypothesis predicts the evidence. The posterior P(A|B) is the probability after seeing the evidence. It is what Bayes' theorem computes. The evidence P(B) is the total probability of what was observed, across all hypotheses. It normalises the posterior. The base rate is the prior frequency of the thing in question. Ignoring it is the base rate fallacy, and it is why a 99 percent accurate test can still be wrong 9 times out of 10. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, state the prior probability of the hypothesis before the evidence. Second, find the likelihood: the probability of the evidence if the hypothesis is true. Third, find the total probability of the evidence, across all hypotheses. Fourth, multiply prior by likelihood and divide by the evidence to get the posterior. Fifth, check the posterior is between 0 and 1 and compare it with the prior. Worked examples: A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test is 99 percent sensitive (it catches 99 percent of cases) and has a 1 percent false positive rate. The prior is 0.001. The likelihood of a positive test given the disease is 0.99. The total probability of a positive test is 0.001 times 0.99 + 0.999 times 0.01 = 0.00099 + 0.00999 = 0.01098. So the posterior is 0.00099 / 0.01098 = 0.0902, about 9 percent. A positive result raises the probability from 0.1 percent to 9 percent — a large increase, but still far from certainty, because the disease is rare and false positives are common.

Real applications: Spam filters are Bayesian: each word updates the probability that a message is spam, and the filter acts when the posterior crosses a threshold. Medical diagnosis uses Bayes' theorem to combine test results with base rates, so that rare diseases are not over-diagnosed. Search and rescue uses Bayes' theorem: the search for Air France flight 447 in 2009 used Bayesian updating to narrow the search area. Machine learning is largely Bayesian: models update their beliefs as data arrives, exactly as the theorem prescribes.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is Bayes' theorem controversial? The theorem is not: it is a mathematical identity. The controversy is about the prior — where it comes from and whether it is allowed to be subjective. What is the base rate fallacy? It is ignoring the prior: treating the accuracy of a test as the probability of the disease, when the rarity of the disease matters just as much. Can Bayes' theorem be used twice? Yes: the posterior from one piece of evidence becomes the prior for the next. Updating is sequential, and the order does not matter. What if the prior is zero? Then the posterior stays zero, whatever the evidence: a hypothesis given no prior chance can never be revived. That is Cromwell's rule — never assign probability zero to anything that is not logically impossible.

Independence

ID: JAH-PRBBS-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-probability-signature#JAH-PRBBS-000006 k=eb4b3b4d

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a multiplication ladder from coins to cards, with the two-aces contrast as the running example.

Definition: Two events are independent when the occurrence of one does not change the probability of the other: P(A and B) = P(A) times P(B), or equivalently P(A|B) = P(A). Coin tosses are independent: the result of one toss tells you nothing about the next. Independence is the assumption behind most elementary probability, and also the assumption most often violated in real life.

Definition: The expectation, or mean, of a random variable is its long-run average: the sum of each value times its probability. Definition: The law of large numbers says that the average of many independent trials tends to the expected value as the number of trials grows.
