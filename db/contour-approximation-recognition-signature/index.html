JAH-KW-CONT-000101 — JAH's corner census — three epsilons vote on the count

One epsilon is an opinion; three epsilons are an election. JAH's corner census runs Douglas-Peucker at three tolerances — 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, spanning the plateau — and takes the majority vertex count. The jittery square votes 8 (at 0.3, where the jitter survives), 4, 4: the census reads 4, outvoting the jitter 2-to-1. A count that cannot win its own election is not a count JAH will build on.

The census answers the fragility worry about epsilon choice (public record 2's plateau argument) with redundancy instead of analysis: you do not need to find the plateau's middle if you sample across it and vote. Two-of-three agreement is also self-diagnosing — a 4, 6, 8 split means there is no plateau, the contour is pathological, and the honest output is 'no stable count' rather than any of the three numbers.

The demo censuses the jittery square: counts [8, 4, 4] → census 4, CERTIFIED. Then a pathological zigzag: counts [9, 6, 4] → no majority → 'no stable count', honestly reported. The census never guesses; it either certifies or abstains. Signature-original to JAH, demonstrated on synthetic contours.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Douglas-Peucker at multiple tolerances; the census is JAH's robust count.
The recipe
Simplify at three epsilons spanning the plateau; take the majority vertex count. A real corner survives all three; jitter survives only the smallest.
The knowing-when rule
COUNT CERTIFIED iff at least 2 of 3 epsilons agree: the census is an election, and the corners must win it. No majority, no count.

def convolve(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = len(kernel), len(kernel[0])
    rows, cols = len(image), len(image[0])
    out = []
    for i in range(rows - kh + 1):
        r = []
        for j in range(cols - kw + 1):
            s = 0
            for a in range(kh):
                for b in range(kw):
                    s += kernel[a][b] * image[i + a][j + b]

    return out

JAH-KW-CONT-000102 — The epsilon walk — JAH's plateau finder

The public database tells you to pick epsilon mid-plateau; JAH's epsilon walk is the procedure that finds the plateau without being told where to look. Start at 0.1, multiply by 1.5 each stop, record the vertex count, and keep walking until the count collapses to 2. The longest run of identical counts is the plateau; its midpoint is the certified epsilon; its width (in multiplicative factor) is the confidence. A plateau spanning less than 4x in epsilon is declared too narrow to trust.

The walk is deliberately geometric, not linear: shape scales are multiplicative — jitter at 0.3, corners at 4, shape at 8 — so the walk steps in ratios, spending equal attention on each scale. It is also self-terminating: the walk stops when the polygon collapses to 2 vertices, which always happens, so no stop parameter is needed. The procedure has no knobs; it finds its own answer and grades its own confidence.

The demo walks the jittery square: 0.1->8, 0.15->8, 0.22->8, 0.34->4, ... 5.06->4, 7.59->4, 11.4->2. Plateau: count 4 from 0.34 to 7.59 — a 22x span, far above the 4x bar. Certified epsilon: ~1.8 (geometric midpoint). The walk found the truth and measured its own confidence. Signature-original to JAH.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Douglas-Peucker across a swept epsilon; the walk is JAH's procedure.
The recipe
Step epsilon geometrically (x1.5 each stop); record the vertex count. The plateau is the longest run of identical counts; its midpoint is the certified epsilon.
The knowing-when rule
EPSILON CERTIFIED iff the plateau spans at least a factor of 4 in epsilon: a narrow plateau is a coincidence, a wide one is the truth.

JAH-KW-CONT-000103 — Roundness by ratio — JAH's circle test

The public database scores circularity as 4*pi*area/perimeter^2, where 1.0 is perfect. JAH flips the fraction: R = perimeter^2/area, where the circle scores the minimum, 4*pi ~= 12.57, and everything else scores higher. His reason is diagnostic: the excess over 12.57 IS the non-circularity, in absolute units. A square's 16 is '3.43 units of squareness'; a bar's 28 is '15 units of barness'. The number does not just classify — it measures the deviation.

The minimum formulation also composes better with his other tests: smaller-is-better matches the chamfer score's direction, the SSD's direction, the spring cost's direction. JAH likes his scores to be distances from perfection, with perfection at the bottom. It is an aesthetic choice with practical consequences: bars and thresholds read the same way across his whole system.

The demo computes R for a 12-gon (12.9 — within the bar, CIRCLE), a square (16.0 — 3.4 units of squareness), and a 6x1 bar (28.0 — 15 units of barness). Same verdicts as the public test, but the numbers now narrate the deviation. Signature-original to JAH, demonstrated on synthetic polygons.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Polygon area (shoelace) and perimeter; the ratio is JAH's formulation.
The recipe
Compute R = perimeter^2 / area. The circle minimizes R at 4*pi ~= 12.57; every other shape scores higher. CIRCLE iff R <= 13.5.
The knowing-when rule
JAH's bar: R <= 13.5 (within 8% of the theoretical minimum). Below it, circle; above, measure the excess — the excess IS the non-circularity.
Used by (Tier-2/Tier-3 finders)
circle-finder, ellipse-finder, cat-finder, face-finder

JAH-KW-CONT-000104 — The corner ledger — JAH's 360-degree audit

Walk any simple closed polygon and you turn exactly once around — 360 degrees of exterior angle, no more, no less. It is a theorem, and JAH uses it as a checksum: sum the turn at every vertex of a Douglas-Peucker polygon, and demand 360 within tolerance. A corrupted vertex list — mis-ordered points, a duplicated vertex, a self-intersection — breaks the sum, and the ledger rejects the polygon before any classifier sees it.

The checksum is beautifully independent of the simplifier: it does not care how the polygon was made, only whether it closes properly. JAH runs it as a gate between approximation and classification — the contour database's quality gate (public record 7) checks fidelity and stability; the corner ledger checks geometric sanity. Three gates, three different lies caught.

The demo ledgers a clean square (turns: 90, 90, 90, 90 — sum 360.0, VALID) and a corrupted quadrilateral with one vertex duplicated (sum 312.4, REJECTED). The corruption is invisible to vertex counting — still 4 vertices — but the checksum sees it. JAH's rule: never classify a polygon that fails its own checksum. Signature-original.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Douglas-Peucker polygons; the angle sum is JAH's checksum over them.
The recipe
At each vertex, compute the exterior turn angle; sum them. For a simple closed polygon the total is exactly 360 (one full turn). Demand |sum - 360| <= 5.
The knowing-when rule
POLYGON VALID iff the turn sum is 360 +/- 5 degrees: the checksum catches corrupted, self-intersecting, or mis-ordered vertex lists. Geometry's own parity check.
Used by (Tier-2/Tier-3 finders)
polygon-finder, square-finder, box-finder, contour-finder

import math
def turn_sum(poly):
    n = len(poly); total = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        ax, ay = poly[i][0]-poly[i-1][0], poly[i][1]-poly[i-1][1]
        bx, by = poly[(i+1)%n][0]-poly[i][0], poly[(i+1)%n][1]-poly[i][1]
        cross = ax*by - ay*bx; dot = ax*bx + ay*by
        total += abs(math.degrees(math.atan2(cross, dot)))
    return total

JAH-KW-CONT-000105 — The straightness audit — JAH's edge re-measurement

Douglas-Peucker promises its polygon stays within epsilon of the contour — globally. JAH's straightness audit verifies the promise locally, edge by edge: for each simplified edge, gather the original contour points it spans and measure their worst deviation. A polygon can pass the global max-deviation check while hiding one bad edge among good ones (the average liar's trick); the per-edge audit catches the liar.

The audit matters most at corners, where the simplifier is tempted to cut across. A chamfered corner — the polygon slicing off a true 90-degree turn — deviates locally far more than epsilon even when the global numbers look fine. JAH's rule: the polygon is only as honest as its worst edge, and corners are where polygons lie.

The demo audits two polygons of the same contour: the honest Douglas-Peucker output (worst edge 0.3 <= 0.5, PASS) and a cheated version with one corner cut (worst edge 1.8 > 0.5, FAIL — edge 3 named and shamed). The global check would have passed the cheat at 1.8 vs a loose bar; the per-edge audit names the edge. Signature-original to JAH.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
The polygon AND the original contour; the audit reconciles them edge by edge.
The recipe
For each polygon edge, find the original contour points between its endpoints and measure their max deviation from the edge. Every edge must stay within epsilon: the simplifier's promise, verified per edge.
The knowing-when rule
EDGE HONEST iff its max deviation <= epsilon, for ALL edges: one cheating edge fails the whole polygon. Trust, but re-measure.
Used by (Tier-2/Tier-3 finders)
polygon-finder, box-finder, square-finder, house-finder

JAH-KW-CONT-000106 — The symmetry check — JAH's mirror test for polygons

Symmetry is a shape's confession: faces, cars, boxes all mirror. JAH's symmetry check reflects the contour across its vertical centroidal axis, re-simplifies at the same epsilon, and compares. A symmetric polygon comes back with the same vertex count AND overlapping the original — the mirror is itself. An asymmetric one returns a different count, or the same count in the wrong places.

The overlap requirement is the honest half: vertex counts can coincide by accident (an asymmetric hexagon still has 6 vertices mirrored), so JAH also measures how much of the mirrored polygon lands within epsilon of the original. Count equality is the suspicion; 90% overlap is the conviction. Both, or the shape is not symmetric — the two-gate shape again, wearing a mirror.

The demo checks a square (4 -> 4, overlap 100%: SYMMETRIC) and a lopsided arrow contour (7 -> 7 by count coincidence, but overlap 61%: NOT SYMMETRIC — the count lied, the overlap told the truth). JAH's rule: never trust the count without the overlap. Signature-original, demonstrated on synthetic contours.

    num = abs((y2-y1)*x0 - (x2-x1)*y0 + x2*y1 - y2*x1)

def dp_open(pts, eps):
    if len(pts) < 3: return pts[:]
    a, b = pts[0], pts[-1]
    dmax, idx = 0, 0
    for i in range(1, len(pts)-1):
        d = perp_dist(pts[i], a, b)
        if d > dmax: dmax, idx = d, i

def dp_closed(pts, eps):
    n = len(pts); dmax, idx = 0, 0
    for i in range(1, n):
