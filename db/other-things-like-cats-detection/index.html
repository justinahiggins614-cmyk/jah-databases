JAH-DET-000021 — Finding dogs: the documented pipeline, from datasets to CNNs

The scan recovers the planted template exactly. That is template matching's honest jurisdiction: known rigid patterns. Everything else about dog detection — floppy ears at any angle, any breed, any pose — belongs to trained classifiers, and the documented training ground is public: 20,580 labeled photographs in Stanford Dogs.

The deeper point for this database: 'things like cats' are found by the same ladder. Dogs differ from cats not in the kernels but in the labels — the pipeline is species-blind and the training data is everything. Change the photographs, and the cat-finder becomes a dog-finder.

JAH-DET-000022 — Finding human faces: Viola-Jones Haar cascades (2001)

The face pattern's eye-band and nose-bridge features both fire far above the scrambled pattern's — the features measure exactly the light-dark geography of a human face: sockets darker than cheeks, bridge brighter than its sides. Boosting's job in the documented system was to discover such features automatically from labeled faces; the demo hand-places them to show what the discovered ones look like.

The cascade is the unsung documented hero: a 24x24 window sliding over a 320x240 image at several scales visits ~160,000 windows, and the cascade's first two-feature stage rejects the overwhelming majority. Face detection became real-time not because the features are clever but because the system refuses to waste time on obvious non-faces.

JAH-DET-000023 — Finding cars: HOG+SVM and the road to YOLO

The histogram reads like a car's blueprint in statistics: heavy horizontal bins from the roofline and body sills, vertical bins from the pillars, and a broad spread from the curved wheel arches. A linear SVM trained on such vectors — the documented HOG+SVM recipe — separates cars from road scenes because few non-car things combine long horizontals, verticals, and twin arcs in one window.

Modern documented detectors replaced the hand-built histogram with learned features but kept the geometry of the problem: find the box, name what's inside. YOLO's documented contribution was doing both in a single pass — but under the hood, its early layers still learn to love rooflines and wheel arcs, the same orientations this histogram counts.

JAH-DET-000024 — Finding corners: the Harris detector (Harris & Stephens, 1988)

The numbers sort themselves exactly as the 1988 paper promises: a large positive R for the corner, a negative R for the edge, essentially zero for the flat. No eigenvalues were computed — the determinant-minus-k-trace-squared formula encodes the classification directly, which was Harris and Stephens's elegant trick.

This record is infrastructure for the whole lane: whenever another record says 'ear tip' or 'roofline corner', the Harris response is the documented way to find such points reliably. Corners are where detection becomes matchable — the same corner looks the same from frame to frame, which is why trackers, stitchers, and 3D reconstructors all start here.

JAH-DET-000025 — Finding straight lines: the Hough transform (1962/1972)

The accumulator's top cell holds the votes of the collinear points while noise votes scatter across hundreds of cells — the peak-finding formulation turns a fragile connecting problem into a robust voting problem. That robustness is the documented reason the Hough transform survived from 1962 into modern lane-finding: real lane markings are broken, dirty, and partial, and voting doesn't care.

The cat-lane connection: whiskers are short line segments, and the documented Hough machinery (in its probabilistic and segment-detecting variants) is one honest way to find them — though the Signature lane's whisker kernel treats them as texture instead. Two documented philosophies, same whiskers.

JAH-DET-000026 — Finding textures: Local Binary Patterns (Ojala et al., 1996)

The spot center and the weave produce clearly different codes, and the histogram's most common entries describe the background texture — exactly the documented behavior: LBP turns texture into countable micro-patterns. The lighting invariance deserves emphasis: because every decision is a comparison, doubling the illumination changes nothing, which is why LBP survived the deep-learning wave in inspection and biometrics niches.

For the cat story, LBP is the coat detector: stripes, spots, and solids are different histograms, and a documented fur-classification stage could sit between the shape pipeline and the final verdict — another reminder that 'finding a cat' decomposes into finding head-shape AND finding fur-texture, each with its own documented tools.

JAH-DET-000027 — Finding blobs: Laplacian of Gaussian and Difference of Gaussians

The matched blob's center response dwarfs the oversized blob's — the kernel is tuned to its scale and says so numerically. This is the documented contract of LoG/DoG detection: you don't just find blobs, you find blobs *of a size*, and repeating the search across scales (Lowe's scale space) finds blobs of all sizes.

The lineage matters for this lane: Marr and Hildreth proposed LoG edge/blob theory in 1980, Lowe operationalized DoG extrema in 2004, and every 'find the round parts' stage since — including the Signature eye experiments — stands on the documented fact demonstrated here: second derivatives find centers.

JAH-DET-000028 — Finding motion: frame differencing and block matching

The difference image isolates exactly the two square positions — old and new — and block matching recovers the true (2,1) displacement. These two operations are the documented atoms of motion analysis: differencing for detection ('something moved here'), matching for estimation ('it went there').

In the cat story, motion is the trigger, not the identifier: a wildlife camera's documented pipeline differences frames to wake up, then runs the object pipeline to ask *what* moved. And the honest limitation is documented too: differencing fails on camouflaged motion (a tabby walking across a textured rug changes few pixels) — which is why real systems fuse motion with appearance.

JAH-DET-000029 — Finding smiles and eyes: Haar cascades in OpenCV

Both atomic measurements behave: eyes read darker than their sockets, the smile arc reads brighter than a straight band across the same mouth region. AdaBoost's documented job was to find thousands of such measurements and weight them; the cascade's job was to order them cheapest-first. The shipped XML files are that process frozen into data — which is why downloading haarcascade_smile.xml gives you a working smile detector with zero training on your part.

The lane lesson: 'things like cats' includes things *on* faces, and the same 2001 machinery detects them all. The features change (arcs for smiles, blobs for eyes, bands for faces); the system — integral image, boosting, cascade — stays the same.

JAH-DET-000030 — Finding license plates: the documented ALPR pipeline

The edge-density scan peaks exactly at the plate rows: character strokes are the densest vertical-edge structure on a car's front, denser than the windshield boundary and far denser than the bumper. That single observation — plates are edge-dense bands — is the documented heart of classical plate localization, and the demo shows it working.

The theoretical marking is doing honest work in this record: a full ALPR system also segments and reads characters, and those stages are genuinely harder (fonts vary, plates tilt, dirt obscures). Modern documented systems do it all with deep networks, but the classical pipeline's first half — edges to plate box — remains the clearest illustration in the lane of detection as *measurement with a purpose*.

# NCC template matching: locate a planted floppy-ear dog template
import math, random
random.seed(21)
T=[[0]*6 for _ in range(6)]
for y in range(2,5):
    for x in range(1,3): T[y][x]=1
    for x in range(4,6): T[y][x]=1
for y in range(1,4):
    for x in range(2,4): T[y][x]=1
IMG=[[random.random()*0.3 for _ in range(12)] for _ in range(12)]
py,px=4,5
for y in range(6):
    for x in range(6): IMG[py+y][px+x]=T[y][x]
def ncc(p,t):
    n=36; mp=sum(sum(r) for r in p)/n; mt=sum(sum(r) for r in t)/n
    num=sum((p[y][x]-mp)*(t[y][x]-mt) for y in range(6) for x in range(6))
    dp=math.sqrt(sum((p[y][x]-mp)**2 for y in range(6) for x in range(6)))
    dt=math.sqrt(sum((t[y][x]-mt)**2 for y in range(6) for x in range(6)))
    return num/(dp*dt) if dp*dt else 0.0
best,bloc=-2,None
for y in range(7):
    for x in range(7):
        s=ncc([row[x:x+6] for row in IMG[y:y+6]],T)
        if s>best: best,bloc=s,(y,x)
print("planted at:",(py,px),"| detected at:",bloc,"| NCC =",round(best,4))
print("Stanford Dogs (2012): 20,580 images, 120 breeds — the documented training ground.")
