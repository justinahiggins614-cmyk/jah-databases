The Atom Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established The ICRP Reference Man elemental composition is published and standard, and the atom counts follow from it by fixed arithmetic.

The atom is the smallest unit of the human body: about seven thousand trillion trillion atoms, mostly hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, and nitrogen, arranged into the molecules of a 70-kilogram adult man.

A 70-kilogram adult man is made of atoms, and the count can be stated exactly once a reference body is fixed: the ICRP Reference Man weighs 70 kilograms and stands 170 centimetres tall, and its elemental composition is published as masses, oxygen 43 kilograms, carbon 16 kilograms, hydrogen 7 kilograms, nitrogen 1.8 kilograms, calcium 1.0 kilogram, and phosphorus 0.78 kilograms, with the remainder in smaller amounts. Counting atoms means turning each mass into moles by dividing by the molar mass, then multiplying by Avogadro's number, 6.02214076 times ten to the twenty-third per mole, exact since the 2019 revision of the SI. The total comes to about seven times ten to the twenty-seventh atoms, and this record derives the oxygen count in full.

Oxygen is the heaviest element in the body at 43 kilograms. Convert to grams, 43,000 grams, and divide by the molar mass of oxygen, 16.0 grams per mole, which gives 2,687.5 moles. Multiply by Avogadro's number, 6.02214076 times ten to the twenty-third, and the result is about 1.618 times ten to the twenty-seventh oxygen atoms. Hydrogen, the lightest element, gives the largest count: 7,000 grams divided by 1.008 grams per mole, times Avogadro's number, is about 4.2 times ten to the twenty-seventh atoms.

History

Counting the atoms of the body became possible in stages: Dalton's atomic theory in 1808 gave chemistry its counting unit, Avogadro's hypothesis in 1811 tied gas volumes to particle numbers, and the mole was fixed by the twentieth century. The ICRP published its Reference Man in 1975, giving the first standard elemental composition of the human body, revised in 2002.

The molecule is the next level up from the atom: water, proteins, lipids, and DNA, the four molecular families that make up the mass of the human body.

The atoms of the body are not loose; they are joined into molecules, and the mass of a 70-kilogram man divides among four molecular families that can each be weighed. Fat, the stored lipid, is about 10 to 14 kilograms depending on the individual, and mineral makes up most of the rest. The Cell Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established The 37.2 trillion cell census and the textbook red blood cell concentration are published, and the total follows from them by fixed arithmetic.

The cell is the living unit of the body: about 37.2 trillion of them in one adult man, counted by type, with red blood cells the most numerous by far.

A 70-kilogram man is not one mass but about 37.2 trillion cells, the figure published by Bianconi and colleagues in 2013 after adding up the published counts for each cell type. Red blood cells dominate the count: about 5 million per microlitre of blood, and with about 5 litres of blood that is about 25 trillion red blood cells, roughly two-thirds of all the cells in the body. Other counts are far smaller: about 300 billion platelets, about 1 billion white blood cells at any moment, and about 86 billion neurons in the brain. Cells range in size from about 5 micrometres for the smallest to about 150 micrometres for the largest, and every count here is approximate, because cell numbers vary from person to person and from day to day.

A single microlitre of blood, a speck smaller than a pinhead, holds about 5 million red blood cells. A man has about 5 litres of blood, which is 5 million microlitres, so the multiplication gives about 25 trillion red blood cells. That is roughly two out of every three cells in the whole body. The brain, by contrast, holds about 86 billion neurons, which is less than one percent of the red blood cell count, though each neuron does far more work than one cell.

History

Cells were first seen by Hooke in 1665 and named cells; Schwann and Schleiden stated cell theory in 1838 and 1839, that all living things are made of cells. Blood cell counts were standardized in the twentieth century, and the first full-body cell census, adding up every published cell-type count, was published by Bianconi and colleagues in 2013, giving 37.2 trillion cells.

Evidence: established The four tissue types and the textbook muscle share are standard and published, and the muscle mass follows from them by fixed arithmetic.

The tissue is the next level up from the cell: four tissue types, epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous, woven into every organ of the body.

Cells do not work alone; they are organized into four tissue types, and every organ is a mixture of them. Epithelial tissue covers surfaces and lines cavities, including the skin and the lining of the gut. Connective tissue supports and binds, including bone, cartilage, fat, and blood itself. Muscle tissue contracts, in three forms: skeletal, cardiac, and smooth. Nervous tissue carries signals, in the brain, the spinal cord, and the nerves. By mass, skeletal muscle is the largest tissue: about 40 percent of a 70-kilogram man, about 28 kilograms. Fat tissue is about 10 to 14 kilograms, blood about 5 kilograms, and the skin, the largest organ, about 4 kilograms.

Skeletal muscle is the largest tissue by mass: 70 kilograms times a share of about 0.40 gives about 28 kilograms of skeletal muscle, spread across more than 600 muscles. Fat tissue is the next largest soft tissue at about 10 to 14 kilograms. Blood, counted as a connective tissue because it has cells in a fluid matrix, is about 5 kilograms. The skin, an epithelial organ, is about 4 kilograms and about 1.8 square metres in area.

History

Tissues were first classified by Bichat around 1800, who described 21 tissue types from dissection without a microscope. The microscope reduced the list to four fundamental types by the late nineteenth century, and modern histology textbooks still teach the same four: epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous, each with its subtypes.
