Sense and Reference JAH-LSEM-000001 · compounded

Sense and reference are the two sides of meaning distinguished by the philosopher Gottlob Frege in 1892. The reference of an expression is the thing or things in the world it picks out; the sense is the way it presents that thing, the mode of presentation. Two expressions can share a reference while differing in sense: 'the morning star' and 'the evening star' both refer to the planet Venus, but they present it differently. The distinction explains how a true statement of identity can be informative: 'the morning star is the evening star' tells us something, while 'the morning star is the morning star' tells us nothing, because the two expressions have different senses.

Sense and reference are the two sides of meaning distinguished by the philosopher Gottlob Frege in 1892. The distinction explains how a true statement of identity can be informative: 'the morning star is the evening star' tells us something, while 'the morning star is the morning star' tells us nothing, because the two expressions have different senses.

The Problem

How can two expressions refer to the same thing yet differ in meaning, and how can a true identity statement be informative?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: identify the reference. For a name or description, ask what object in the world it picks out: both 'the morning star' and 'the evening star' pick out Venus. Step 2: identify the sense. Ask how the expression presents its referent: 'morning star' presents Venus as the bright object seen in the morning, 'evening star' as the one seen in the evening. Step 3: apply to identity. 'A = B' is informative when A and B have different senses but the same reference, and trivial when the senses are the same. Step 4: extend. The same distinction applies to whole sentences: a sentence's reference is its truth value, its sense is the thought it expresses.

The Answer

Meaning has two layers: reference, the thing an expression picks out, and sense, the way it presents it. Expressions can share a reference with different senses, which is what makes some true identity statements informative.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

The distinction is contested: direct-reference theorists argue that names have no descriptive sense, and the notion of sense itself is hard to define precisely. The account describes Frege's framework, not a settled consensus.

Math Proof

Let ref(E) be the reference of expression E and sense(E) its sense. ref('morning star') = ref('evening star') = Venus, but sense('morning star') ≠ sense('evening star'). Informative identity holds when ref(A) = ref(B) and sense(A) ≠ sense(B).

Science Proof

The distinction is supported by the fact that speakers can understand both expressions and learn the identity as a discovery — astronomy established that the morning and evening stars are one planet. Formal semantics builds the distinction into its models: intensions capture sense, extensions capture reference.

Truth Conditions JAH-LSEM-000002 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

Truth conditions are the conditions under which a sentence is true: to know the meaning of a sentence, on the truth-conditional view, is to know what the world would have to be like for the sentence to be true. The sentence 'Snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white. The idea was developed by Alfred Tarski in the 1930s for formal languages and applied to natural language by Donald Davidson, who proposed that a theory of meaning for a language is a theory that generates, for every sentence, a statement of its truth conditions.

History

Tarski's 1933 work on the concept of truth gave the first precise account of truth for formal languages. Davidson's 1967 paper 'Truth and Meaning' argued that Tarski's method could serve as a theory of meaning for natural languages, launching truth-conditional semantics.

Description

Truth conditions are the conditions under which a sentence is true: to know the meaning of a sentence, on the truth-conditional view, is to know what the world would have to be like for the sentence to be true. The sentence 'Snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white.

The idea was developed by Alfred Tarski in the 1930s for formal languages and applied to natural language by Donald Davidson, who proposed that a theory of meaning for a language is a theory that generates, for every sentence, a statement of its truth conditions.

The Problem

What is the meaning of a sentence, and how can meaning be stated precisely enough to explain how we understand sentences we have never heard before?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: state the T-schema. For any sentence S, 'S' is true if and only if p, where p states the condition: 'Snow is white' is true if and only if snow is white. Step 2: build compositionally. The truth conditions of a whole sentence are computed from the meanings of its parts and how they are combined, so a finite theory covers infinitely many sentences. Step 3: test. A speaker who knows the theory can work out the truth conditions of a new sentence, which explains the productivity of understanding. Step 4: note the limits. Truth conditions alone do not capture everything about meaning: 'Snow is white' and 'Snow is white and 2 + 2 = 4' have the same truth conditions in the actual world but seem to differ in meaning.

The Answer

A sentence's meaning can be given by its truth conditions — what the world must be like for it to be true — and a compositional theory of truth conditions explains how speakers understand new sentences.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

Truth conditions do not capture tone, emphasis, or the difference between sentences true in the same worlds. The account is the classical truth-conditional view, not the only theory of meaning.

Math Proof

Let T(S) be the truth value of sentence S. T('Snow is white') = true iff snow is white. A theory covering 1,000 vocabulary items and 100 syntactic rules can generate truth conditions for an unbounded number of sentences.

Science Proof

The T-schema is the standard formal tool for stating truth conditions, and compositional theories generate correct truth conditions for a wide range of sentence types. The approach is the foundation of formal semantics, though its limits are acknowledged: sentences with the same truth conditions can differ in meaning, which motivates richer theories.

Compositionality JAH-LSEM-000003 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

Compositionality is the principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. The meaning of 'red ball' comes from the meaning of 'red', the meaning of 'ball', and the rule for combining an adjective with a noun. The principle, associated with Frege, explains the productivity of language: speakers understand sentences they have never heard before because they can compute their meanings from familiar parts and familiar rules.

History

The principle was stated by Frege in the late 19th century and became the working assumption of formal semantics in the 20th century, notably in the work of Richard Montague in the 1970s, who showed how to compute the meanings of English sentences compositionally.

Description

Compositionality is the principle that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. The meaning of 'red ball' comes from the meaning of 'red', the meaning of 'ball', and the rule for combining an adjective with a noun.

The principle, associated with Frege, explains the productivity of language: speakers understand sentences they have never heard before because they can compute their meanings from familiar parts and familiar rules.

The Problem

How do speakers understand sentences they have never heard before, and what determines the meaning of a whole sentence?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: list the parts. Break the sentence into its words and their meanings. Step 2: find the structure. Identify how the parts are combined — which word modifies which, which is subject and which is object. Step 3: apply the rules. Each combination rule says how the meaning of the whole is computed from the meanings of the parts: for example, an adjective-noun combination denotes the intersection of the adjective's set and the noun's set. Step 4: compute. Work from the smallest parts upward until the meaning of the whole sentence is derived. Step 5: check. The same parts in a different structure can give a different meaning — 'dog bites man' versus 'man bites dog' — which confirms that structure, not just the parts, matters.

The Answer

The meaning of a sentence is computed from the meanings of its parts plus the rules of combination, which is what makes it possible to understand new sentences.

When It Applies

This analysis applies to the cases described in the method, within the limits stated below; outside those cases the conclusions do not transfer automatically.

Limits

Idioms, metaphors and context-dependent expressions challenge strict compositionality, and the principle does not by itself say what meanings are — only how they combine.

Math Proof

Let M(E) be the meaning of expression E. For a complex expression E made of parts A and B combined by rule R: M(E) = R(M(A), M(B)). With 1,000 words and 100 rules, the number of distinct sentences is unbounded.

Science Proof

Compositional theories successfully predict the meanings of new sentences, and the contrast between 'dog bites man' and 'man bites dog' shows that structure matters. Apparent counterexamples — idioms such as 'kick the bucket' — are handled by listing them as units, though the scope of non-compositional meaning is debated.

Synonymy JAH-LSEM-000004 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

Entailment is the basic notion of logical consequence in semantics, and it is what makes reasoning with language possible. 'Every student did not pass' is ambiguous: it can mean no student passed, or not every student passed — the negation can take scope over the quantifier or under it. Step 3: test presupposition. 'The king of France is not bald' still presupposes a king of France, showing presuppositions survive negation. Step 4: test processing. Experiments show people take about 100-200 milliseconds longer to verify a negative sentence than an affirmative one, and they sometimes first represent the affirmative and then negate it.

Results reported

Negation reverses truth, but its scope, its interaction with quantifiers and presupposition, and its processing cost make it one of the most complex operations in semantics.

Skeptical analysis

Negation in natural language is richer than the logical operator: it can deny, correct, or express irony, and its scope is often ambiguous. The account presents the standard formal and experimental view.
