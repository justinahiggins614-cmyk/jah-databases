What Bioacoustic Recording Is JAH-BARA-000001 · simple

Bioacoustic recording is the capture of animal sounds with microphones or hydrophones so they can be measured, compared, and archived, rather than only heard in the moment. A recording preserves the timing, pitch, and loudness pattern of a call, which lets researchers count calls, measure call rates, compare populations, and detect changes over seasons and years. Modern bioacoustics began with portable tape recorders and now uses autonomous recording units that sit in a forest or reef for months, waking on schedule to record. This record's primary layer is biophony, the animal sounds being recorded, but every recording also captures geophony, such as wind and water, and anthrophony, such as traffic and machinery, and analysis must separate those layers rather than blur them.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: the method's validity rests on calibration: a recorder whose sensitivity is measured against a known reference tone produces measurements that can be compared across sites and years, while an uncalibrated recorder produces only relative impressions.

Math proof

Math proof: a 10-minute recording contains 10 x 60 = 600 seconds of sound to analyse.

Microphones, Hydrophones, and Recorders JAH-BARA-000002 · simple

Different sounds need different sensors. Airborne sounds are captured with microphones, underwater sounds with hydrophones, and ground vibrations from large animals with seismic sensors or accelerometers. Each sensor has a frequency response, the range of frequencies it captures evenly, and a sensitivity, how much electrical output it gives per unit of sound pressure, and both must match the target: a microphone meant for human speech will miss bat ultrasound and distort elephant infrasound. Recorders add their own choices: sample rate, bit depth, and file format, usually uncompressed WAV for analysis. This record's primary layer is biophony, but the sensor choice is judged by how cleanly it separates biophony from geophony and anthrophony in the same recording.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: sensor specifications are verified by calibration tones: playing a tone of known frequency and level into the sensor and checking that the recording reproduces it confirms the frequency response and sensitivity on paper.

Math proof

Math proof: a sensor flat from 20 to 20,000 hertz covers a bandwidth of 20,000 - 20 = 19,980 hertz.

Sampling Rate and the Nyquist Rule JAH-BARA-000003 · simple

Digital recording measures sound pressure at regular intervals, and the sample rate is how many measurements are taken per second. The Nyquist rule states that a sample rate can faithfully represent frequencies only up to half the sample rate, called the Nyquist frequency: to capture a 20 kilohertz sound, the sample rate must exceed 40,000 samples per second, and in practice recorders use 44,100 or 48,000 samples per second for audible sound. Sounds above the Nyquist frequency do not simply vanish; they fold back into the recording as false lower frequencies, called aliasing, which is why recorders filter out frequencies above Nyquist before sampling. This rule governs every layer: biophony, geophony, and anthrophony are all subject to the same sampling limit, and a recording that aliases cannot be repaired afterwards.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, proved in the 20th century, is the mathematical foundation of all digital audio, and aliasing is demonstrated whenever an unfiltered high tone appears as a false low tone in a recording.

Math proof

Math proof: at 48,000 samples per second the Nyquist frequency is 48,000 / 2 = 24,000 hertz, so a 30,000 hertz bat call would alias and must not be recorded at that rate.

Source

Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem (as of October 2026).

Spectrograms: Pictures of Sound JAH-BARA-000004 · simple

A spectrogram is a picture of sound with time running left to right, frequency running bottom to top, and loudness shown by darkness or colour. It is computed by slicing a recording into short overlapping windows and measuring the frequency content of each window, a procedure called the short-time Fourier transform. Spectrograms let researchers see what ears miss: the rising sweep of a bat call, the harmonic stack of a bird song, the repeated pulses of a frog call, and the separate frequency bands used by different species in a chorus. Reading a spectrogram takes care: window length trades time resolution against frequency resolution, so a short window pinpoints when a sound happened but blurs its pitch, and a long window does the opposite. Spectrograms display all three layers at once, biophony, geophony, and anthrophony, which is why analysts learn to recognise each layer's visual signature.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: the short-time Fourier transform is a standard, mathematically defined analysis, and spectrogram measurements of call duration and frequency have been validated against direct waveform measurements in published studies.

Math proof

Math proof: a 10-second recording sliced into windows of 0.01 seconds gives 10 / 0.01 = 1,000 windows across the spectrogram.

Source

Frequency Bands and the Fourier Transform JAH-BARA-000005 · simple

Any sound can be described as a sum of pure tones at different frequencies, and the Fourier transform is the mathematical operation that finds how much energy a recording contains at each frequency. Bioacousticians divide the spectrum into bands, for example low frequencies below 1 kilohertz where many large mammals call, mid frequencies where most birds sing, and high frequencies above 20 kilohertz where bats echolocate, and they measure how much energy falls in each band. Band occupancy, the share of total energy in each band, is a simple summary that already reveals whether a recording is dominated by bird song, by insect chorus, or by low engine rumble. All three soundscape layers occupy bands, so band analysis is one tool for separating biophony from geophony and anthrophony.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: Fourier analysis is the standard mathematical basis of acoustics, and band-energy measurements are reproducible: the same recording analysed with the same bands gives the same band shares.

Math proof

Math proof: bands holding 30, 50, and 20 units of energy total 100 units, so their shares are 30 percent, 50 percent, and 20 percent.

Source

Field Recording Protocols: Doing It Right JAH-BARA-000006 · simple

Good field recording is a protocol, not just pressing record. The recordist notes the date, time, location, weather, habitat, equipment, and settings for every recording, keeps the microphone clear of handling noise and wind with a windshield, avoids talking during takes, and records a spoken slate or written log identifying each take. Repeated recordings at the same site at the same times of day let researchers compare seasons and years, and standardised protocols let different teams' recordings be pooled. Missing metadata is the commonest reason a recording becomes useless: a beautiful call with no location or date cannot be used as evidence. Protocols cover all three layers: the log notes wind and rain for geophony, nearby roads and aircraft for anthrophony, and the calling species for biophony.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: standardised protocols are what make long-term monitoring valid: without matched times, places, and settings, apparent change over years may reflect changed methods rather than changed wildlife.

Math proof

Math proof: recording 5 minutes every hour for 24 hours yields 5 x 24 = 120 minutes, or 2.0 hours, of recording per day.

Source

The Acoustic Complexity Index (ACI) JAH-BARA-000007 · compounded

The Acoustic Complexity Index, introduced by Pieretti and colleagues, summarises how variable a soundscape is over time: it measures how much the loudness in each frequency band changes from one moment to the next, and adds those changes up. A soundscape full of varied animal calls, with many onsets and stops, scores high, while a steady drone, such as constant traffic or wind, scores low, because its loudness barely changes. ACI is therefore useful for comparing sites and tracking change, but it is not a species counter: it cannot say which species are present, and a noisy road can inflate it if the noise itself fluctuates. This record describes ACI correctly as a variability measure, not as an invented formula, and it applies to the whole soundscape, biophony plus geophony plus anthrophony, which is why ACI results must always be reported alongside a listening check.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: Pieretti et al. validated ACI against direct counts of bird vocalisations, showing that ACI tracks vocal activity in field recordings, while also noting its sensitivity to fluctuating non-biological noise.

Math proof

Math proof: if band changes of 4, 6, and 10 units are summed, the ACI-style total for those bands is 4 + 6 + 10 = 20 units.

Source

Pieretti et al., Acoustic Complexity Index (as of October 2026).

Acoustic Diversity and Evenness Indices (ADI and AEI) JAH-BARA-000008 · compounded

The Acoustic Diversity Index (ADI) and Acoustic Evenness Index (AEI) summarise how sound energy is spread across frequency bands. ADI applies the Shannon diversity idea to the soundscape: if energy is spread evenly across many bands, diversity is high, and if one band dominates, diversity is low. AEI measures the complementary idea, unevenness, using the Gini coefficient: a high AEI means energy is concentrated in a few bands. Together they give a compact fingerprint of a soundscape, useful for comparing habitats and tracking change, but like ACI they do not identify species, and they respond to geophony and anthrophony as well as biophony. A storm's broadband noise raises diversity measures without any animal being involved, which is why index results are always interpreted with the layers in mind.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: ADI and AEI were introduced and tested in soundscape ecology studies, where they distinguished habitats with different acoustic structure and tracked changes across sites, with their limitations documented alongside their uses.

Math proof

Math proof: energy shares of 0.25, 0.25, 0.25, and 0.25 across 4 bands are perfectly even, giving the maximum diversity for 4 bands.

Source

Soundscape-index literature: ADI and AEI (as of October 2026).

Automated Detection and Classification JAH-BARA-000009 · compounded

When recorders run for months, the recordings far exceed what humans can listen to, so researchers use automated detectors: software that scans recordings for sounds matching a target pattern, such as a species' call template or a learned classifier. A detector's quality is reported as precision, the share of its detections that are truly the target, and recall, the share of true calls it finds, and both matter: a detector with high recall but low precision floods researchers with false alarms. Template matching works well for stereotyped calls, while machine-learning classifiers handle variable calls but need large labeled training sets and can fail on unfamiliar populations. Detection output is always validated against human listening on a sample, and the layers matter: a detector tuned to biophony must reject geophony and anthrophony that resemble its target.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: detector validation studies report precision and recall on held-out recordings, and the standard practice of human verification on a random sample is what keeps automated counts honest.

Math proof

Math proof: a detector that finds 80 of 100 true calls has recall of 80 / 100 = 0.80, and if 80 of its 100 detections are true, its precision is also 0.80.

Source

Automated detector validation literature: precision and recall reporting (as of October 2026).

Autonomous Recording Units and Arrays JAH-BARA-000010 · compounded

An autonomous recording unit, or ARU, is a weatherproof recorder with a microphone, battery, and memory that runs unattended on a programmed schedule for weeks or months, and arrays of ARUs spread across a landscape let researchers monitor many sites at once. Arrays can also localise callers: when the same call reaches several recorders at slightly different times, the time differences reveal the caller's position, a technique called acoustic localisation. ARUs transformed soundscape ecology because they remove the observer effect, record through the night and in bad weather, and produce comparable samples across sites. Their limits are real: batteries and memory run out, microphones degrade, and a recorder hears only its own small radius, so array design, spacing, and maintenance are part of the science. ARU recordings contain all three layers, and analysis separates biophony from geophony and anthrophony.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: ARU-based monitoring has been validated against human point counts in multiple studies, showing comparable species detection when schedules and placement are matched, and acoustic localisation is validated by tests with known sound sources.

Math proof

Math proof: an array of 12 ARUs recording 2 hours per day for 30 days yields 12 x 2 x 30 = 720 recorder-hours.

Measuring Noise: Levels and Signal-to-Noise Ratio JAH-BARA-000011 · compounded

Sound level is measured in decibels, a logarithmic scale referenced to a standard pressure, and the signal-to-noise ratio, or SNR, measures how far a target sound stands above background noise. A call at 60 decibels against background noise at 50 decibels has an SNR of 10 decibels, which is usually easy to detect, while the same call against 58 decibels of noise has an SNR of only 2 decibels and may be missed. Because decibels are logarithmic, adding two equal sounds raises the level by only about 3 decibels, not by doubling the decibel number, and a 10-decibel rise is perceived as roughly a doubling of loudness. Noise measurement matters for all three layers: biophony is the signal, while geophony and anthrophony are usually the noise, though in soundscape ecology all three are studied in their own right.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: decibel arithmetic and SNR are defined by acoustic physics, and detection studies confirm that SNR, not absolute level alone, predicts whether a call is detected by listeners or software.

Math proof

Math proof: two sounds at 60 decibels each combine to 10 x log10(10^6 + 10^6) = 63.0 decibels, a rise of about 3 decibels.

Source

Acoustic physics: decibel scale and signal-to-noise ratio (as of October 2026).

Passive Acoustic Monitoring Over Seasons and Years JAH-BARA-000012 · complex

Passive acoustic monitoring, or PAM, is the long-term use of unattended recorders to track wildlife and soundscapes over seasons and years without disturbing the animals. PAM reveals phenology, the timing of breeding choruses, migrations, and daily activity patterns, and it detects change: a chorus that starts earlier each spring, a species that stops calling at a site, or a soundscape that grows louder with a new road. Because recorders run on fixed schedules, PAM samples are comparable across years in a way casual recordings are not, provided equipment and placement stay consistent. PAM data cover all three layers, and long-term indices must separate a genuine biophony change, such as a lost species, from a geophony change, such as a wetter year, or an anthrophony change, such as new traffic.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: multi-year PAM studies have documented shifts in calling phenology and species presence that match independent survey data, validating PAM as a monitoring method rather than a mere recording convenience.

Math proof

Math proof: recording 10 minutes per hour, 24 hours a day, for 365 days yields 10 x 24 x 365 = 87,600 minutes, or 1,460 hours, per year.

Machine Learning Classifiers and Their Pitfalls JAH-BARA-000013 · complex

Machine learning classifiers, trained on thousands of labeled spectrograms or waveforms, can identify species in recordings at scales humans cannot match, and tools such as BirdNET have brought automated bird identification to recordists worldwide. Their pitfalls are equally well documented: a classifier trained on one region can fail in another where the same species sounds different, rare species with few training examples are recognised poorly, and background sounds can be misclassified as species, producing false records. Best practice therefore requires local validation, a confidence threshold below which detections are treated as unconfirmed, and human review of surprising results. Classifiers process all three layers, and a classifier's training data must include geophony and anthrophony examples labeled as non-target, or it will mistake wind and machinery for animals.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: published evaluations of BirdNET and similar classifiers report accuracy on held-out data and document regional performance drops, which is why local validation is a stated requirement rather than an optional extra.

Math proof

Math proof: if a classifier labels 1,000 clips and 900 are correct, its accuracy is 900 / 1,000 = 0.90, or 90 percent.

Source

Published evaluations of BirdNET and similar classifiers (as of October 2026).

Calibration, Metadata, and Archiving Standards JAH-BARA-000014 · complex

A recording is only reusable evidence if its calibration and metadata are preserved. Calibration means measuring the recorder's sensitivity with a reference tone so levels can be stated in real decibels; metadata means the date, time, location, equipment, settings, habitat, and recordist for every file; and archiving means storing uncompressed originals with stable identifiers in a repository where others can find them. Standards such as Darwin Core for biodiversity data and the FAIR principles, findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable, guide modern archives. Without these, recordings decay into orphan files: sounds with no provenance, no comparable levels, and no way for another researcher to verify them. Standards apply to all three layers, because a future researcher needs to know which layer each sound belongs to.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: archival standards are validated by reuse: recordings with full metadata and calibration are the ones cited and reanalysed in later studies, while uncalibrated, undocumented recordings are rarely reusable.

Math proof

Math proof: archiving 1,000 files of 5.5 megabytes each needs 1,000 x 5.5 = 5,500 megabytes, about 5.5 gigabytes.

Source

FAIR archiving principles; Darwin Core biodiversity data standard (as of October 2026).

Ethics of Playback and Recording JAH-BARA-000015 · complex

Recording and playback affect the animals being studied, so bioacoustics has ethical rules. Playback can attract predators to a calling animal, disrupt breeding by drawing a territorial male away, or stress animals if repeated, so researchers limit playback duration and repetitions, avoid sensitive periods such as nesting where possible, and seek permits for protected species. Recording itself is less intrusive, especially with autonomous units, but even recorders must be placed without damaging habitat or disturbing nest sites. In human landscapes, recordists also respect privacy: a recorder near homes can capture human speech, which is anthrophony of a sensitive kind, and such recordings must be handled under privacy rules. Ethics therefore touches all three layers: biophony as the subject, geophony and anthrophony as context, and human speech as a privacy boundary.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: disturbance studies document measurable responses to playback, including elevated stress hormones and territory abandonment after excessive playback, which is the evidence base for playback limits.

Math proof

Math proof: limiting playback to 3 repetitions of 10 seconds gives 3 x 10 = 30 seconds of playback per trial.

Source

Playback disturbance studies: stress and territory-abandonment evidence (as of October 2026).
