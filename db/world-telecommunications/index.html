What Telecommunications Is

ID: JAH-TELC-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Telecommunications is the transmission of information over a distance by electrical, radio, optical, or other electromagnetic means. It includes the telephone, radio, television, the internet, and mobile networks, and it lets people speak, write, and share images and data with others anywhere in the world, instantly. Telecommunications works by converting information into signals, sending those signals through a channel, such as a wire, a fibre, or the air, and converting them back at the other end. It is one of the most important technologies of the modern world, because it connects people, businesses, and governments. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Telecommunications began with the electric telegraph in the 1830s and 1840s, when Samuel Morse in the United States and William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in Britain built systems that sent messages as electrical pulses along wires. The telephone, patented by Alexander Graham Bell in 1876, let people speak over wires, and the first telephone exchanges opened in the late 1870s. Radio, developed by Guglielmo Marconi in the 1890s and early 1900s, sent signals without wires, and the first transatlantic radio signal was sent in 1901. Television broadcasting began in the 1930s, satellite communications began with Telstar in 1962, and the internet, developed from the ARPANET in the late 1960s, became public in the 1990s. Mobile phones, first demonstrated in 1973 and widely sold from the 1980s, and fibre optics, developed in the 1970s, have made telecommunications faster, cheaper, and available almost everywhere. In the 20th century, telecommunications became a regulated industry, with governments licensing operators and managing the spectrum, and in the 1980s and 1990s many countries privatised their telephone companies and introduced competition, which lowered prices and spread access. The mobile phone, which began as a luxury, became a necessity, and the internet, which began as a research network, became the main medium of communication. The history of telecommunications is therefore also a history of policy, of markets, and of the changing relationship between technology and society.

Key concepts: A signal is a variation in a physical quantity, such as voltage or light, that carries information. A channel is the path a signal travels, such as a wire, a fibre, or a radio link. A transmitter is a device that sends a signal, and a receiver is a device that receives it. Bandwidth is the range of frequencies a channel can carry, or, in everyday use, the rate at which it can carry data. A network is a set of connected devices and links that lets information travel between any two points. A protocol is a set of rules that devices follow to communicate, so that different makers' equipment can work together. A network effect is the benefit of a larger network: a telephone is more useful when more people have one, and the internet is more useful when more people are on it. A standard is a published specification that equipment must follow to work together, and standards are set by bodies such as the ITU and the IEEE. Interoperability is the ability of equipment from different makers to work together, which is the purpose of standards. A bit is a binary digit, and a byte is eight bits, the units in which digital information is measured.

How it works: Telecommunications works in three stages. First, information, such as speech, text, or video, is converted into a signal: a microphone turns sound into an electrical signal, a camera turns light into a digital signal, and a keyboard turns keystrokes into data. Second, the signal is sent through a channel: it may travel as electricity along a copper wire, as light along a glass fibre, or as radio waves through the air, and it may be amplified, switched, and routed along the way. Third, the receiver converts the signal back into information: a speaker turns an electrical signal into sound, and a screen turns data into an image. Digital systems add a step: the signal is converted into numbers, usually binary, which can be stored, compressed, and corrected for errors, which is why modern telecommunications is almost entirely digital. Telecommunications also depends on the physical layer: the wires, fibres, and radio links that carry signals, and on the power supply, which keeps the network running. A network that loses power stops working, which is why telephone exchanges and base stations have backup batteries and generators, and why the resilience of the network is a matter of planning, not only of technology. The network is also vulnerable to damage, from storms, from earthquakes, and from deliberate attack, and its operators plan for failure by building redundancy, so that traffic can be rerouted when a link is lost.

Worked examples: Take a telephone call. A person speaks into a microphone, which turns the sound into an electrical signal, and the signal travels through the network to the other person's phone, where a speaker turns it back into sound. The whole journey takes a fraction of a second, and the delay, called latency, is usually less than 0.1 seconds for a local call. A second example is a text message: the phone converts the text into digital data, sends it by radio to the nearest base station, and the network routes it to the recipient's phone, usually in a few seconds. A third example is a video call: the camera and microphone produce a stream of data, which is compressed, sent over the internet, and decompressed at the other end, so that two people can see and hear each other in real time, even on opposite sides of the world. A second worked example uses the growth of the internet. In 1995, there were about 16 million internet users in the world, and in 2024 there were more than 5 billion, a rise of more than 300 times in less than 30 years. The number of mobile subscriptions rose from about 1 billion in 2002 to more than 8 billion in 2024, a rise of 8 times in 22 years. These are among the fastest growth rates of any technology in history, and they show why telecommunications is one of the most important industries in the world.

Real applications: Telecommunications is used for almost everything that involves communication at a distance. It carries voice calls, text messages, emails, and video calls between people. It carries business data, financial transactions, and government communications. It broadcasts radio and television, it provides the internet, and it connects the sensors and devices of the Internet of Things. It is also essential in emergencies, when the ability to call for help can save lives, and in disasters, when communications are often the first service to be restored. The study of telecommunications is therefore the study of how the modern world stays connected. Telecommunications also serves science: radio telescopes study the universe, satellites monitor the climate, and the internet lets scientists share data and collaborate across the world. The same networks that carry a phone call also carry the data from a telescope in Chile to a laboratory in Europe, and the same fibre that carries a video also carries the readings from a sensor on the ocean floor. Telecommunications is therefore an infrastructure for all of science, as well as for business and for personal life.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is telecommunications the same as the internet? No, the internet is one part of telecommunications, which also includes the telephone network, radio, television, and satellite links, and the internet itself runs on telecommunications networks. Does telecommunications need wires? No, radio, mobile, and satellite systems work without wires, though the core of the network, which carries the most traffic, is mostly fibre-optic cable, which is a wire of glass. Is telecommunications a recent invention? The telegraph is almost 200 years old, and the telephone is almost 150, but the modern form, digital, mobile, and global, is only a few decades old. Can telecommunications work in space? Yes, satellites carry signals between continents and to spacecraft, and the delay, which is about 0.25 seconds for a geostationary satellite, is the main limit. Is telecommunications a utility? In many countries it is treated as one, because it is essential, and it is regulated to ensure universal access, fair prices, and reliable service, though it is usually provided by private companies rather than by the state. Does telecommunications need government? Yes, for spectrum management, for competition, and for universal service, though the balance between government and market is debated in every country. Can telecommunications be private? A private network, such as a company's internal network, can be built and run by its owner, but it still depends on public networks and on standards to connect to the wider world.

Signals: Analogue and Digital

ID: JAH-TELC-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: An analogue signal is a continuous signal that varies smoothly, like the sound of a voice or the voltage from a microphone, while a digital signal is a signal made of discrete values, usually the binary digits 0 and 1, which represent numbers. Analogue signals were the basis of early telecommunications, from the telephone to radio, but digital signals have taken over, because they can be stored, copied, and transmitted without the gradual loss of quality that affects analogue signals, and because they can be corrected for errors. The change from analogue to digital is one of the most important shifts in the history of telecommunications. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Analogue telecommunications began with the telephone in 1876, which sent the voice as a continuously varying electrical current, and with radio in the 1890s, which sent sound as a modulated radio wave. Digital telecommunications began in the 1930s and 1940s, when engineers learned to convert signals into numbers, notably through the work of Harry Nyquist, who showed in 1928 how often a signal must be sampled, and Claude Shannon, who in 1948 founded information theory and showed the limits of digital communication. The first digital telephone systems appeared in the 1960s, and the change to digital accelerated in the 1980s and 1990s, when mobile phones, compact discs, and the internet made digital the standard. Analogue television broadcasting ended in most countries in the 2000s and 2010s, when digital television took over, and analogue mobile phone networks were switched off in the 2000s, so that today almost all telecommunications is digital. Digital signals have also made new services possible. A digital signal can be compressed, so that a video that would need a gigabit per second uncompressed can be sent at a few megabits per second, and it can be encrypted, so that only the intended recipient can read it. It can be stored, so that a programme can be recorded and watched later, and it can be copied without loss, so that a file can be shared with millions of people. These are the properties that make the internet possible, and they are the reason that the change from analogue to digital is often called a revolution, not merely an improvement.

Key concepts: An analogue signal is a continuous signal, whose value can take any value in a range, such as the voltage from a microphone. A digital signal is a signal made of discrete values, usually binary, 0 and 1, which represent numbers. Sampling is the process of measuring an analogue signal at regular intervals, to turn it into a digital signal. Quantisation is the process of rounding each sample to the nearest of a set of values, which introduces a small error. A bit is a binary digit, 0 or 1, and a byte is eight bits, the unit in which computer data are usually measured. The Nyquist rate is the minimum sampling rate needed to capture a signal without loss, which is twice the highest frequency in the signal. Amplitude modulation, or AM, varies the amplitude of a carrier wave to carry information, and it is used for long-wave and medium-wave radio. Frequency modulation, or FM, varies the frequency, and it gives higher quality sound, which is why it is used for music radio. Phase modulation varies the phase, and it is used in digital systems, where the phase can represent bits. Pulse code modulation, or PCM, is the method used to digitise a telephone call, by sampling and quantising the voice, and it is the basis of digital telephony. Error correction is the addition of extra bits that let the receiver detect and fix errors, which is why digital signals are so robust.

How it works: An analogue signal is sent as it is: a microphone produces a voltage that follows the sound wave, and that voltage is sent along a wire or modulated onto a radio wave. A digital signal is made by sampling the analogue signal many times a second and recording each sample as a number. For a telephone call, the voice is sampled 8,000 times a second, and each sample is recorded as an 8-bit number, giving a data rate of 64,000 bits per second, which is the standard rate for a digital telephone call. At the other end, the numbers are turned back into a voltage, which drives a speaker and reproduces the sound. Digital signals can be compressed, to use less bandwidth, and they can be protected with error-correcting codes, which add extra bits that let the receiver detect and fix errors, so that a digital signal can be copied many times without loss, unlike an analogue signal, which degrades with each copy. Digital signals also have limits. Sampling must be fast enough, or information is lost, a problem called aliasing, in which a high frequency is mistaken for a low one. Quantisation introduces noise, called quantisation noise, which is the price of representing a continuous signal with a finite number of values. And a digital signal needs a clock, a timing reference, so that the receiver knows when each bit arrives, which is why digital systems include synchronisation signals. These are engineering problems, and they are solved by choosing the sampling rate, the number of bits, and the error correction to match the application.

Worked examples: Take a telephone call. The voice is sampled 8,000 times a second, with 8 bits per sample, so the data rate is 8,000 times 8, or 64,000 bits per second, which is 64 kilobits per second. A three-minute call therefore produces 64,000 times 180 seconds, or 11,520,000 bits, which is about 1.44 megabytes. A second example is music: a compact disc samples sound 44,100 times a second, with 16 bits per sample and two channels, for a data rate of about 1.4 million bits per second, which is why a CD holds about 700 megabytes for 80 minutes of music. A third example is the Nyquist rate: the human voice contains frequencies up to about 4,000 hertz, so it must be sampled at least 8,000 times a second, which is why the telephone uses 8,000 samples per second. A second worked example uses aliasing. A signal with a frequency of 6,000 hertz, sampled at 8,000 times a second, is below the Nyquist rate of 12,000 samples per second, so it will be misread as a lower frequency, which is why a telephone, which samples at 8,000 times a second, filters out frequencies above 4,000 hertz before sampling. The filter is as important as the sampler, and it is a reminder that digitisation is not only about sampling but also about preparing the signal. A compact disc, which samples at 44,100 times a second, can capture frequencies up to about 22,000 hertz, which covers the range of human hearing, up to about 20,000 hertz.

Real applications: Analogue and digital signals are used throughout telecommunications. Digital signals carry telephone calls, text messages, internet data, and television, because they are robust and flexible. Analogue signals are still used in the first stage, where a microphone or a camera produces an analogue signal that is then digitised, and in radio, where the digital signal is modulated onto an analogue radio wave for transmission. The understanding of signals is therefore the foundation of all telecommunications, and it is the reason that the subject is taught as a branch of electrical engineering as well as of computer science. Analogue and digital signals are also used in combination. A digital signal is often modulated onto an analogue carrier for transmission, as in Wi-Fi, where the digital data are modulated onto a radio wave, and in fibre, where the digital data are modulated onto light. The receiver demodulates the carrier to recover the digital signal, and then decodes it. The combination is the basis of all modern telecommunications: digital for processing and storage, analogue for transmission over the physical channel, and the two together make the system work.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is digital always better than analogue? For most purposes yes, because digital signals can be copied and corrected without loss, but analogue signals can be simpler and can have higher resolution in some applications, such as vinyl records, which some listeners prefer. Does digital mean the signal is perfect? No, sampling and quantisation introduce small errors, and a digital signal can still be lost if the channel is too noisy, though error correction makes it far more robust than analogue. What is the difference between a bit and a byte? A bit is a single binary digit, 0 or 1, and a byte is eight bits, so a data rate of 64 kilobits per second is 8 kilobytes per second. Can an analogue signal be sent over a digital network? Yes, it is first digitised, sent as digital data, and then converted back to analogue at the other end, which is how a telephone call works today. Is analogue radio still used? Yes, AM and FM radio are still broadcast in most countries, though digital radio is growing, and many listeners still use analogue sets. Does digital sound better? Digital sound can be better, because it does not degrade with distance or with copying, but a poorly compressed digital signal can sound worse than a good analogue one, so the quality depends on the implementation, not only on the technology. Can a digital signal be sent over an analogue channel? Yes, by modulation, which is how a modem sends digital data over an analogue telephone line, and how Wi-Fi sends digital data over a radio wave.

The Telephone

ID: JAH-TELC-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: The telephone is a device that lets two people speak to each other over a distance, by converting sound into an electrical signal, sending it through a network, and converting it back into sound. It was the first telecommunications technology that let people have a conversation in real time over a long distance, and it transformed business, government, and personal life. The telephone network, called the public switched telephone network, was the largest machine ever built, connecting hundreds of millions of phones, and it is now being replaced by internet-based calling, though the telephone itself remains in use. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The telephone was patented by Alexander Graham Bell in the United States on 7 March 1876, though Elisha Gray filed a similar patent on the same day, and the question of who invented it was disputed for years. The first telephone exchange, where callers could be connected to each other, opened in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1878, and the first long-distance line, between Boston and New York, opened in 1884. In the early 20th century, telephone networks spread around the world, and the first transatlantic telephone cable, TAT-1, opened in 1956, letting people call across the Atlantic. Automatic exchanges, which replaced human operators, spread in the 20th century, and the first mobile phone call was made by Martin Cooper of Motorola on 3 April 1973. In the 2000s, voice over internet protocol, or VoIP, let telephone calls travel over the internet, and the old copper telephone network is now being retired in many countries, including the United Kingdom, where the switch-off is under way in the 2020s. The telephone network also had a social history. In the early days, telephone operators, mostly women, connected calls by hand, and they were the human face of the network, knowing their subscribers and their habits. The introduction of automatic exchanges in the 20th century removed the operator, and the introduction of direct dialling let callers dial long-distance numbers themselves, without an operator's help. The telephone also changed language: the word hello, popularised by Thomas Edison as a telephone greeting, became the standard way of answering a call, and the telephone directory became one of the most widely distributed books in the world.

Key concepts: A telephone exchange is a building where telephone lines are connected, so that a caller can reach the person they are calling. A subscriber line is the wire from a home or office to the local exchange. A trunk line is a high-capacity line between exchanges, which carries many calls at once. The public switched telephone network, or PSTN, is the worldwide network of telephone exchanges and lines. Voice over internet protocol, or VoIP, is a technology that sends telephone calls as data over the internet, rather than over a dedicated telephone network. A mobile phone is a telephone that connects by radio to a base station, rather than by a wire to an exchange. A telephone number is an address in the telephone network, made of a country code, an area code, and a subscriber number, which together identify a single line. A private branch exchange, or PBX, is a telephone exchange inside a company, which connects its internal phones and links them to the public network. A trunk call is a long-distance call, carried on a trunk line between exchanges. A local call is a call within the same exchange area, which was once cheaper than a trunk call, though the distinction has faded with flat-rate pricing. Caller identification, or caller ID, is a service that shows the caller's number to the recipient, introduced in the 1980s.

How it works: A telephone call works by converting sound into an electrical signal and back again. The caller speaks into a microphone, which produces a voltage that follows the sound wave. In a traditional telephone, that voltage travels along a copper wire to the local exchange, which connects it to the trunk line, and then to the exchange near the recipient, and then along the subscriber line to the recipient's phone, where a speaker turns the voltage back into sound. In a modern digital telephone, the voice is sampled 8,000 times a second and sent as 64 kilobits per second of data, and in a mobile phone it is sent by radio to the nearest base station, which connects it to the network. The exchange, or its modern equivalent, a switch or a router, finds the best route for the call, and the call is set up in a fraction of a second. The telephone network was also the first network to be regulated as a monopoly, because it was a natural monopoly: it was cheaper for one company to provide the network than for two to duplicate it. The regulator set the prices, the quality, and the obligation to serve everyone, and the monopoly was accepted as the price of universal service. In the 1980s, the monopoly was challenged, and competition was introduced, first in long-distance calls and then in local calls, which lowered prices and increased choice, though it also raised questions about who would serve rural areas, where competition is not profitable, which is why universal service obligations remain.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A traditional telephone call uses 64 kilobits per second of data, so a one-hour call uses 64,000 times 3,600 seconds, or 230,400,000 bits, which is about 28.8 megabytes. The first transatlantic telephone cable, TAT-1, opened in 1956 and could carry 36 simultaneous calls, while a modern fibre-optic cable can carry millions of calls at once. The first mobile phone, the Motorola DynaTAC, weighed about 1.1 kilograms and could talk for 30 minutes before its battery ran out, while a modern smartphone weighs about 200 grams and can talk for many hours. The number of telephone lines in the world peaked at about 1.2 billion in the mid-2000s, before mobile phones overtook them, and there are now more than 8 billion mobile subscriptions in the world. A second worked example uses the growth of the network. In 1900, there were about 1.5 million telephones in the United States, and in 2000 there were about 200 million, a rise of more than 100 times in a century. In the United Kingdom, the number of telephones rose from about 200,000 in 1900 to about 30 million in 2000. The growth was driven by falling prices, by the spread of exchanges, and by the value of the network, which grows as more people join it, a network effect that made the telephone more useful with every new subscriber.

Real applications: The telephone is used for voice calls, for emergency calls, and for the fax and dial-up internet that once ran over its lines. Its network is the foundation of the modern telecommunications system, and its numbering plan, with country codes and area codes, is still used for mobile phones and for internet calling. The telephone also changed society: it let businesses coordinate across distances, it let families stay in touch, and it created the expectation that a person can be reached at any time, an expectation that mobile phones have made universal. The study of the telephone is therefore the study of how a single invention changed the way people live and work. The telephone also changed the way people speak. A telephone call is a conversation without visual cues, so people learned to use their voice to convey meaning, to say hello and goodbye, and to take turns without seeing each other. The telephone created new social norms, such as not calling late at night, and new anxieties, such as the fear of missing a call, which the answering machine and then voicemail were invented to solve. The mobile phone has changed the norms again, by making the phone personal and always available, so that a call can reach a person anywhere, not only at home or at work.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the telephone? Alexander Graham Bell patented it in 1876, but Elisha Gray filed a similar patent the same day, and Antonio Meucci had demonstrated a similar device earlier, so the invention is now seen as the work of several people, with Bell as the one who patented and commercialised it. Is the telephone obsolete? The traditional landline is being retired, but the telephone as a way of speaking to someone at a distance is not, and it lives on in mobile phones and in internet calling. Can a telephone work without electricity? A traditional telephone is powered by the exchange through its own line, so it works in a power cut, which is one reason it was valued for emergencies, though modern fibre and mobile phones need their own power. Why do phone numbers have country codes? Because the international telephone numbering plan, set by the International Telecommunication Union, assigns each country a code, so that a call can be routed across the world. Is the landline dead? The number of landlines is falling, as people rely on mobiles, but landlines are still used by businesses, by older people, and in areas with poor mobile coverage, and the copper network is being replaced by fibre, which will carry voice as data, so the landline as a service will continue, even if the copper wire does not. Can I still make a call in a power cut? A traditional copper landline is powered by the exchange, so it works in a power cut, but a fibre landline needs power at home, so it does not, which is why the switch to fibre includes backup power for vulnerable users. Why do phone numbers have area codes? Because the numbering plan divides the country into areas, so that a call can be routed to the right exchange, and the area code is part of the address, though with number portability a number can now be kept when a person moves.

Radio

ID: JAH-TELC-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Radio is the transmission of information by electromagnetic waves through the air, without wires. It is used for broadcasting, for mobile phones, for Wi-Fi, for satellite links, and for radar, and it is the basis of all wireless telecommunications. Radio works by modulating a radio wave, which is a wave of a certain frequency, with the information to be sent, and by receiving that wave with an antenna and demodulating it to recover the information. The radio spectrum, the range of frequencies used for radio, is a shared resource, managed by governments and by international agreement. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Radio began with the theory of James Clerk Maxwell, who predicted electromagnetic waves in the 1860s, and the experiments of Heinrich Hertz, who produced and detected them in 1887. Guglielmo Marconi built the first practical wireless telegraph in the 1890s, and in December 1901 he claimed to have sent the first transatlantic radio signal, from Cornwall to Newfoundland, a distance of about 3,500 kilometres. In the 1920s, radio broadcasting began, with the first regular broadcasts in the United States and the United Kingdom, and the British Broadcasting Corporation, the BBC, was founded in 1922. In the 1930s, frequency modulation, or FM, was developed by Edwin Armstrong, giving higher quality sound. After the Second World War, radio spread to television, to radar, and to mobile communications, and in the 21st century digital radio, which sends sound as data, has begun to replace analogue broadcasting in many countries. Radio has also been used for navigation and for science. The first radio navigation systems, developed in the 1930s and 1940s, let aircraft and ships find their position by the timing of radio signals, and the modern Global Positioning System, which uses satellites, is a development of the same idea. Radio astronomy, which began in the 1930s when Karl Jansky detected radio waves from the Milky Way, has discovered pulsars, quasars, and the cosmic microwave background, and it is one of the most productive branches of astronomy. Radar, developed in the 1930s and 1940s, uses radio waves to detect objects, and it is used for air traffic control, for weather forecasting, and for speed enforcement.

Key concepts: An electromagnetic wave is a wave of electric and magnetic fields that travels through space at the speed of light. Frequency is the number of waves per second, measured in hertz, and wavelength is the distance between two peaks of the wave. Modulation is the process of impressing information onto a radio wave, by varying its amplitude, its frequency, or its phase. AM, or amplitude modulation, varies the amplitude, and FM, or frequency modulation, varies the frequency. An antenna is a conductor that radiates or receives radio waves. The radio spectrum is the range of frequencies used for radio, from about 3 kilohertz to 300 gigahertz. A radar is a system that sends out radio waves and listens for their reflection, to detect objects and to measure their distance and speed. A radio telescope is a large dish that collects radio waves from space, to study the universe. A beacon is a radio transmitter that sends a regular signal, used for navigation, so that a receiver can find its position by the signal's direction or timing. A repeater is a station that receives a radio signal and retransmits it, to extend its range, used in hilly areas where a direct signal is blocked. A transceiver is a device that can both transmit and receive, such as a walkie-talkie or a mobile phone.

How it works: Radio works by generating a radio wave at a chosen frequency, called the carrier, and modulating it with the information signal, such as sound. The modulated wave is fed to an antenna, which radiates it into space, where it travels at the speed of light in all directions, or in a beam if the antenna is directional. At the receiver, an antenna picks up the wave, a tuner selects the wanted frequency, and a demodulator recovers the information signal, which is then amplified and turned into sound by a speaker, or into data by a decoder. The strength of the signal falls with distance, roughly as the square of the distance, so a transmitter must be powerful enough, or the receiver sensitive enough, for the signal to be heard, and the choice of frequency matters: low frequencies travel farther and follow the Earth's curve, while high frequencies carry more data but travel in straight lines and are blocked by hills and buildings. Radio also has a history of regulation, because the spectrum is shared and interference is a problem. The first international radio conference was held in Berlin in 1906, and it set the first rules for radio, including the distress signal SOS, which was adopted in 1906 and used famously by the Titanic in 1912. The International Telecommunication Union has managed the spectrum since then, and national regulators license transmitters, so that a broadcaster in one city does not interfere with a broadcaster in another. The regulation of radio is a model for the regulation of all telecommunications, because it shows how a shared resource can be managed by agreement, rather than by ownership.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. The BBC's first broadcasts were on long wave, at a frequency of about 200 kilohertz, with a wavelength of about 1,500 metres, which is why long-wave stations could be heard over hundreds of kilometres. FM radio uses frequencies around 100 megahertz, with a wavelength of about 3 metres, and it carries higher quality sound but over a shorter range, typically tens of kilometres. A mobile phone uses frequencies around 900 megahertz or 1,800 megahertz, with wavelengths of about 33 centimetres and 17 centimetres, and it communicates with a base station a few kilometres away. The speed of light is about 300,000 kilometres per second, so a radio signal crosses the Atlantic, about 5,000 kilometres, in about 0.017 seconds, which is why radio seems instantaneous. A second worked example uses the distance a radio wave travels in a given time. A radio wave travels at the speed of light, 300,000 kilometres per second, so in one millisecond it travels 300 kilometres, and in one microsecond it travels 300 metres. A radar that measures the time for a pulse to return can therefore measure distance: if the pulse returns after 0.001 seconds, the object is about 150 kilometres away, because the pulse travelled 300 kilometres in total, there and back, so the distance is half that. The calculation is the basis of radar, and it is why radar can measure distance so precisely, by measuring time.

Real applications: Radio is used for broadcasting, for communications, and for navigation. It carries music, news, and speech to millions of listeners, it connects mobile phones to their networks, it links aircraft to air traffic control, and it guides ships at sea. It is also used for radar, which detects objects by the reflection of radio waves, and for radio astronomy, which studies the universe by its radio emissions. The radio spectrum is a valuable resource, and it is managed by the International Telecommunication Union, which allocates frequencies to different uses, and by national regulators, which license transmitters to prevent interference. Radio is also used for emergency communications, for amateur radio, and for the Internet of Things, where small devices send data by radio over short distances. Amateur radio, practised by licensed enthusiasts, has contributed to the development of radio, and amateur operators have provided communications in disasters, when other systems have failed, which is why the hobby is valued by emergency planners. The study of radio is therefore the study of a technology that is both old and new, and that remains essential, even as newer technologies appear.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is radio the same as wireless? Radio is the main form of wireless communication, but wireless also includes infrared and visible light links, though radio is by far the most important. Does radio work in space? Yes, radio waves travel through the vacuum of space, which is how spacecraft communicate with Earth, though the delay grows with distance: a signal to Mars takes between 3 and 22 minutes, depending on the planets' positions. Can radio waves be harmful? Radio waves at the power levels used for broadcasting and for mobile phones are non-ionising, meaning they do not have enough energy to damage DNA, and the scientific consensus, based on many studies, is that they do not cause harm at the levels used, though the subject is studied continuously. Why do I lose radio reception in a tunnel? Because the tunnel blocks the radio waves, which cannot pass through rock or metal, and the signal must be carried into the tunnel by a cable or a repeater. Is radio only for broadcasting? No, radio is used for mobile phones, for Wi-Fi, for satellite links, for radar, and for navigation, and broadcasting is only one of its uses, though it was the first and the most familiar. Can radio waves travel through walls? Yes, low frequencies pass through walls more easily than high frequencies, which is why a long-wave station can be heard indoors while a Wi-Fi signal may be weak in another room. Why does FM sound better than AM? Because FM is less affected by the static and interference that affect AM, and because FM uses a wider channel, which can carry higher quality sound, though it has a shorter range.

Television Broadcasting

ID: JAH-TELC-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Television broadcasting is the transmission of moving images and sound to many receivers at once, by radio waves, by cable, or by satellite. It began as a way of sending live pictures over the air, and it has grown into a system that carries hundreds of channels, on-demand video, and interactive services. Television works by capturing a scene with a camera, converting it into a signal, transmitting that signal, and displaying it on a screen. The change from analogue to digital television, completed in most countries in the 2000s and 2010s, increased the number of channels and the quality of the picture. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Television was developed in the 1920s and 1930s by several inventors, including John Logie Baird in the United Kingdom, who demonstrated a mechanical television system in 1926, and Philo Farnsworth in the United States, who demonstrated an electronic system in 1927. The BBC began the world's first regular television service in 1936, from Alexandra Palace in London, and the first television broadcasts in the United States began in the late 1930s. Colour television was introduced in the 1950s and 1960s, and satellite television began in the 1960s, letting channels reach across continents. Cable television spread in the 1970s and 1980s, and digital television, which sends pictures as data, began in the 1990s and replaced analogue in most countries by the 2010s. Streaming television, which sends video over the internet, began in the 2000s and has become a major way of watching, alongside broadcast. Television has also been a force in politics and in culture. The first televised political debate, between John F. Kennedy and Richard Nixon in the United States in 1960, is often credited with helping Kennedy win the election, because viewers thought he looked better on television, while radio listeners thought Nixon had won the debate. The live broadcast of the Moon landing in 1969 was watched by an estimated 600 million people, the largest television audience up to that time, and it showed the power of television to create a shared experience. Television has also been criticised, for its effects on children, for its portrayal of violence, and for its influence on politics, and the debate about its effects continues, as it does for every new medium.

Key concepts: A broadcast is a transmission intended for many receivers, as opposed to a point-to-point call between two people. A channel is a frequency band or a stream assigned to a television service. A transmitter is a station that radiates the television signal, and a receiver, or television set, displays it. Digital television sends pictures as compressed digital data, using standards such as DVB, Digital Video Broadcasting, in Europe, and ATSC in North America. Streaming is the delivery of video over the internet, in which the video is sent as data and played as it arrives, rather than being downloaded first. A satellite is a spacecraft that receives a television signal from the ground and retransmits it to a wide area. A set-top box is a device that receives a television signal and displays it on a screen, used for cable, satellite, and digital television. Aerial, or antenna, is a device that receives broadcast television signals, usually mounted on a roof. A terrestrial broadcast is a broadcast sent from a ground transmitter, as opposed to a satellite broadcast, which is sent from a satellite, or a cable broadcast, which is sent through a cable. A public service broadcaster is a broadcaster funded by the public, such as the BBC, which has a duty to inform, to educate, and to entertain, rather than only to make a profit. A commercial broadcaster is a broadcaster funded by advertising, which must attract viewers to sell advertising space.

How it works: Television works by scanning a scene into lines and frames. A camera captures the scene many times a second, typically 25 or 30 frames a second, and each frame is divided into lines, typically 625 lines in the old European standard and 525 in the old American standard. The brightness and colour of each point are turned into a signal, which is transmitted by radio, by cable, or by satellite. A digital television system compresses the data, because uncompressed video would need far more bandwidth than is available: a high-definition picture, 1920 by 1080 pixels, at 25 frames a second, with 24 bits per pixel, would need about 1.2 billion bits per second uncompressed, but compression reduces it to about 5 to 10 million bits per second, a reduction of more than 100 times. The receiver decompresses the data and displays the picture on the screen, synchronised with the sound. Television broadcasting also depends on standards, which ensure that a television set bought in one country can receive broadcasts in another, or at least in the same region. The European DVB standard, the American ATSC standard, and the Japanese ISDB standard are different, which is why a television bought in Europe may not work in the United States, though modern sets often support several standards. The change from analogue to digital television required new standards, new transmitters, and new receivers, and it was coordinated by governments, which set a date for the switch-off of analogue and helped viewers to prepare, by providing information and, in some cases, financial help.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A standard-definition digital television channel uses about 3 to 5 megabits per second, a high-definition channel uses about 5 to 10 megabits per second, and a 4K ultra-high-definition channel uses about 15 to 25 megabits per second. A two-hour high-definition film at 8 megabits per second contains 8 million times 7,200 seconds, or about 57.6 billion bits, which is about 7.2 gigabytes. The BBC's first television service in 1936 reached only a few thousand viewers in London, while today television reaches billions of people worldwide, and the number of television households in the world is over 1.6 billion. A satellite in geostationary orbit, 35,786 kilometres up, can cover a whole continent, which is why satellite television can reach places that terrestrial transmitters cannot. A second worked example uses the number of pixels in a television picture. A standard-definition picture has 720 by 576 pixels, or about 415,000 pixels, while a high-definition picture has 1920 by 1080 pixels, or about 2.1 million pixels, about five times as many. A 4K picture has 3840 by 2160 pixels, or about 8.3 million pixels, about four times as many as high definition and 20 times as many as standard definition. The rise in pixels is the reason the picture has become sharper, and it is also the reason the bandwidth needed has risen, from a few megabits per second for standard definition to 15 to 25 megabits per second for 4K.

Real applications: Television broadcasting is used for news, entertainment, education, and sport, and it is one of the most widely used media in the world. It is also used for public information, for emergency warnings, and for distance learning, where a broadcast can reach students in remote areas. The advertising carried by commercial television funds much of the programming, and the licence fee funds public broadcasters such as the BBC. The study of television broadcasting is therefore the study of a medium that shapes culture, politics, and public opinion, as well as a technology that carries pictures through the air. Television is also used for education and for public information. Educational television has taught children to read, has taught adults new skills, and has brought the world into classrooms, and public information broadcasts have warned of emergencies, have explained health advice, and have encouraged voting. The power of television to reach a large audience at once makes it a valuable tool for public purposes, as well as for entertainment, and it is why governments regulate it, to ensure that it serves the public interest as well as the commercial interest.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is television the same as video? Television is a system for broadcasting video to many people, while video is the medium itself, which can also be watched on demand, on a disc, or on the internet. Is analogue television still broadcast? In most countries no, analogue broadcasting was switched off in the 2000s and 2010s, and only digital television is broadcast now, though some countries still have analogue services. Does television need the internet? Broadcast television does not, because it is sent by radio, cable, or satellite, but streaming television does, because it is sent as internet data. Why does television use so much bandwidth? Because video contains a great deal of information: a single high-definition frame has over 2 million pixels, and 25 frames a second means over 50 million pixels a second, which is why compression is essential. Is television in decline? The number of people watching traditional broadcast television is falling, especially among young people, who watch more streaming and more video on phones, but the total amount of video watched is rising, and television, in the sense of video on a screen, is more popular than ever, though it is watched in different ways. Does television affect children? Research shows that the content matters more than the amount: educational programmes can help, while violent or inappropriate content can harm, and the advice is to choose content carefully and to watch together. Can television be interactive? Modern television can be interactive, through the internet, letting viewers choose programmes, vote, and shop, and the distinction between television and the internet is blurring, as both are delivered over the same connection.

The Internet

ID: JAH-TELC-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: The internet is a global network of networks, which connects billions of devices and lets them exchange data using a common set of protocols, mainly the Internet Protocol, or IP, and the Transmission Control Protocol, or TCP. It carries the World Wide Web, email, video, and countless other services, and it runs on the telecommunications networks described in this database: fibre-optic cables, mobile networks, and satellites. The internet is not a single network but an agreement between networks to carry each other's traffic, and it is the largest and most important telecommunications system ever built. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The internet began as the ARPANET, a research network funded by the United States Department of Defense, which sent its first message in 1969 between computers at the University of California, Los Angeles, and the Stanford Research Institute. The protocols TCP/IP, which are the basis of the internet, were developed in the 1970s by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, and they were adopted by the ARPANET in 1983. The World Wide Web, invented by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN in 1989, made the internet easy to use, by linking documents with hyperlinks, and the first web browser, Mosaic, appeared in 1993. In the 1990s, the internet became commercial, with internet service providers selling access to the public, and in the 2000s it became mobile, with smartphones bringing it to billions of people. Today, more than 5 billion people use the internet, and it carries a large share of the world's communications. The internet has also changed the economy. It has let small firms reach customers worldwide, it has created new industries, such as e-commerce, social media, and cloud computing, and it has disrupted old ones, such as newspapers, music, and retail. The economist's term for this is creative destruction, the process by which new technologies replace old ones, and the internet is one of the most powerful examples. The internet has also created new jobs, in software, in data, and in digital marketing, and it has changed the nature of work, by letting people work from anywhere, which was shown dramatically during the pandemic of 2020, when millions of people worked from home using the internet.

Key concepts: An internet service provider, or ISP, is a company that sells access to the internet. A router is a device that forwards data between networks, choosing the best path. A packet is a small unit of data, with a header that says where it is from and where it is going, and a payload that carries the data. The Internet Protocol, or IP, is the protocol that addresses packets and routes them, and an IP address is the numerical address of a device on the internet. The Domain Name System, or DNS, is the system that translates human-readable names, such as example.com, into IP addresses. The World Wide Web is a system of linked documents, accessed through browsers, which runs on the internet but is not the same as the internet. Cloud computing is the delivery of computing services, such as storage and software, over the internet, from remote servers, rather than from a local computer. A data centre is a large building that houses many servers, which store data and run programs for users around the world. A search engine is a service that indexes the Web and lets users find information, such as Google, which indexes billions of pages. An email is a message sent over the internet, using the Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, or SMTP, and it was one of the first internet services, predating the Web. A social network is a service that lets people connect and share with each other online.

How it works: The internet works by breaking data into packets, sending them independently across the network, and reassembling them at the destination. When a user requests a web page, the browser looks up the domain name in the DNS, finds the IP address of the server, and sends a request as a series of packets, each of which is routed by routers along the best available path, which may differ from packet to packet. The server sends the page back as packets, and the browser reassembles them and displays the page. If a packet is lost, TCP asks for it to be sent again, so the data arrive complete and in order. The system is decentralised: there is no single centre, and if one link fails, packets can take another route, which is why the internet is resilient to failures, though it is not immune to them. The internet is governed by a set of organisations, not by a single government. The Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers, or ICANN, coordinates domain names and IP addresses, the Internet Engineering Task Force, or IETF, sets the technical standards, and the Internet Society promotes the internet's development. The governance is multi-stakeholder, meaning governments, companies, and users all take part, and it is a model for how a global resource can be managed without a single owner. The debate about internet governance, including who should control domain names and whether governments should have more power, is ongoing, and it reflects the tension between the internet as a global network and the desire of governments to control what happens within their borders.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. The internet carries data at rates measured in terabits per second on its busiest links, and the total traffic is measured in exabytes per month, with one exabyte equal to a billion gigabytes. A typical home broadband connection in the United Kingdom is 50 to 100 megabits per second, which can download a 1-gigabyte file in about 80 to 160 seconds, because 1 gigabyte is 8,000 megabits, divided by 100 megabits per second is 80 seconds. The first ARPANET link ran at 50 kilobits per second, which is 2,000 times slower than a modern 100-megabit connection. There are more than 5 billion internet users in the world, and the number of connected devices, including phones, computers, and sensors, is estimated at more than 15 billion. A second worked example uses the growth of the Web. The first website, created by Tim Berners-Lee at CERN in 1991, was a single page explaining the Web, and by 1995 there were about 20,000 websites, by 2000 about 17 million, and by 2024 more than 1.8 billion, though many are inactive. The growth is exponential, doubling every few years in the early days, and it shows how a simple idea, linking documents with hyperlinks, can grow into a global system, because each new page makes the Web more useful, a network effect that drives its growth.

Real applications: The internet is used for almost everything: for communication, for commerce, for education, for entertainment, and for government. It carries email, video calls, online shopping, banking, and streaming, and it is the platform for the World Wide Web, for social media, and for cloud computing, in which data and programs are stored on remote servers rather than on a local device. The internet has changed the economy, by letting firms reach customers worldwide, and it has changed society, by letting people communicate instantly across the world. Its governance, by organisations such as the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers, is a matter of international cooperation, and its future, including the growth of the Internet of Things, is a central question for telecommunications. The internet has also raised questions about privacy, about security, and about the concentration of power. The data collected by websites and by apps can reveal a great deal about a person, and the laws that protect privacy, such as the General Data Protection Regulation in the European Union, which came into force in 2018, are an attempt to give users control over their data. The security of the internet, which was not designed with security in mind, is a continuing challenge, and the concentration of the internet in a few large companies has raised questions about competition, which are now the subject of regulation in the European Union and elsewhere.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the internet the same as the World Wide Web? No, the internet is the network, and the Web is a service that runs on it, along with email, video calls, and many others. Who owns the internet? No one: it is a network of networks, owned by many companies, universities, and governments, and coordinated by standards bodies and by agreements between networks. Can the internet be switched off? A single country can restrict access, and a single network can fail, but the internet as a whole has no single switch, because it is decentralised, though it depends on physical cables and on the power supply, which can be disrupted. Is the internet free? Access is sold by providers, and many services are funded by advertising, so the internet is not free to use, though much of its content is free to read. Is the internet a public good? It has some features of a public good, because it is non-rival in use, but it is provided by private companies and it is not free, so it is better described as a privately provided infrastructure with public benefits, which is why governments regulate it and subsidise access in rural areas. Can the internet be censored? Governments can block access to sites, can filter traffic, and can shut down the internet in their country, as has happened in several countries, though the decentralised design makes total control difficult, and users can often circumvent blocks with VPNs and other tools. Does the internet make people smarter? It gives access to more information than ever before, but it also spreads misinformation, and the skill of judging what is true has become more important, not less, which is why digital literacy is now taught in schools.

Mobile Phones and Cellular Networks

ID: JAH-TELC-000007 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A mobile phone is a portable telephone that connects by radio to a cellular network, a network of base stations, each serving a small area called a cell, which together cover a country. When a phone moves, the network hands it over from one cell to the next, so the call continues without interruption. Mobile phones have grown from simple voice devices into smartphones, which are computers with a phone, a camera, and an internet connection, and there are now more mobile subscriptions in the world than people. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The first mobile phone call was made by Martin Cooper of Motorola on 3 April 1973, using a handset that weighed about 1.1 kilograms. The first commercial mobile networks opened in the early 1980s, in Japan and in Scandinavia, using analogue technology, called first generation, or 1G. Digital mobile networks, 2G, began in the early 1990s, with the GSM standard in Europe, which let phones send text messages and roam between countries. Third generation, 3G, began in the early 2000s and brought mobile internet, fourth generation, 4G, began in the late 2000s and brought fast mobile broadband, and fifth generation, 5G, began in the late 2010s and brought higher speeds and lower latency. The first smartphone, the IBM Simon, appeared in 1994, and the iPhone, launched in 2007, made the smartphone the dominant form of mobile phone. Mobile phones have also changed the way people use money. In Kenya, the mobile money service M-Pesa, launched in 2007, lets people send money by text message, and it is used by millions of people who do not have a bank account, to pay for goods, to send money to family, and to save. The service has been studied by economists, who found that it reduced poverty, by letting people send money quickly in an emergency, and it is now a model for mobile money in other countries. The mobile phone has therefore become a bank, as well as a telephone, and it has brought financial services to people who were previously excluded.

Key concepts: A cell is the area served by one base station, typically a few kilometres across in a city and larger in the countryside. A base station is a fixed radio station that communicates with mobile phones in its cell, and it is connected to the core network by a fibre or microwave link. A handover is the transfer of a call from one cell to another as the phone moves. Roaming is the use of a phone on another operator's network, for example when travelling abroad. A SIM, or subscriber identity module, is a small card or chip that identifies the subscriber to the network. A generation, such as 4G or 5G, is a set of standards for mobile networks, each faster and more capable than the last. A smartphone app, or application, is a program that runs on a phone, providing a service, such as messaging, banking, or navigation. A mobile network operator is a company that runs a mobile network, such as EE, O2, Three, and Vodafone in the United Kingdom. A virtual network operator is a company that sells mobile services but does not own a network, renting capacity from an operator instead. A handset is a mobile phone, and a tariff is the price plan for using it, which may include a number of minutes, texts, and data. A data allowance is the amount of data a tariff includes, after which the speed may be reduced or extra charges may apply.

How it works: A mobile phone works by radio. When it is switched on, it searches for the strongest base station and registers with the network, which records its location. When a call is made, the phone sends a radio signal to the base station, which forwards it to the core network, which routes it to the recipient, either another mobile phone or a fixed phone. As the phone moves, the network measures the signal strength from neighbouring base stations, and when another cell becomes stronger, it hands the call over, in a fraction of a second, so the user does not notice. Data work the same way: the phone sends packets by radio to the base station, which forwards them to the internet. The frequencies are reused in cells that are far apart, so the same frequency can be used many times across a country, which is why the system is called cellular. Mobile networks also have to manage the spectrum efficiently, because the demand for data grows faster than the spectrum available. They do this by using smaller cells, so that the same frequency can be reused more often, by using better modulation, so that more bits are carried per hertz, and by using Wi-Fi to offload traffic, so that a phone in a home or office uses Wi-Fi instead of the mobile network. The combination of these techniques has kept pace with the growth in demand, though the demand continues to grow, and the next generation, 6G, will need new spectrum and new techniques to keep up.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A 4G network can deliver speeds of about 100 megabits per second to a phone, and a 5G network can deliver over 1,000 megabits per second in ideal conditions, though typical speeds are lower. A base station in a city serves a cell a few hundred metres to a few kilometres across, and a country the size of the United Kingdom has tens of thousands of base stations. The first mobile phone weighed 1.1 kilograms and cost about 4,000 dollars in 1983, while a modern smartphone weighs about 200 grams and costs a few hundred pounds, and it is far more powerful than the computers that guided Apollo to the Moon. There are more than 8 billion mobile subscriptions in the world, and the number of smartphones is over 6 billion, so mobile phones are the most widely used telecommunications device in history. A second worked example uses the growth of mobile data. In 2010, the average mobile user in the United Kingdom used about 100 megabytes of data a month, and in 2024 the average was about 10 gigabytes a month, a rise of 100 times in 14 years. A gigabyte is 1,000 megabytes, so the rise is from 0.1 gigabytes to 10 gigabytes, and it reflects the shift from text and email to video, which uses far more data. A video stream at high definition uses about 1 gigabyte per hour, so 10 gigabytes a month is about 10 hours of video, or about 20 minutes a day, which is a modest amount by today's standards, and the average continues to rise.

Real applications: Mobile phones are used for calls, texts, internet access, photography, navigation, and payment, and they have become the main way most people access the internet, especially in poor countries, where a mobile phone is often a person's first computer. They are used by businesses to reach customers, by governments to deliver services, and by aid agencies to send money and information to people in need. The cellular network is also used for the Internet of Things, connecting sensors, vehicles, and machines. The study of mobile phones is therefore the study of how a single device has changed the way the world communicates, works, and lives. Mobile phones are also used for health, for education, and for agriculture. In poor countries, a phone can give a farmer the price of crops in the market, a patient the result of a test, and a student access to lessons, all of which were difficult or impossible before the phone. The phone is therefore a tool for development, as well as for communication, and the spread of mobile phones is one of the most important developments in the fight against poverty, because it brings information and services to people who were previously isolated.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are mobile phones safe? The radio waves used by mobile phones are non-ionising, and the scientific consensus, based on many studies, is that they do not cause harm at the levels used, though the subject is studied continuously and phones are tested against safety limits. Does 5G cause harm? No, 5G uses radio waves in the same range as earlier generations and as Wi-Fi, and there is no evidence that it causes harm, though claims to the contrary have spread online and have been rejected by health authorities. Can a mobile phone work without a signal? It can use Wi-Fi for calls and data, and it can work as a camera and a computer, but it cannot make a cellular call without a base station in range. Why do phones get hot? Because the processor and the radio use power, and the heat is the waste from that power, which is why a phone gets warm during a long call or when it is charging. Is a smartphone a computer? Yes, a modern smartphone is more powerful than the computers that were used to send people to the Moon, and it is used as a computer, for email, for browsing, and for apps, as well as a phone. Does a mobile phone need a SIM? A SIM identifies the subscriber to the network, and it can be a physical card or an eSIM, which is built into the phone and can be programmed remotely, and a phone without a SIM can still make emergency calls and can use Wi-Fi. Why does my phone lose signal in a lift? Because the lift is a metal box, which blocks radio waves, and the signal cannot reach the phone inside, which is why lifts and basements often have no coverage, unless a repeater is installed.

Satellites

ID: JAH-TELC-000008 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A communications satellite is a spacecraft in orbit around Earth that receives signals from the ground and retransmits them to another place on the ground, letting signals cross continents and oceans without cables. Satellites are used for television, for telephone calls, for the internet, for navigation, and for weather forecasting, and they are essential for reaching remote places, ships at sea, and aircraft in flight. Most communications satellites are in geostationary orbit, 35,786 kilometres above the equator, where they stay over the same point on Earth, though new constellations of low Earth orbit satellites, only a few hundred kilometres up, are now providing internet to remote areas. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, was launched by the Soviet Union on 4 October 1957, and it transmitted a simple radio beep. The first communications satellite, Telstar 1, was launched on 10 July 1962, and it relayed the first live television pictures across the Atlantic. The first geostationary communications satellite, Syncom 3, was launched in 1964, and it relayed television from the Tokyo Olympics. In 1965, Intelsat 1, nicknamed Early Bird, became the first commercial communications satellite, with a capacity of 240 telephone calls. The Global Positioning System, or GPS, began in the 1970s and became fully operational in 1995, providing navigation to the whole world. In the 2010s and 2020s, companies began launching constellations of hundreds or thousands of low Earth orbit satellites, such as Starlink, to provide internet, and the number of satellites in orbit has grown rapidly, to more than 10,000 active satellites in the 2020s. Satellites have also been used for science. The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, is a satellite that observes the universe, and the James Webb Space Telescope, launched in 2021, observes in infrared, and both have transformed astronomy. Earth observation satellites, such as Landsat, launched in 1972, and the Copernicus Sentinels, launched from 2014, monitor the planet's land, oceans, and atmosphere, and their data are used for weather forecasting, for farming, and for climate science. The study of satellites is therefore not only about communications but also about science, and the same technology that carries a telephone call also carries the data that let us understand the Earth and the universe.

Key concepts: A geostationary orbit is an orbit 35,786 kilometres above the equator, in which a satellite circles Earth in 24 hours, so it stays over the same point on the ground. A low Earth orbit is an orbit a few hundred kilometres up, in which a satellite circles Earth in about 90 minutes, so it moves across the sky and a constellation of many satellites is needed for continuous coverage. A transponder is the equipment on a satellite that receives a signal, amplifies it, and retransmits it on a different frequency. A ground station is a station on Earth that communicates with a satellite, with a large dish antenna. A constellation is a group of satellites working together to provide coverage. A satellite bus is the platform of a satellite, which provides power, propulsion, and control, while the payload is the equipment that does the job, such as a transponder for communications or a camera for observation. A launch vehicle is the rocket that carries a satellite into orbit, and the cost of launching has fallen sharply with reusable rockets, such as the Falcon 9, first landed in 2015. Space debris is the collection of dead satellites and fragments in orbit, which is a growing hazard, because a collision can create more debris, a cascade called the Kessler syndrome, proposed by Donald Kessler in 1978. A satellite operator is a company that runs satellites, such as Intelsat, SES, and Eutelsat, which sell capacity to broadcasters and to internet providers.

How it works: A satellite works by receiving a signal from a ground station, amplifying it, and retransmitting it to another ground station or to many receivers. A geostationary satellite is useful because it is always in the same place in the sky, so a dish can be pointed at it once and left there, which is why satellite television dishes are fixed. The signal takes about 0.25 seconds to go from the ground to the satellite and back, because the distance is about 72,000 kilometres each way at the speed of light, which is noticeable in a telephone call but not in television. A low Earth orbit satellite is much closer, so the delay is only a few milliseconds, but it moves quickly across the sky, so the ground station must track it or hand over to the next satellite in the constellation, which is why low Earth orbit internet needs hundreds or thousands of satellites. Satellites also have a military history. The first satellites were launched for reconnaissance, to photograph the Earth, and the Global Positioning System was developed by the United States military, though it is now used by civilians worldwide. The use of satellites for military purposes is governed by the Outer Space Treaty of 1967, which says that space is the province of all humanity and that weapons of mass destruction are banned in space, though the treaty does not ban all military uses. The growth of satellite constellations has raised new questions about the militarisation of space, and about the need for rules to prevent conflict in orbit, which is why space law is a growing field.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A geostationary satellite is 35,786 kilometres up, and a signal travelling to it and back covers about 71,572 kilometres, taking about 0.24 seconds at the speed of light. A low Earth orbit satellite at 550 kilometres up is about 65 times closer, and the round trip takes about 0.004 seconds. The first commercial satellite, Intelsat 1, could carry 240 telephone calls, while a modern high-throughput satellite can carry hundreds of gigabits per second, equivalent to millions of calls. GPS satellites orbit at about 20,200 kilometres, and a receiver needs signals from at least four satellites to calculate its position, by measuring the time each signal took to arrive. There are more than 10,000 active satellites in orbit in the 2020s, most of them in low Earth orbit. A second worked example uses the number of satellites. In 2010, there were about 1,000 active satellites in orbit, and in 2024 there were more than 10,000, a rise of 10 times in 14 years, driven mainly by the launch of large constellations for internet, such as Starlink, which alone has more than 6,000 satellites. The growth is expected to continue, with tens of thousands more planned, which raises concerns about collisions, about light pollution for astronomy, and about the need for rules to manage the crowded orbits, especially in low Earth orbit, where most new satellites are being placed.

Real applications: Satellites are used for television, for telephone and internet, for navigation, for weather, and for Earth observation. They let television reach remote villages, they let ships and aircraft stay in contact, they let farmers monitor their crops, and they let scientists study the climate. The Global Positioning System, and similar systems from Europe, Russia, and China, provide navigation to billions of people, and they are used by aircraft, by ships, by cars, and by smartphones. The study of satellites is therefore the study of how the sky has become part of the telecommunications network, and how space has become an essential part of life on Earth. Satellites are also used for disaster relief. When an earthquake or a flood destroys the ground network, satellites can provide communications for rescue teams, and Earth observation satellites can map the damage, so that aid can be directed where it is needed. The International Charter on Space and Major Disasters, founded in 2000, lets countries request satellite images in an emergency, and it has been used in hundreds of disasters, from earthquakes to oil spills. The study of satellites is therefore also the study of how space can help people on Earth in their hour of need.

Common questions and misconceptions: Do satellites fall down? A satellite in orbit is in continuous free fall around Earth, and it stays up because it moves sideways fast enough to keep missing the ground, though low satellites are slowed by the thin atmosphere and eventually fall back, unless they are boosted. Can a satellite be seen? Yes, many satellites are visible as moving points of light in the night sky, and the International Space Station is the brightest, visible to the naked eye as a bright, steady light crossing the sky. Are satellites the same as the Moon? No, the Moon is a natural satellite, while communications satellites are artificial, built and launched by people, and they are much smaller and much closer to Earth. Will there be too many satellites? The number is growing rapidly, and astronomers are concerned about light pollution and about collisions, which is why new rules for space traffic are being discussed. Can a satellite stay up forever? No, a satellite in low orbit is slowed by the thin atmosphere and will fall back to Earth within years or decades, unless it is boosted, while a satellite in geostationary orbit is so high that it will stay for centuries, though it will eventually be moved to a graveyard orbit, above the geostationary belt, when it is retired. How are satellites powered? By solar panels, which generate electricity from sunlight, and by batteries, which store it for when the satellite is in Earth's shadow, and the panels must be large, because the satellite needs power for its transponders, its computers, and its propulsion. Are satellites visible? Yes, many are visible as moving points of light, and the International Space Station is the brightest, though the new constellations of thousands of satellites are making the night sky brighter, which is a concern for astronomers.

Fibre Optics

ID: JAH-TELC-000009 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Fibre optics is the transmission of information as light through thin strands of glass or plastic, called optical fibres, which carry far more data, over longer distances, and with less loss than copper wires. Fibre is the backbone of the modern internet: it connects continents by undersea cables, it connects cities, and it increasingly connects homes, in a service called fibre to the premises. A single fibre can carry many separate signals at once, by using different colours of light, a technique called wavelength division multiplexing, and the capacity of a fibre is still growing as new techniques are developed. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Fibre optics began with the understanding of total internal reflection, known since the 19th century, and the first practical fibres were developed in the 1950s for medical endoscopes, which let doctors see inside the body. In 1966, Charles Kao, working in the United Kingdom, showed that glass fibre could carry light over long distances if the glass was pure enough, work that won him the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009. The first fibre-optic telephone links were installed in the late 1970s, and the first transatlantic fibre-optic cable, TAT-8, was laid in 1988, carrying 40,000 telephone calls at once, far more than the copper cables it replaced. In the 1990s and 2000s, fibre spread through the core of the internet, and in the 2010s and 2020s it began to reach homes, with fibre to the premises now available to a majority of homes in many countries. Fibre optics has also been used for sensing. A fibre can be used as a sensor, because the light travelling through it is affected by temperature, by pressure, and by strain, so a single fibre laid along a pipeline, a bridge, or a border can detect leaks, cracks, or intrusions, over tens of kilometres, with a resolution of metres. Distributed fibre sensing, which uses the backscattered light to measure along the whole fibre, is now used to monitor pipelines, to detect earthquakes, and to monitor the health of structures, and it is a growing application of the same technology that carries the internet.

Key concepts: An optical fibre is a thin strand of glass, about the thickness of a human hair, which guides light along its length by total internal reflection. Total internal reflection is the reflection of light inside a material when it hits the boundary at a shallow angle, so the light stays inside the fibre instead of escaping. A laser is a device that produces a narrow beam of light of a single colour, used to send signals through a fibre. Wavelength division multiplexing is the technique of sending many signals at once through one fibre, each on a different wavelength, or colour, of light. An amplifier is a device that boosts a signal, and in fibre systems an erbium-doped fibre amplifier boosts the light directly, without converting it to electricity. An erbium-doped fibre amplifier is a device that amplifies light in a fibre, by passing it through a fibre doped with the element erbium, which is pumped by a laser, and it is the reason that fibre signals can travel thousands of kilometres without being converted to electricity. A splice is a joint between two fibres, made by fusing them together, which must be precise, because any gap loses light. A connector is a device that joins two fibres, or a fibre to a device, and it must be clean, because dust on the end of a fibre can block the light. Single-mode fibre is a fibre with a narrow core, which carries one mode of light and is used for long distances, while multi-mode fibre has a wider core, carries several modes, and is used for short distances, such as inside a building.

How it works: Fibre works by total internal reflection. Light enters the fibre at one end and is reflected from the walls of the fibre, bouncing along its length, even when the fibre bends, as long as the bend is not too sharp. The light is produced by a laser, which is switched on and off billions of times a second to send digital data, and at the other end a photodetector turns the light back into an electrical signal. The light travels at about 200,000 kilometres per second in glass, slower than in a vacuum, because glass slows light, but it loses very little energy: a modern fibre loses about 0.2 decibels per kilometre, so a signal can travel about 100 kilometres before it needs amplification, and with amplifiers it can cross oceans. Many wavelengths can be sent at once, each carrying its own data, so a single fibre can carry terabits per second. Fibre optics also has a history of standards. The ITU and the IEEE have set standards for fibre, for the wavelengths used, and for the connectors, so that equipment from different makers can work together. The standards have evolved as the technology has improved, from the first fibres, which lost 20 decibels per kilometre, to modern fibres, which lose 0.2 decibels per kilometre, a hundredfold improvement, which is why fibre can now carry signals across oceans without repeaters every few kilometres, though repeaters are still needed for the longest links.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A fibre loses about 0.2 decibels per kilometre, so after 100 kilometres it has lost 20 decibels, which means the power is 100 times smaller, because 10 decibels is a factor of 10, and 20 decibels is a factor of 100. A single fibre can carry over 100 terabits per second in laboratory tests, and commercial systems carry tens of terabits per second, which is enough for millions of simultaneous video streams. The first transatlantic fibre cable, TAT-8, laid in 1988, could carry 40,000 telephone calls, while a modern transatlantic cable can carry over 100 terabits per second, which is millions of times more. A home fibre connection of 1 gigabit per second can download a 1-gigabyte file in about 8 seconds, because 1 gigabyte is 8 gigabits, divided by 1 gigabit per second is 8 seconds. A second worked example uses the capacity of a fibre. A single fibre, using wavelength division multiplexing with 100 wavelengths, each carrying 100 gigabits per second, can carry 10 terabits per second, which is 10,000 gigabits per second. A cable with 10 fibres can carry 100 terabits per second, and a cable with 100 fibres can carry 1 petabit per second, which is a million gigabits per second. The numbers show why fibre is the backbone of the internet: a single cable, no thicker than a garden hose, can carry the traffic of a whole country.

Real applications: Fibre optics is used for the backbone of the internet, for undersea cables, for the connections between cities, and increasingly for the connection to homes and businesses. It is also used in medicine, in endoscopes, and in sensors, where a fibre can measure temperature, pressure, or strain along its length. The study of fibre optics is therefore the study of how light has become the main carrier of information, and how a strand of glass thinner than a hair can carry the communications of a whole country. Fibre is also used in medicine, in endoscopes that let doctors see inside the body, and in lasers, which are used in surgery and in manufacturing. The same physics, guiding light through glass, is used in all of these applications, and the study of fibre optics is therefore a study of a technology with many uses, from communications to medicine to sensing, all based on the same principle of total internal reflection.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is fibre faster than copper? Yes, fibre carries far more data and over longer distances, because light in glass loses less energy than electricity in copper, and because fibre can use many wavelengths at once. Can fibre be cut? Yes, and when a cable is cut, by an anchor or by an earthquake, traffic is rerouted through other cables, which is why the network is built with redundancy. Is fibre the same as broadband? Broadband is a service, and fibre is one way of delivering it, alongside copper, cable, and mobile, though fibre is the fastest and is becoming the standard. Why is fibre made of glass? Because glass is transparent to the infrared light used in fibre, and it can be made very pure, so that light travels through it with little loss, and it is cheap and abundant, being made from sand. Is fibre fragile? A single fibre is thin and can break if bent too sharply, but a cable contains many fibres, protected by layers of plastic and steel, so it is strong, and it can be laid on the ocean floor and under streets without breaking. Can fibre carry power? No, fibre carries light, not electricity, so it does not carry power, though a cable may include a copper conductor to power repeaters, and the equipment at each end needs power. Is fibre the future? Fibre is the fastest and highest-capacity medium available, and it is being rolled out to homes and businesses around the world, though mobile and satellite will continue to serve places where fibre is not economic, such as remote areas and moving vehicles.

Wi-Fi and Wireless Networks

ID: JAH-TELC-000010 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Wi-Fi is a wireless networking technology that lets devices connect to a local network and to the internet by radio, without a cable. It is used in homes, in offices, in cafes, and in public places, and it is the main way laptops, tablets, and smartphones connect to the internet indoors. Wi-Fi works in the unlicensed radio bands, mainly 2.4 gigahertz and 5 gigahertz, and it follows standards set by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, called IEEE 802.11, which have been improved over the years to give higher speeds and better capacity. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Wi-Fi began in the 1990s, when the IEEE published the first 802.11 standard in 1997, with a speed of 2 megabits per second, and the Wi-Fi Alliance was formed in 1999 to certify products that worked together. The 802.11b standard, published in 1999, brought speeds of 11 megabits per second and made Wi-Fi popular in homes and offices. The 802.11g standard in 2003 raised the speed to 54 megabits per second, 802.11n in 2009 raised it to hundreds of megabits per second, and 802.11ac in 2013 and 802.11ax, known as Wi-Fi 6, in 2020 raised it to gigabits per second. The name Wi-Fi was chosen as a play on hi-fi, and it is a trademark of the Wi-Fi Alliance, though it is now used generically for the technology. Today, Wi-Fi is in billions of devices, and it carries a large share of internet traffic, especially indoors, where mobile signals are weaker. Wi-Fi has also been used for location. A phone can estimate its position indoors, where GPS does not work, by measuring the signal strength from nearby Wi-Fi access points, whose positions are known, a technique called Wi-Fi positioning, which is used in shopping centres, in airports, and in hospitals to guide people and to track equipment. The technique is also used by apps to provide location-based services, such as finding the nearest shop or restaurant, and it is a reminder that a network designed for communication can also be used for sensing, because the signal carries information about the environment as well as about the data.

Key concepts: A wireless access point is a device that connects wireless devices to a wired network, usually by radio. A router is a device that forwards data between networks, and a home router usually includes a Wi-Fi access point. A frequency band is a range of radio frequencies, and Wi-Fi uses the 2.4 gigahertz band, which has a longer range but more interference, and the 5 gigahertz band, which is faster but has a shorter range, and the newer 6 gigahertz band, used by Wi-Fi 6E. A channel is a subdivision of a band, and Wi-Fi devices choose a channel to avoid interference from neighbours. The SSID, or service set identifier, is the name of a Wi-Fi network, which is broadcast so that devices can find it. A mesh network is a network in which several access points work together, so that a device can move between them without losing its connection, and the access points forward traffic for each other, extending the coverage. A hotspot is a place where Wi-Fi is available, often in a cafe, a hotel, or a station, and it may be free or paid for. A captive portal is a web page that a user must accept before using a hotspot, often to agree to terms or to watch an advertisement. WPA3 is the latest standard for Wi-Fi security, which encrypts the connection and protects against password guessing, replacing WPA2, which was introduced in 2004.

How it works: Wi-Fi works by radio. A device that wants to connect listens for the access point's SSID, and it associates with the access point, exchanging credentials if the network is secured. Data are sent as packets by radio, using a technique called carrier sense multiple access, in which a device listens before it transmits, to avoid colliding with another device that is already transmitting, and if a collision happens, it waits a random time and tries again. The data rate depends on the standard, on the signal strength, and on how many devices are sharing the channel: a Wi-Fi 6 access point can in theory deliver over 1 gigabit per second, but a single device in a busy network will get less. The access point is connected by a cable to the internet, so Wi-Fi is the last link in the chain, from the wired network to the wireless device. Wi-Fi also has a history of interference. The 2.4 gigahertz band is shared with Bluetooth, with microwave ovens, and with many other devices, so it is crowded, and a Wi-Fi network in a flat may compete with dozens of neighbours' networks, which is why the 5 gigahertz band, which is less crowded, is often faster, even though it has a shorter range. The management of interference is a central problem in Wi-Fi, and it is solved by choosing a channel, by using a wider channel when it is free, and by the access point and the device negotiating the best rate for the conditions, which is why Wi-Fi speeds vary so much in practice.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. The first Wi-Fi standard in 1997 ran at 2 megabits per second, while Wi-Fi 6, published in 2020, can in theory run at over 9 gigabits per second, a rise of more than 4,000 times. A 2.4 gigahertz signal has a wavelength of about 12.5 centimetres, and a 5 gigahertz signal has a wavelength of about 6 centimetres, which is why 5 gigahertz signals are blocked more easily by walls. A home Wi-Fi network typically covers a house, with a range of tens of metres, and a public hotspot covers a cafe or a station. A file of 1 gigabyte, which is 8,000 megabits, takes about 80 seconds to download at 100 megabits per second, and about 8 seconds at 1 gigabit per second, which shows why faster Wi-Fi matters for video and for large files. A second worked example uses the number of Wi-Fi devices. There are more than 4 billion Wi-Fi devices in use in the world, and the number is growing by hundreds of millions a year, driven by phones, by laptops, and by the Internet of Things, which uses Wi-Fi to connect sensors and appliances. A home may have 20 or more Wi-Fi devices, from phones and laptops to televisions, speakers, and thermostats, and the access point must serve them all, sharing its capacity, which is why a modern access point can handle dozens of devices at once, though each one gets a share of the total, not the full speed.

Real applications: Wi-Fi is used in homes, in offices, in schools, and in public places, and it is the main way most people connect to the internet indoors. It is used to connect laptops, phones, printers, televisions, and the devices of the Internet of Things, such as smart speakers and thermostats. It is also used in industry, in warehouses, and in hospitals, where it connects equipment and staff. The study of Wi-Fi is therefore the study of how the last few metres of the internet are delivered, and how a shared radio channel can serve many devices at once. Wi-Fi is also used in public services. Schools use Wi-Fi to connect classrooms, hospitals use it to connect medical devices, and cities use it to provide public internet in parks and squares. The provision of public Wi-Fi is a matter of policy, because it can reduce the digital divide, by giving internet access to people who cannot afford it at home, and it is funded by governments, by businesses, and by charities, which see it as a public good, like a library or a park.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is Wi-Fi the same as the internet? No, Wi-Fi is a way of connecting to a local network, which is then connected to the internet by a cable, so Wi-Fi without an internet connection lets devices talk to each other but not to the wider internet. Is Wi-Fi safe? Wi-Fi networks can be secured with encryption, such as WPA3, which protects data from eavesdroppers, but an open network, without a password, can be overheard, so users should avoid sending sensitive information on open networks. Does Wi-Fi work through walls? Yes, but walls weaken the signal, especially at 5 gigahertz, which is why a router in one room may give a weak signal in another, and why mesh networks, with several access points, are used in larger homes. Why is Wi-Fi slow sometimes? Because many devices share the same channel, because neighbours' networks interfere, or because the internet connection itself is slow, and the cause can be diagnosed by testing the Wi-Fi and the internet separately. Is Wi-Fi the same as Bluetooth? No, Wi-Fi is a local area network technology, which connects devices to a network and to the internet over tens of metres, while Bluetooth is a personal area network technology, which connects devices to each other over a few metres, such as a phone to headphones, and it uses less power. Can Wi-Fi be used outdoors? Yes, outdoor access points can cover a park, a campus, or a street, though the range is limited by power and by obstacles, and the weather can affect the signal, though not as much as for higher frequencies. Why is my Wi-Fi slow in the evening? Because many people are using it at once, in your home and in your neighbours' homes, and the channel is shared, so the speed falls when the network is busy, which is why a wired connection is faster and more reliable for a single device.

Data Transmission and Bandwidth

ID: JAH-TELC-000011 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Bandwidth is the capacity of a communications channel, measured as the rate at which it can carry data, usually in bits per second. A channel with a bandwidth of 100 megabits per second can carry 100 million bits every second, and a file of a given size takes a time equal to its size divided by the bandwidth. Bandwidth is the most important measure of a connection, because it decides how fast a file downloads, how many video streams can be watched at once, and how quickly a web page loads. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The concept of bandwidth comes from the work of Harry Nyquist in the 1920s, who showed the limits of a channel, and Claude Shannon, who in 1948 published A Mathematical Theory of Communication, which founded information theory and gave the formula for the maximum data rate of a channel, now called the Shannon limit. The first telegraph lines carried a few bits per second, the first telephone lines carried 64 kilobits per second, and the first modems, which let computers use telephone lines, carried 300 bits per second in the 1960s. Broadband, which brought megabits per second to homes, began in the late 1990s with ADSL and cable, and fibre broadband, which brings gigabits per second, began in the 2010s. The growth of bandwidth has been one of the fastest in history, doubling roughly every few years, and it is the reason the internet can carry video, which was impossible on the first networks. Data transmission has also been improved by compression. Compression is the process of removing redundancy from data, so that it takes less space and less bandwidth to send, and it is essential for video, which would otherwise need far more bandwidth than is available. Lossless compression, used for text and for data, removes redundancy without losing any information, so the original can be reconstructed exactly, while lossy compression, used for audio and video, removes information that the human eye or ear is less sensitive to, so the file is smaller but not identical to the original. The choice between the two depends on the application: a bank record must be lossless, while a video can be lossy, as long as the loss is not noticeable.

Key concepts: Bandwidth is the data rate a channel can carry, in bits per second. Latency is the delay, the time a signal takes to travel from sender to receiver, which is separate from bandwidth: a channel can have high bandwidth and high latency, like a satellite link, or low bandwidth and low latency, like an old telephone line. Throughput is the actual data rate achieved, which is usually less than the bandwidth, because of overhead, errors, and sharing. A bit per second, or bps, is one binary digit per second, and the units are kilobits, megabits, and gigabits per second, where a megabit is a million bits. The Shannon limit is the maximum data rate of a channel, set by its bandwidth in hertz and by its signal-to-noise ratio. A modem is a device that converts digital data into a form that can be sent over an analogue channel, such as a telephone line, and back again, and the word is short for modulator-demodulator. ADSL, or asymmetric digital subscriber line, is a technology that sends broadband over a copper telephone line, with a higher speed downstream, to the user, than upstream, from the user, because most users download more than they upload. A leased line is a dedicated connection between two points, rented from a provider, which gives a guaranteed bandwidth and is used by businesses. A contention ratio is the ratio of the number of users sharing a connection to the capacity, so a contention ratio of 50 to 1 means 50 users share a connection, and each gets a share of the total, not the full speed.

How it works: Data transmission works by sending bits through a channel, and the rate is limited by the channel's bandwidth and by its noise. Shannon's formula says the maximum data rate in bits per second is the bandwidth in hertz times the logarithm base 2 of one plus the signal-to-noise ratio. A channel with a bandwidth of 1 megahertz and a signal-to-noise ratio of 1,000, which is 30 decibels, can carry about 1 million times log2(1001), which is about 10 million bits per second. In practice, the rate is lower, because real systems do not reach the limit, and because the channel is shared. The data are sent as packets, and the time to send a file is its size in bits divided by the data rate, plus the latency, which is the time for the first bit to arrive. For a large file, the transmission time dominates, while for a short message, the latency dominates, which is why a satellite link feels slow for a video call, even though its bandwidth is high. Data transmission also depends on the quality of the channel. Noise, which is unwanted variation in the signal, limits the data rate, and the signal-to-noise ratio, measured in decibels, is the key measure: a higher ratio means a cleaner signal and a higher data rate. Interference from other signals, from electrical equipment, and from the atmosphere also limits the rate, and the techniques of modern communications, such as error correction, such as adaptive modulation, and such as multiple antennas, are all designed to get more data through a noisy channel, closer to the Shannon limit, which is the theoretical maximum.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A file of 1 gigabyte is 8,000 megabits, so at 100 megabits per second it takes 80 seconds to download, at 10 megabits per second it takes 800 seconds, or about 13 minutes, and at 1 gigabit per second it takes 8 seconds. A high-definition video stream needs about 5 megabits per second, so a 100-megabit connection can in theory carry 20 such streams at once, though in practice fewer, because of overhead. The Shannon limit for a telephone channel, with a bandwidth of 3,400 hertz and a signal-to-noise ratio of about 1,000, is about 34,000 bits per second, which is why the first modems, which ran at 300 bits per second, were far below the limit, and modern modems, which use better techniques, get much closer to it. A second worked example uses the Shannon limit for a Wi-Fi channel. A Wi-Fi channel has a bandwidth of 20 megahertz and a signal-to-noise ratio of 100, which is 20 decibels, so its Shannon limit is 20 million times log2(101), which is about 133 million bits per second. A real Wi-Fi system, using 802.11n, can achieve about 100 megabits per second on such a channel, which is close to the limit, showing how efficient modern systems are. A channel with a higher signal-to-noise ratio, for example 1,000, or 30 decibels, has a limit of about 200 megabits per second, which is why a strong signal gives a faster connection.

Real applications: Bandwidth is used to describe and to sell internet connections, and it is the main factor in the quality of a connection for video, for gaming, and for large downloads. It is also used in network design, where the bandwidth of each link must be enough for the traffic it carries, and in regulation, where governments set targets for broadband speeds, such as the United Kingdom's target of gigabit-capable broadband for most homes. The study of bandwidth is therefore the study of how much information a channel can carry, and how to use that capacity well. Data transmission is also the basis of the digital economy. The ability to send data quickly and cheaply has let firms outsource work, let people work from home, and let services be delivered online, from banking to education to health. The growth of data transmission has been one of the fastest in history, and it is expected to continue, driven by video, by the Internet of Things, and by new applications, such as virtual reality, which will need far more bandwidth than today's applications, and which will drive the next generation of networks.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is bandwidth the same as speed? Bandwidth is the capacity, and the speed a user experiences depends on bandwidth, on latency, and on the server, so a high-bandwidth connection can still feel slow if the latency is high or the server is slow. Does more bandwidth always help? For a large download yes, but for a short message the latency matters more, and for a video call the latency and the stability matter as much as the bandwidth. What is the difference between megabits and megabytes? A megabit is a million bits, and a megabyte is 8 million bits, so a connection of 100 megabits per second downloads at 12.5 megabytes per second, and the confusion between the two is a common source of misunderstanding. Can bandwidth be increased without limit? No, the Shannon limit sets a maximum for a given bandwidth and noise, so to increase the data rate a channel must use more bandwidth, or reduce its noise, or use a better technique that gets closer to the limit. Is the Shannon limit a law of nature? It is a mathematical result, based on information theory, and it sets the maximum data rate for a given bandwidth and noise, though it does not say how to achieve it, and real systems approach it but do not reach it. Can data be sent faster than the speed of light? No, no signal can travel faster than light, and the latency of a link is set by the distance divided by the speed of light in the medium, which is why a satellite link has a high latency, even though its bandwidth is high. Does compression lose data? Lossless compression does not, while lossy compression does, but the loss is chosen to be imperceptible, so a compressed video looks the same as the original, even though some data have been discarded.

Switching and Routing

ID: JAH-TELC-000012 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Switching and routing are the processes that connect a call or a data packet to its destination through a network. Switching is the act of connecting one link to another, so that a signal can travel from its source to its destination, and routing is the act of choosing the best path for it to take. In the telephone network, switching was done by operators and then by automatic exchanges; in the internet, routing is done by routers, which forward packets from one network to another, using tables that list the best next step for each destination. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Switching began with the first telephone exchanges in 1878, where operators connected callers by plugging cords into a switchboard. Automatic switching was invented by Almon Strowger in 1891, who built a system that let callers dial a number and be connected without an operator. Circuit switching, in which a dedicated path is set up for the whole call, was the basis of the telephone network for a century. Packet switching, in which data are broken into packets that are sent independently, was proposed by Paul Baran in the early 1960s and by Donald Davies in 1965, and it became the basis of the ARPANET in 1969 and of the internet. Routing protocols, which let routers share information about the network, were developed in the 1970s and 1980s, and the Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP, which routes traffic between networks, was standardised in 1989 and is still used today. Switching and routing have also been used to build the telephone network's replacement. The old telephone network used circuit switching, in which a dedicated path was held for the whole call, while the new network, based on the internet, uses packet switching, in which voice is sent as packets, a technology called Voice over IP, or VoIP. The change has let telephone calls be carried on the same network as data, which is more efficient, and it has let new services, such as video calls and messaging, be added, because they are all just data. The retirement of the old copper network, under way in the United Kingdom in the 2020s, is the end of circuit switching for telephony, after more than a century.

Key concepts: Circuit switching is a method in which a dedicated path is set up for the duration of a communication, as in a traditional telephone call. Packet switching is a method in which data are broken into packets, each sent independently, as in the internet. A router is a device that forwards packets between networks, choosing the next step from its routing table. A switch is a device that forwards data within a network, usually by the destination address. A routing table is a list of destinations and the best next step for each. The Border Gateway Protocol, or BGP, is the protocol that routers use to exchange information about the paths between networks. A virtual circuit is a path through a packet-switched network that behaves like a circuit, with a guaranteed bandwidth and delay, used for services that need quality, such as voice and video. A label-switched path is a path set up by Multiprotocol Label Switching, or MPLS, a technology that lets a network operator set up virtual circuits through its network, to guarantee quality for its customers. A software-defined network, or SDN, is a network in which the control, which decides where packets go, is separated from the forwarding, which sends them, so that the network can be managed by software, from a central controller, which is more flexible than the traditional distributed routing. A network function is a function, such as a firewall or a router, which in a modern network may be run as software on a general-purpose server, rather than as a dedicated box, a practice called network function virtualisation.

How it works: Switching works by making a connection. In a circuit-switched network, the caller dials a number, the exchange finds a free path to the destination, and the path is held for the whole call, even during silences, which wastes capacity but guarantees quality. In a packet-switched network, each packet carries its destination address, and each router reads the address, looks up the best next step in its routing table, and forwards the packet, so packets from the same message can take different paths and arrive out of order, to be reassembled at the destination. Routing tables are built by routing protocols, which let routers tell each other about the links they know, so that the whole network learns the best paths, and if a link fails, the routers learn a new path, which is why the internet can survive failures. Routing also has a history of failures. The Border Gateway Protocol, which routes traffic between networks, was designed when the internet was small and trusted, and it has no built-in security, so a network can accidentally or deliberately claim addresses that are not its own, and traffic can be hijacked, as happened in 2008 when YouTube was taken offline, and in 2018 when traffic for Google was routed through China. The security of BGP is now a major concern, and new techniques, such as Resource Public Key Infrastructure, or RPKI, which lets a network prove that it owns an address, are being deployed to prevent hijacking, though adoption is slow, because it requires cooperation from all networks.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A traditional telephone call uses a circuit of 64 kilobits per second for the whole call, even when no one is speaking, so a trunk line that can carry 1,000 calls needs a capacity of 64 megabits per second. A packet-switched network carrying the same 1,000 calls, with each call active only half the time, needs only about 32 megabits per second on average, because the silences can be used by other calls, which is why packet switching is more efficient. The internet's routers forward billions of packets a day, and the largest routers can forward trillions of bits per second. The Border Gateway Protocol manages the routes between more than 70,000 networks, called autonomous systems, which together make up the internet. A second worked example uses the number of routes. The internet's routing table, which lists the best path to each network, has more than 900,000 entries, and it grows by tens of thousands a year, as new networks are added. Each router must store the table and update it when the network changes, which is a major task, and the growth of the table is a concern for the future of the internet, because it requires more memory and more processing, and because a larger table is slower to update. The growth is managed by aggregating routes, so that many small networks are represented by a single entry, a technique called CIDR, or Classless Inter-Domain Routing, introduced in the 1990s.

Real applications: Switching and routing are used in every network, from the telephone network to the internet, and they are the reason a call or a packet can reach any destination in the world. They are also used in mobile networks, where the network must track a moving phone and hand it over between cells, and in data centres, where switches connect thousands of servers. The study of switching and routing is therefore the study of how a network finds its way, and how it recovers when a link fails, which is the key to the resilience of modern communications. Switching and routing are also used in data centres, where thousands of servers are connected by switches, and the traffic between them, called east-west traffic, is now larger than the traffic to the outside world, called north-south traffic, because applications are distributed across many servers. The design of data centre networks, with their high bandwidth and low latency, is a major field, and it is driven by the needs of cloud computing, which requires servers to communicate quickly with each other, to serve a user request that may involve dozens of servers working together.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is circuit switching obsolete? It is still used for some purposes, but packet switching has taken over for most traffic, because it is more efficient and more flexible, and even telephone calls now travel as packets over the internet. Do packets always take the same route? No, each packet is routed independently, so packets from the same message can take different paths, and they are reassembled at the destination, which is why the internet can survive a failure on one path. Can routing go wrong? Yes, a misconfiguration in BGP can send traffic to the wrong place, as happened in 2008 when a Pakistani network accidentally claimed YouTube's addresses and took the site offline for many users, which is why routing security is a growing concern. Why does a call sometimes drop? Because the handover between cells failed, or because the network lost the connection, and the call must be set up again, which is a failure of switching, not of the phone itself. Is routing the same as switching? Routing chooses the path between networks, while switching forwards data within a network, though the terms are often used interchangeably, and a modern device may do both. Can a packet be lost? Yes, a packet can be lost if a router is congested or if a link fails, and TCP, the transport protocol, detects the loss and asks for the packet to be sent again, so the data arrive complete, though with a delay, which is why a lost packet causes a brief pause in a video call. Why does the internet sometimes slow down? Because a link is congested, because a router is busy, or because a route has changed, and the traffic must take a longer path, which is why the internet's performance varies, even though its design is resilient.

Protocols and Standards

ID: JAH-TELC-000013 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A protocol is a set of rules that devices follow to communicate, and a standard is a protocol, or a set of protocols, that has been agreed by an organisation and is used by many makers, so that equipment from different companies can work together. Protocols define how data are formatted, how errors are handled, and how devices take turns to speak, and standards ensure that a phone from one maker can call a phone from another, and that a computer from one maker can connect to the internet. Without protocols and standards, telecommunications would be a collection of incompatible systems, and the global network would not exist. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Protocols began with the telegraph, where Morse code was a protocol for sending letters as dots and dashes, and with the telephone, where signalling protocols let exchanges set up calls. The most important protocols are TCP/IP, developed in the 1970s by Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn, which became the standard for the internet in 1983, and the GSM standard for mobile phones, developed in Europe in the 1980s and adopted worldwide in the 1990s. Standards are set by organisations such as the International Telecommunication Union, or ITU, founded in 1865 as the International Telegraph Union, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers, or IEEE, and the Internet Engineering Task Force, or IETF, which publishes the standards for the internet as Requests for Comments, or RFCs. The World Wide Web Consortium, or W3C, sets standards for the Web, and the 3rd Generation Partnership Project, or 3GPP, sets standards for mobile networks, including 4G and 5G. Protocols have also been the subject of competition. The history of the internet includes a battle between TCP/IP, which won, and the OSI model, a seven-layer model developed by the International Organization for Standardization in the 1970s and 1980s, which was more formal but more complex, and which was never widely adopted for the internet, though its layer names are still used to describe networks. The lesson is that a simple protocol that works is more likely to succeed than a perfect protocol that is hard to implement, and that adoption, not elegance, decides which standard wins. The same lesson applies to other standards, from mobile phones to Wi-Fi, where the winner is often the one that is adopted first and widely, not the one that is technically best.

Key concepts: TCP, or Transmission Control Protocol, is a protocol that ensures data arrive complete and in order, by numbering packets and asking for missing ones to be resent. IP, or Internet Protocol, is a protocol that addresses packets and routes them between networks. HTTP, or Hypertext Transfer Protocol, is the protocol used by the Web to request and send pages. DNS, or Domain Name System, is the protocol that translates domain names into IP addresses. A standard is a published specification that equipment must follow to be compatible, and a standards body is an organisation that writes and maintains standards, such as the ITU, the IEEE, and the IETF. Interoperability is the ability of equipment from different makers to work together, which is the purpose of standards. An RFC, or Request for Comments, is a document published by the IETF, which may be a standard, a proposal, or an informational note, and the first RFC was published in 1969. A draft standard is a protocol that is being tested, before it becomes a full standard. A de facto standard is a protocol that is widely used, even though it has not been formally standardised, such as the original HTTP, which was used before it was standardised. A proprietary protocol is a protocol owned by a company, which may not work with other companies' products, and it is the opposite of an open standard, which anyone can use. Interoperability testing is the process of checking that products from different makers work together, which is done by the Wi-Fi Alliance and by other bodies.

How it works: Protocols work in layers. The internet uses a layered model, in which each layer provides a service to the layer above it: the physical layer carries bits over a wire or by radio, the network layer, IP, routes packets, the transport layer, TCP, ensures they arrive correctly, and the application layer, HTTP or email, provides the service the user sees. A device that sends data passes it down through the layers, each adding its own header, and the receiving device passes it up through the layers, each removing its header, so that the application at the top receives the original data. Standards define each layer, so that a device from one maker can talk to a device from another, as long as both follow the same standards, and new standards can be added without changing the whole system, which is why the internet has been able to grow and change for decades. Protocols also have to evolve. The Internet Protocol version 4, or IPv4, which was standardised in 1981, uses 32-bit addresses, which give about 4.3 billion addresses, and it ran out of addresses in the 2010s, because the internet grew faster than expected. The replacement, IPv6, uses 128-bit addresses, which give about 340 trillion trillion trillion addresses, a number so large that it will never run out, and it has been deployed gradually since the 1990s, though IPv4 is still widely used, and the two must work together, which is why the transition is slow. The story of IPv4 and IPv6 is a reminder that a protocol designed for a small network may not scale to a global one, and that planning for growth is essential.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. The ITU was founded in 1865, making it one of the oldest international organisations, and it now has 193 member states. The IETF has published more than 9,000 RFCs, which are the standards and proposals for the internet, and the first RFC was published in 1969. The GSM standard, published in the early 1990s, was adopted in more than 200 countries, and it let a phone bought in one country work in another, which is why roaming became possible. The 3GPP, which sets the standards for 4G and 5G, has hundreds of member companies, and a 5G standard, Release 15, was published in 2018, with later releases adding new features. The number of standards is large, but they are all needed, because each one defines a small part of a very large system. A second worked example uses IPv4 addresses. IPv4 uses 32 bits, so the number of addresses is 2 to the power of 32, which is 4,294,967,296, or about 4.3 billion. The world's population is about 8 billion, so there are not enough IPv4 addresses for every person to have one, let alone for every device, which is why network address translation, or NAT, is used, to share one public address among many private devices, and why IPv6, with 128 bits, was developed. IPv6 has 2 to the power of 128 addresses, which is about 340 trillion trillion trillion, a number that is hard to comprehend, but which ensures that every device can have its own address, forever.

Real applications: Protocols and standards are used everywhere in telecommunications. They let a phone call a phone, a computer connect to the internet, and a television receive a broadcast, regardless of the maker. They are also used in regulation, where governments require equipment to meet standards for safety and for spectrum use, and in trade, where standards let products be sold in many countries. The study of protocols and standards is therefore the study of how cooperation is built into technology, and how a global network can be made from the products of competing companies, by agreement on the rules. Protocols are also used in security. The TLS protocol, which secures the Web, has been revised several times, with TLS 1.3, published in 2018, being faster and more secure than its predecessors, because it removes old, weak features and it encrypts more of the handshake, so that an eavesdropper learns less. The evolution of TLS shows how a protocol can be improved, by learning from attacks and by removing what is no longer safe, and it is a model for how standards should be maintained, not only written once and left alone.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who sets the standards? Standards are set by international bodies, such as the ITU, the IEEE, and the IETF, in which companies, governments, and experts work together, and the process is open, so that anyone can contribute, though in practice large companies have the most influence. Are standards the same as laws? No, standards are voluntary, but governments often require equipment to meet them, and the market usually adopts them, because a product that does not follow the standard will not work with other products. Can a standard be changed? Yes, standards are revised, and new versions are published, though old versions may remain in use for years, which is why a modern phone can still connect to an old network. Why are there so many standards? Because telecommunications is a large and complex system, with many layers and many functions, and each one needs its own rules, so the total number is in the thousands. Is TCP/IP the only protocol? No, there are many protocols, for email, for the Web, for video, and for the Internet of Things, but TCP/IP is the foundation, because it is the protocol that lets networks talk to each other, and all other protocols run on top of it. Can a protocol be patented? A protocol itself is usually published as a standard, which anyone can use, though the implementation may be patented, and the debate about patents in standards, called standard-essential patents, is a major issue in telecommunications, because a company that owns a patent essential to a standard can demand high royalties, which is why standards bodies require patents to be licensed on fair terms. Why are there so many RFCs? Because the internet is large and complex, and each RFC defines a small part of it, from the format of an email address to the routing of packets, and the total number, over 9,000, reflects the size of the system.

The Electromagnetic Spectrum and Frequencies

ID: JAH-TELC-000014 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: The electromagnetic spectrum is the range of all electromagnetic radiation, from radio waves at the lowest frequencies to gamma rays at the highest, and telecommunications uses the radio part of the spectrum, from about 3 kilohertz to 300 gigahertz. Different services use different frequencies: long-wave radio uses low frequencies that travel far, FM radio uses higher frequencies that carry better sound, mobile phones use still higher frequencies that carry more data, and satellite links use the highest radio frequencies. The spectrum is a finite resource, and it is managed by allocating bands to different uses, so that services do not interfere with each other. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The spectrum was first explored by James Clerk Maxwell, who predicted electromagnetic waves in the 1860s, and by Heinrich Hertz, who produced them in 1887. The first radio transmissions used long waves, because they travelled far, and the first transatlantic signal in 1901 used a frequency of about 500 kilohertz. In the 1920s, broadcasting began on medium wave, and in the 1930s FM began on very high frequencies, around 100 megahertz. After the Second World War, television used higher frequencies, and in the 1970s and 1980s mobile phones began on frequencies around 900 megahertz. The International Telecommunication Union, founded in 1865, has managed the spectrum since the early 20th century, holding World Radiocommunication Conferences to allocate frequencies, and the first such conference was held in 1906. In the 21st century, the demand for spectrum has grown with mobile data, and new bands, including millimetre-wave frequencies above 24 gigahertz, have been opened for 5G. The spectrum is also used for science. Radio astronomy needs quiet bands, where no transmissions are allowed, so that telescopes can hear faint signals from space, and the ITU protects those bands, though they are under pressure from new services, such as satellite constellations, which can interfere with them. The spectrum is also used for Earth observation, for weather forecasting, and for navigation, and the frequencies used for those purposes are protected, because interference could cause a wrong forecast or a wrong position, with serious consequences. The management of the spectrum is therefore not only about commerce but also about science and safety, and the balance between them is a matter for international agreement.

Key concepts: Frequency is the number of waves per second, measured in hertz, and wavelength is the distance between two peaks, which is the speed of light divided by the frequency. A band is a range of frequencies assigned to a service, such as the FM band from 88 to 108 megahertz. A channel is a subdivision of a band, assigned to a single station or service. Interference is the disruption of a signal by another signal on the same or a nearby frequency, which is why frequencies are licensed and managed. The radio spectrum is the part of the electromagnetic spectrum used for radio, from about 3 kilohertz to 300 gigahertz, and it is divided into bands with names such as low frequency, medium frequency, high frequency, very high frequency, and ultra high frequency. A megahertz is a million hertz, a gigahertz is a billion hertz, and a terahertz is a trillion hertz, and the units are used to describe frequencies, from radio to light. A decibel is a logarithmic unit, used to describe ratios, such as the gain of an antenna or the loss in a cable, and 10 decibels is a factor of 10, 20 decibels is a factor of 100, and 3 decibels is a factor of about 2. A hertz is one cycle per second, named after Heinrich Hertz, who first produced radio waves. The electromagnetic spectrum is divided into regions with names, such as radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma ray, in order of increasing frequency, and telecommunications uses the radio and microwave regions.

How it works: The spectrum is managed by allocation and by licensing. The ITU allocates bands to services on a global basis, for example the band from 88 to 108 megahertz is allocated to FM broadcasting worldwide, and national regulators, such as Ofcom in the United Kingdom and the Federal Communications Commission in the United States, license transmitters within those bands, so that two stations in the same area do not use the same frequency. The choice of frequency is a trade-off: low frequencies travel far and penetrate buildings, but they carry little data, while high frequencies carry more data but travel in straight lines and are blocked by obstacles. A mobile network therefore uses low frequencies for coverage, to reach rural areas, and high frequencies for capacity, to serve many users in a city, and it reuses frequencies in cells that are far apart, so the same frequency can be used many times. The spectrum is also a source of revenue for governments, which auction licences to use it. The first spectrum auctions, in the United States in the 1990s, were designed by economists, including Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson, who won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2020 for their work on auction theory, and the auctions raised billions of dollars, while also assigning the spectrum to the firms that valued it most. The design of an auction matters: a poorly designed auction can let a firm buy spectrum cheaply, or can leave spectrum unsold, while a well-designed auction raises revenue and assigns the spectrum efficiently, which is why auction theory is now a central part of spectrum management.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A frequency of 100 megahertz has a wavelength of 3 metres, because the speed of light, 300 million metres per second, divided by 100 million hertz is 3 metres. A frequency of 900 megahertz has a wavelength of about 33 centimetres, and a frequency of 28 gigahertz, used for millimetre-wave 5G, has a wavelength of about 1 centimetre. The FM band, from 88 to 108 megahertz, is 20 megahertz wide, and it is divided into channels of 200 kilohertz, so it holds 100 channels. The whole radio spectrum, from 3 kilohertz to 300 gigahertz, spans a factor of 100 million in frequency, but only a small part is suitable for mobile communications, which is why spectrum is valuable and is often auctioned for billions of pounds. A second worked example uses the value of spectrum. The United Kingdom's 3G auction in 2000 raised 22.5 billion pounds for five licences, an average of 4.5 billion pounds per licence, which was far more than expected, and it showed how valuable spectrum is. The United States' C-band auction in 2021 raised more than 80 billion dollars, a record, for spectrum to be used for 5G, which shows that the value has grown as mobile data has grown. The revenue goes to the government, which can use it to fund public services, though the operators pass the cost on to consumers in higher prices, which is a trade-off that regulators consider when they set the reserve price and the conditions of the auction.

Real applications: The spectrum is used for broadcasting, for mobile phones, for Wi-Fi, for satellite links, for radar, and for navigation, and its management is one of the most important tasks in telecommunications. Governments auction spectrum to mobile operators, raising billions, and they reserve spectrum for emergency services, for the military, and for scientific uses, such as radio astronomy, which needs quiet bands to hear faint signals from space. The study of the spectrum is therefore the study of a shared and finite resource, and of how it is divided fairly between competing uses, which is a matter of economics, of engineering, and of international politics. The spectrum is also shared in new ways. Cognitive radio is a technology that lets a device sense which frequencies are free and use them, without interfering with the licensed user, and dynamic spectrum sharing lets a mobile network share a band between 4G and 5G, depending on demand. These techniques make better use of the spectrum, by letting it be used when it would otherwise be idle, and they are the future of spectrum management, as the demand for spectrum continues to grow and the supply of new bands becomes scarcer.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the spectrum running out? The demand for spectrum grows with mobile data, but new techniques, such as using higher frequencies, sharing spectrum, and reusing frequencies in small cells, have kept pace, so the spectrum is not running out, though it is becoming more crowded and more valuable. Can anyone use any frequency? No, most frequencies are licensed, and using one without a licence is illegal, though some bands, such as the Wi-Fi bands, are unlicensed, meaning anyone can use them if they follow the rules on power and on sharing. Why are some frequencies more valuable than others? Because low frequencies travel far and penetrate buildings, so they are good for coverage, while high frequencies carry more data, so they are good for capacity, and the most valuable bands are those that do both reasonably well. Does the spectrum belong to anyone? It is a natural resource, managed by governments on behalf of the public, and licensed to users, with the revenue going to the state. Is spectrum a natural resource? Yes, it is a natural resource, like land or water, in the sense that it exists in nature and is finite, though it is not consumed by use, because a frequency can be reused in another place, and it can be shared in time, so it is a renewable resource, if it is managed well. Can spectrum be owned? No, it is licensed, not owned, and the licence is for a period, after which it can be renewed or re-auctioned, which is why operators must plan for the expiry of their licences. Why is spectrum auctioned? Because it is valuable and scarce, and an auction assigns it to the firm that values it most, while raising revenue for the government, which is more efficient than giving it away or assigning it by a beauty contest, in which firms compete on their plans, though both methods have been used.

Antennas

ID: JAH-TELC-000015 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: An antenna is a device that converts electrical signals into radio waves, for transmission, and radio waves into electrical signals, for reception. It is the interface between a radio and the air, and its size, shape, and direction decide how well a signal is sent and received. Antennas range from the small antennas inside a mobile phone to the huge dishes used for satellite links and for radio astronomy, and they are essential to all wireless telecommunications. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Antennas began with the experiments of Heinrich Hertz in 1887, who used a simple dipole antenna, two rods, to produce and detect radio waves. Guglielmo Marconi used larger antennas for his first wireless transmissions in the 1890s, and his transatlantic signal in 1901 used a large antenna supported by kites and masts. In the 1930s, the Yagi-Uda antenna, invented in Japan, gave a directional beam for television and for radar, and in the 1940s radar antennas, including the parabolic dish, were developed for the war. In the 1960s, large dishes were built for satellite communications, including the 64-metre dish at Parkes in Australia, and in the 1970s the first phased array antennas, which steer a beam electronically without moving, were developed for military radar. Today, antennas are in every phone, every Wi-Fi router, and every base station, and the largest, such as the 500-metre FAST telescope in China, are among the largest structures on Earth. Antennas have also been used for radar, which was developed in the 1930s and 1940s, when Robert Watson-Watt in the United Kingdom built the first practical radar system, which detected aircraft by the reflection of radio waves, and it was used in the Battle of Britain in 1940 to warn of incoming bombers. Radar antennas are often parabolic dishes, which send a narrow beam and listen for the echo, and the time for the echo to return gives the distance, while the direction gives the bearing. Modern radar uses phased arrays, which can steer the beam electronically and track many objects at once, and it is used for air traffic control, for weather forecasting, and for speed enforcement, as well as for military purposes.

Key concepts: A dipole is the simplest antenna, two conductors fed at the centre, which radiates in all directions around it. A parabolic dish is a curved reflector that focuses radio waves onto a receiver, or that sends a narrow beam, used for satellite links and for radio astronomy. A phased array is an antenna made of many small elements, whose signals are combined with different phases to steer the beam electronically, without moving the antenna. Gain is a measure of how much an antenna concentrates its power in a direction, compared with an antenna that radiates equally in all directions. Polarisation is the orientation of the electric field of a radio wave, and an antenna must match the polarisation of the signal it receives. A horn antenna is an antenna shaped like a horn, used for microwave links, because it is efficient and has a wide bandwidth. A helical antenna is an antenna wound in a helix, used for satellite links, because it produces a circularly polarised wave, which is less affected by the ionosphere. A patch antenna is a flat antenna, printed on a circuit board, used in mobile phones and in Wi-Fi routers, because it is small and cheap. An antenna array is a group of antennas working together, to give higher gain or to steer a beam, and it is the basis of massive MIMO in 5G. The radiation pattern is a diagram showing how an antenna radiates in different directions, which is used to design it for its purpose.

How it works: An antenna works by resonance. An electrical signal in the antenna makes electrons move back and forth, and their acceleration produces a radio wave, which radiates away at the speed of light. The antenna's length is related to the wavelength: a dipole is most efficient when it is about half a wavelength long, which is why a mobile phone antenna for 900 megahertz, with a wavelength of 33 centimetres, is about 16 centimetres long, though modern phones use smaller antennas with clever designs. A parabolic dish works by reflection: radio waves hitting the dish are reflected to a focus, where the receiver sits, so the dish collects waves over a large area and concentrates them, giving high gain. A phased array works by timing: the signals from many elements are delayed by different amounts, so they add up in one direction and cancel in others, steering the beam without moving the antenna, which is how 5G base stations direct signals to individual phones. Antennas also have to be matched to their feed, the cable that carries the signal to and from the antenna, because a mismatch reflects power back, wasting it and possibly damaging the transmitter. The standing wave ratio, or SWR, measures the mismatch, and a low SWR means a good match, while a high SWR means a poor match. The design of an antenna is therefore a matter of matching, of resonance, and of the radiation pattern, and it is a central part of radio engineering, taught to every telecommunications engineer.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A dipole for FM radio, at 100 megahertz, with a wavelength of 3 metres, is about 1.5 metres long, which is why FM antennas are about that size. A dipole for a mobile phone at 900 megahertz, with a wavelength of 33 centimetres, is about 16 centimetres long, and a dipole for Wi-Fi at 2.4 gigahertz, with a wavelength of 12.5 centimetres, is about 6 centimetres long. A satellite dish of 60 centimetres diameter, used for television, has a gain of about 35 decibels, which means it collects about 3,000 times as much power as an antenna that radiates in all directions. The FAST radio telescope in China has a dish 500 metres across, and it can detect signals far fainter than any smaller antenna could, which is why it is used to study pulsars and fast radio bursts. A second worked example uses the gain of a dish. A dish with a diameter of 1 metre, used at a frequency of 12 gigahertz, with a wavelength of 2.5 centimetres, has a gain of about 40 decibels, which is a factor of 10,000, meaning it collects 10,000 times as much power as an isotropic antenna, which radiates equally in all directions. A dish with a diameter of 10 metres has a gain about 100 times higher, because the gain grows with the square of the diameter, so a larger dish is dramatically more sensitive, which is why radio telescopes are built as large as possible, and why the FAST telescope, with a dish 500 metres across, is so powerful.

Real applications: Antennas are used in every wireless system, from a mobile phone to a satellite dish, and their design is a central part of telecommunications engineering. They are also used in radar, to detect aircraft and weather, in radio astronomy, to study the universe, and in navigation, to receive signals from satellites. The study of antennas is therefore the study of how to send and receive radio waves efficiently, and how to shape a beam to reach the intended receiver and not others, which is a matter of physics, of engineering, and of design. Antennas are also used in the Internet of Things, where small, low-power antennas let sensors communicate over short distances, and in wearable devices, where an antenna must work close to the human body, which absorbs radio waves and detunes the antenna, a challenge for designers. The study of antennas is therefore a study of how to make a device that works in the real world, not only in theory, and how to balance size, efficiency, and cost, which is a matter of engineering judgement as much as of physics.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why are antennas different sizes? Because an antenna's size is related to the wavelength, so low frequencies need large antennas and high frequencies need small ones, which is why a long-wave antenna is huge and a Wi-Fi antenna is tiny. Can an antenna be too small? Yes, a very small antenna is inefficient, though modern designs use clever shapes and materials to make small antennas work well, which is why a phone can have several antennas in a small space. Does a bigger antenna always work better? A bigger antenna has higher gain, so it can send and receive over longer distances, but it is also more directional, so it must be pointed accurately, which is why a satellite dish must be aimed precisely. Are antennas dangerous? An antenna itself is not dangerous, but a high-power transmitter can produce strong fields near the antenna, which is why transmitters are kept away from people and are regulated for safety. Is an antenna the same as a dish? A dish is one kind of antenna, a parabolic reflector, while an antenna is the general term for any device that sends or receives radio waves, including a dipole, a patch, and a helix. Can an antenna receive a signal from any direction? A dipole receives from all directions around it, while a dish receives only from the direction it is pointed, which is why a dish must be aimed, and why a dipole is used when the direction is unknown. Why do phones have several antennas? Because a phone uses several bands and several technologies, such as mobile, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and GPS, each of which needs its own antenna, and the antennas must be designed not to interfere with each other, in a very small space.

Undersea Cables

ID: JAH-TELC-000016 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Undersea cables are cables laid on the ocean floor, which carry almost all international internet traffic, along with telephone calls and data. They are made of optical fibres, protected by layers of steel and plastic, and they cross oceans in a network of more than a million kilometres of cable, connecting continents. Undersea cables are the hidden backbone of the global internet, and they carry far more traffic than satellites, because they have far higher capacity and lower delay. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The first undersea cable was laid across the English Channel in 1850, and the first transatlantic telegraph cable was laid in 1858, though it failed after a few weeks; a successful cable was laid in 1866, and it carried telegrams between Europe and North America. The first transatlantic telephone cable, TAT-1, was laid in 1956 and could carry 36 calls. The first transatlantic fibre-optic cable, TAT-8, was laid in 1988 and could carry 40,000 calls. Since then, the number and capacity of cables have grown rapidly, and there are now more than 400 active undersea cables, with a total length of over 1.3 million kilometres, carrying more than 99 percent of international data traffic. The cables are laid by specialised ships, and they are repaired by ships that can lift a damaged section to the surface, splice in a new piece, and lower it back to the seabed. Undersea cables have also been the subject of history. The first transatlantic cable, laid in 1866, was a major achievement, and it was celebrated as a wonder of the age, because it let a message cross the Atlantic in minutes, instead of the 10 days it took by ship. The cable was laid by the ship Great Eastern, the largest ship of its time, and it was a feat of engineering, of finance, and of international cooperation, involving British and American investors. The cable's success showed that the world could be connected by wire, and it was the start of the global telecommunications network, which has grown from that single cable to more than 400 cables today.

Key concepts: A submarine cable is a cable laid on the seabed, containing optical fibres and, in the case of a repeatered cable, amplifiers powered by electricity sent through the cable. A repeater is an amplifier placed along the cable, typically every 50 to 100 kilometres, which boosts the optical signal. A landing station is a building on the shore where a cable comes ashore and connects to the land network. A cable system is a set of cables connecting two or more landing stations, often owned by a consortium of companies. A cable fault is a break or damage to a cable, usually caused by a ship's anchor, by fishing, or by an earthquake, which is repaired by a cable ship. A cable ship is a ship designed to lay and repair undersea cables, with large drums to hold the cable, with equipment to bury it in shallow water, and with robots to find and lift a damaged cable. A plough is a device towed behind a cable ship, which buries the cable in the seabed, to protect it from anchors and fishing, in water up to about 1,500 metres deep. A repeater housing is a strong, watertight case that holds an amplifier, and it must withstand the pressure at the bottom of the ocean, which can be hundreds of times atmospheric pressure. A branching unit is a device that lets a cable branch to a third landing station, so that one cable can serve several places, which is common in modern systems.

How it works: An undersea cable works like a land fibre, but it must survive the ocean. The fibres are protected by layers: a core of glass fibres, a layer of steel wires for strength, and a coating of plastic to keep out water, and in shallow water an extra layer of armour protects against anchors and fishing gear. The signal is sent as light, and it is amplified by repeaters, which are powered by a current sent through the cable's copper conductor from the landing stations. The cable is laid by a ship that pays it out from a large drum, letting it sink to the seabed, where it follows the contours of the ocean floor, avoiding mountains and trenches where possible. The cable is mapped precisely, so that a repair ship can find a fault, which is located by sending a pulse along the fibre and measuring the reflection, a technique called optical time-domain reflectometry. Undersea cables are also vulnerable to natural disasters. An earthquake off Taiwan in 2006 damaged several cables and disrupted internet traffic across Asia for weeks, because the cables were the main link between Asia and the rest of the world, and the repair took time, because the damage was in deep water. A volcanic eruption in Tonga in 2022 cut the country's only cable, leaving it without internet for weeks, until a repair ship could reach it, which showed how dependent an island nation is on a single cable, and why redundancy, with more than one cable, is essential. The resilience of the cable network is therefore a matter of planning, of redundancy, and of rapid repair, and it is a concern for governments and for operators.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. There are more than 400 active undersea cables, with a total length of over 1.3 million kilometres, which is more than 30 times the circumference of Earth. A modern transatlantic cable can carry over 100 terabits per second, which is enough for millions of simultaneous video streams, and the total capacity of all cables is measured in petabits per second. The first transatlantic cable in 1866 could carry about 8 words a minute, while a modern cable can carry the equivalent of billions of words a second, a rise of many orders of magnitude. A cable fault is usually repaired in one to two weeks, though it can take longer in bad weather, and the network is built with redundancy, so traffic can be rerouted through other cables while one is repaired. A second worked example uses the length of a cable. The transatlantic cable from the United Kingdom to the United States is about 6,000 kilometres long, and a signal travelling through it at 200,000 kilometres per second takes about 0.03 seconds to cross, which is 30 milliseconds, a small delay that is not noticeable in a video call, though it adds to the total latency of the internet. A cable from Europe to Asia, via the Suez Canal, is about 15,000 kilometres long, and the signal takes about 0.075 seconds, or 75 milliseconds, to cross it. The delay is small, but it matters for applications that need instant response, such as financial trading, where a few milliseconds can be worth millions of pounds, which is why new, shorter cables are built to reduce the delay.

Real applications: Undersea cables are used for almost all international communications, including the internet, telephone calls, and financial transactions, and they are essential to the global economy. They are also a matter of security: cables can be tapped, they can be damaged deliberately, and their protection is a concern for governments, which is why their routes are often kept secret and why navies patrol the areas where they lie. The study of undersea cables is therefore the study of the hidden infrastructure of the internet, and of how the world stays connected across oceans. Undersea cables are also a matter of international law. The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, signed in 1982, gives all countries the right to lay cables on the seabed beyond territorial waters, and it requires them to protect cables from damage, though enforcement is difficult, because the ocean is vast and the cables are hard to monitor. The protection of cables is now a matter of national security, and several countries have navies that patrol cable routes, and laws that make it a crime to damage a cable deliberately, though accidental damage by anchors and fishing is far more common than deliberate damage.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are undersea cables the same as internet cables? Undersea cables are the main way the internet crosses oceans, and they carry internet traffic, telephone calls, and other data, so they are the internet's international links. Can a shark bite a cable? Sharks have bitten cables, though it is rare, and the main causes of faults are anchors, fishing, and earthquakes, which together account for most damage. How deep are the cables? They lie on the seabed, which can be more than 8,000 metres deep in the deepest trenches, though most cables lie in shallower water, and the pressure at that depth is enormous, which is why the cables are so strongly built. Who owns the cables? They are owned by consortia of telecommunications companies and, increasingly, by large internet companies, such as Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon, which have invested in cables to carry their own traffic. Are undersea cables the only way to cross oceans? No, satellites can also carry traffic across oceans, but they have lower capacity and higher delay, so they are used for remote places and for backup, while cables carry the bulk of the traffic, more than 99 percent of international data. Can a cable be tapped? Yes, a cable can be tapped, by bending the fibre to leak light, though it is difficult and it is usually done by governments, not by criminals, and the traffic is usually encrypted, so tapping the cable does not reveal the content. How are cables repaired? A repair ship finds the fault, lifts the cable to the surface, cuts out the damaged section, splices in a new piece, and lowers it back, a process that takes days to weeks, depending on the weather and the depth.

Broadcasting and Streaming

ID: JAH-TELC-000017 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Broadcasting is the transmission of audio and video to many receivers at once, by radio, television, or the internet, while streaming is the delivery of audio and video over the internet, in which the media are played as they arrive, rather than being downloaded first. Broadcasting was the dominant form of television and radio for most of the 20th century, and streaming, which began in the 2000s, has become a major alternative, letting viewers watch what they want, when they want, on any device. The two are converging: broadcasters now stream, and streaming services now broadcast live events. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Broadcasting began with radio in the 1920s, when the BBC was founded in 1922 and the first regular broadcasts began in the United States, and with television in the 1930s, when the BBC began the world's first regular television service in 1936. Satellite broadcasting began in the 1960s, cable television spread in the 1970s and 1980s, and digital broadcasting, which sends pictures as data, began in the 1990s. Streaming began with the first internet radio in the 1990s, and with video streaming in the 2000s, when YouTube was founded in 2005 and Netflix began streaming in 2007. In the 2010s, streaming grew rapidly, and by the 2020s it had become the main way many people watch television, with streaming services spending billions on programming and reaching hundreds of millions of subscribers worldwide. Broadcasting has also been a tool for education. The Open University, founded in the United Kingdom in 1969, used television and radio broadcasts to teach students who could not attend a university, and it was one of the first distance-learning institutions, reaching hundreds of thousands of students. The BBC's educational programmes, from children's television to adult learning, have taught generations, and the use of broadcasting for education continues, with online courses and with educational channels on streaming services. The power of broadcasting to reach a large audience at once makes it a valuable tool for education, especially in countries where schools are scarce.

Key concepts: A broadcast is a one-to-many transmission, in which the same signal is sent to all receivers at once. A stream is a one-to-one transmission over the internet, in which each viewer receives their own copy, so that they can pause, rewind, and choose what to watch. A content delivery network, or CDN, is a network of servers around the world that store copies of popular content close to viewers, so that it can be delivered quickly. Buffering is the storing of a short amount of video ahead of what is being played, so that a brief interruption in the network does not stop playback. Adaptive bitrate streaming is a technique that changes the quality of the video to match the available bandwidth, so that the video keeps playing even when the connection slows. A public service announcement is a message broadcast for the public good, such as a health warning or a safety message, which is carried free or at a low cost by broadcasters. A watershed is a time in the evening after which programmes unsuitable for children may be broadcast, in the United Kingdom at 9 pm, which is a rule set by the regulator to protect children. A licence fee is a fee paid by television owners to fund a public broadcaster, such as the BBC, which is 169.50 pounds a year in the United Kingdom in 2024. A ratings system is a system that measures how many people watch a programme, used by broadcasters and advertisers to judge its popularity, and it is based on a sample of households with meters that record what they watch.

How it works: Broadcasting works by sending the same signal to everyone: a transmitter radiates a radio signal, a satellite beams it to a wide area, or a cable network carries it to every home, and each receiver tunes to the channel it wants. The cost does not depend on the number of viewers, so broadcasting is efficient for popular live events, such as a football match watched by millions. Streaming works differently: each viewer requests a video from a server, and the server sends a stream of data to that viewer alone, so the cost grows with the number of viewers, but each viewer can choose what to watch and when. A CDN reduces the cost by storing copies of popular videos on servers close to viewers, so the data travel a short distance, and adaptive bitrate streaming adjusts the quality to the viewer's connection, dropping to a lower resolution when the bandwidth falls, so the video does not stop. Streaming has also changed the economics of media. A streaming service charges a subscription, typically 10 pounds a month, and it must attract enough subscribers to pay for the programmes it makes, which can cost hundreds of millions of pounds for a single series. The competition between services has driven up the cost of programming, and it has led to a proliferation of services, so that a viewer who wants to watch everything must subscribe to several, which is expensive, and it has led to calls for bundling, in which several services are sold together for a single price. The economics of streaming is therefore a new field, and it is still evolving, as the market matures and as services seek profitability.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A high-definition stream uses about 5 megabits per second, so a two-hour film uses about 4.5 gigabytes, because 5 million bits per second times 7,200 seconds is 36 billion bits, or 4.5 gigabytes. A 4K stream uses about 15 to 25 megabits per second, so the same film uses about 13.5 to 22.5 gigabytes. Netflix, the largest streaming service, has more than 260 million subscribers, and YouTube has more than 2 billion users a month, who watch more than a billion hours of video a day. A live broadcast of a football match can be watched by hundreds of millions of people at once, using the same spectrum, while the same event streamed to each viewer individually would need far more bandwidth, which is why broadcasters still use broadcasting for live events, and why streaming services use CDNs to share the load. A second worked example uses the cost of streaming. A service with 100 million subscribers, each paying 10 pounds a month, earns 1 billion pounds a month, or 12 billion pounds a year, which is enough to fund a large slate of programmes, though the cost of making them, of marketing, and of technology is also large, and many services have lost money for years while they grew. A service with 10 million subscribers earns 100 million pounds a month, or 1.2 billion pounds a year, which is far less, and it must spend less on programmes, which is why scale matters in streaming, and why the largest services dominate. The calculation shows why streaming is a business of scale, and why smaller services struggle to compete.

Real applications: Broadcasting and streaming are used for news, entertainment, education, and sport, and they are the main ways most people receive media. Broadcasting is regulated, with licences, with rules on content, and with public service obligations, while streaming is less regulated, though it is increasingly subject to rules on content and on competition. The two are converging, and the future of television is likely to be a mix: broadcast for live events, streaming for on-demand viewing, and a single internet connection carrying both. The study of broadcasting and streaming is therefore the study of how media reach people, and how technology changes the way they watch and listen. Broadcasting and streaming are also subject to regulation. Broadcasters must follow rules on impartiality, on accuracy, and on the protection of children, while streaming services, which are newer, are subject to fewer rules, though that is changing, as governments in the European Union and the United Kingdom introduce new laws to regulate online content, to protect children, and to ensure competition. The regulation of media is a balance between freedom of expression, which is essential to democracy, and the protection of the public, which is essential to safety, and it is a debate that continues, as the media change.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is streaming replacing broadcasting? Streaming has grown rapidly, but broadcasting remains important for live events, for news, and for reaching people without a fast internet connection, and many broadcasters now do both. Does streaming use more bandwidth than broadcasting? For a single viewer, streaming uses more, because each viewer gets their own stream, while broadcasting sends one signal to all, but streaming lets each viewer choose, which broadcasting cannot, so the two serve different needs. Why does a stream sometimes buffer? Because the network cannot deliver data fast enough, either because the connection is slow or because the server is busy, and the player waits for more data before it continues, which is why a faster connection and a CDN reduce buffering. Can I broadcast myself? Yes, anyone with a phone and an internet connection can stream live video to the world, which is a change from the days when broadcasting needed a licence and a transmitter, and it is why user-generated video has become a major part of the media. Is broadcasting free? Broadcast television funded by advertising is free to watch, though the viewer pays indirectly, by watching advertisements, while public broadcasting funded by a licence fee is paid for directly, and streaming is paid for by subscription, so each model has a different way of paying for the programmes. Does streaming use the internet? Yes, streaming is delivered over the internet, as data, while broadcasting is delivered by radio, cable, or satellite, as a signal, though the distinction is blurring, as broadcasters stream and streaming services broadcast live. Can I watch television without a licence? In the United Kingdom, a licence is needed to watch live television or to use the BBC's iPlayer, but not to watch other on-demand services, though the rules are complex and they are enforced by the regulator.

Emergency Communications

ID: JAH-TELC-000018 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Emergency communications are the telecommunications systems used to call for help, to warn the public, and to coordinate the response to an emergency. They include the emergency telephone number, such as 999 in the United Kingdom and 112 in the European Union, the radio systems used by police, fire, and ambulance services, and the public warning systems that send alerts to mobile phones. Emergency communications must work when other systems fail, so they are built with redundancy, with priority for emergency calls, and with the ability to work in a disaster. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The first emergency telephone number, 999, was introduced in London in 1937, after a fire in which a neighbour had struggled to call the fire brigade, and it was the first emergency number in the world. The number 911 was introduced in the United States in 1968, and the number 112 was introduced in the European Union in 1991, as a single number that works across member states. The first radio systems for emergency services were introduced in the 1930s and 1940s, and they were improved after disasters, such as the King's Cross fire in London in 1987, which showed that the fire brigade's radios did not work underground. In the 2000s, many countries replaced analogue emergency radios with digital systems, such as TETRA in Europe and P25 in the United States, which are more secure and can carry data as well as voice. In the 2010s, public warning systems that send alerts to mobile phones, such as the Wireless Emergency Alerts system in the United States and the UK Emergency Alerts system, launched in 2023, were introduced to warn the public of floods, of severe weather, and of other dangers. Emergency communications have also been tested by disasters. The 7 July 2005 bombings in London overloaded the mobile networks, because millions of people tried to call at once, and the emergency services' radio network, which was separate, continued to work, though it was criticised for not working in the Underground, where the bombings happened. The lessons led to improvements, including the use of text messages to communicate when voice calls fail, and the development of a new emergency network based on 4G, which will work in tunnels and basements. The 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan damaged the mobile networks, and the government used satellite phones and emergency broadcasts to communicate, which showed the value of having several systems, so that if one fails, another can take over.

Key concepts: An emergency number is a short telephone number, such as 999 or 112, that connects a caller to the emergency services. A public warning system is a system that sends alerts to the public, by text, by broadcast, or by siren, to warn of a danger. A TETRA network is a digital radio network used by emergency services in Europe, which provides secure voice and data. A priority call is a call that is given priority over other calls, so that an emergency call is connected even when the network is busy. A resilient network is a network designed to keep working in a disaster, with backup power, with multiple routes, and with the ability to be repaired quickly. A satellite phone is a phone that communicates by satellite, rather than by a base station, so it works anywhere on Earth, including at sea and in remote areas, though it is expensive and it needs a clear view of the sky. A two-way radio is a radio that can both transmit and receive, used by emergency services, by security staff, and by walkers, to communicate over short distances without a network. A siren is a loud warning device, used to alert the public in an area, for example of a flood or of an air raid, and it is still used in some countries, though it is being replaced by mobile alerts. A call centre is a place where emergency calls are answered, by operators who are trained to handle them, and who can dispatch the right service.

How it works: Emergency communications work by giving emergency calls priority and by using dedicated systems. When a person dials 999, the call is routed to an operator, who asks which service is needed and connects the caller, and the operator can see the caller's location, from the telephone network or from the phone's GPS, so that help can be sent even if the caller cannot speak. The emergency services communicate with each other by radio, on a dedicated network that is separate from the public mobile network, so that it is not congested by public calls, and so that it works in places where the public network does not reach, such as tunnels and basements. Public warnings are sent by cell broadcast, a technique that sends a message to all phones in an area at once, without needing to know their numbers, which is why an alert can reach millions of phones in seconds. Emergency communications also depend on the public knowing what to do. The advice in the United Kingdom is to dial 999, to say which service is needed, to give the location, and to stay on the line, and to use the text service, 999, for people who are deaf or who cannot speak, which is a service that lets a person text the emergency services, after registering. The public are also advised to sign up for flood warnings, to listen for alerts, and to follow the advice of the emergency services, which is why public education is part of emergency communications, not only the technology.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. The emergency number 999 was introduced in London in 1937, and it is now used in the United Kingdom, where it receives about 35 million calls a year, of which about a third are genuine emergencies. The number 112 works in all European Union member states, and it can be dialled from any phone, even without a SIM card, and even when the phone is locked. The UK Emergency Alerts system, launched in 2023, can send a message to millions of phones in an area in seconds, and it was first tested nationally in April 2023. The TETRA network used by emergency services in the United Kingdom, called Airwave, has more than 300,000 users, including police, fire, and ambulance services, and it is being replaced by a new system based on 4G, called the Emergency Services Network. A second worked example uses the capacity of an emergency network. The Airwave network in the United Kingdom has more than 300,000 users, and it can handle thousands of simultaneous calls, because it is a dedicated network, with its own spectrum and its own base stations, so it is not congested by public traffic. A public mobile network, by contrast, can be overloaded in an emergency, because millions of people try to call at once, which is why the emergency services use a separate network, and why the public are advised to text rather than call in an emergency, because a text uses far less capacity than a voice call, and it is more likely to get through when the network is busy.

Real applications: Emergency communications are used to save lives, to warn the public, and to coordinate the response to disasters. They are the first service that people turn to in an emergency, and they are the service that must work when others fail, which is why they are given priority in the network, why they have backup power, and why they are tested regularly. The study of emergency communications is therefore the study of how to build a system that works under the worst conditions, and how to ensure that help can always be reached, which is a matter of engineering, of organisation, and of public trust. Emergency communications are also being improved by technology. The new Emergency Services Network in the United Kingdom, based on 4G, will let emergency services use video, data, and location, as well as voice, and it will work in more places, including the Underground, because it will use the mobile network's coverage, with priority for emergency users. The system has been delayed, and its cost has risen, which shows how hard it is to build a new emergency network, and how important it is to get it right, because lives depend on it.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is 999 the same as 112? In the United Kingdom both work, and 112 is the European emergency number, which works across the European Union, while 999 is the United Kingdom's original number, introduced in 1937. Can I call 999 from a mobile without credit? Yes, emergency calls are free, and they can be made even without a SIM card or without credit, and the network must connect them. What happens if I call 999 by accident? The operator will ask if there is an emergency, and if there is no answer, the call may be passed to the police, so it is best to stay on the line and explain that it was an accident, rather than hanging up. Do emergency alerts work on all phones? Cell broadcast alerts work on most modern phones, and they are sent to all phones in an area, regardless of the network, though very old phones may not receive them, and the system is tested to ensure it reaches as many people as possible. Is 999 the only emergency number? No, 112 works in the United Kingdom and across Europe, and it can be dialled from any phone, and the number 101 is for non-emergency police calls, while 111 is for non-emergency medical advice, so the public should use the right number for the right problem, to avoid overloading the emergency number. Can I text 999? Yes, if you are registered for the emergencySMS service, which is for people who are deaf, hard of hearing, or speech-impaired, and it lets you text the emergency services, though it is slower than a call, so a call is preferred if you can make one. What should I do if I cannot speak? The operator will ask you to cough or tap, and if you do not respond, the call may be passed to the police, who can trace the location, so it is best to make a noise if you can, to show that you need help.

Network Security

ID: JAH-TELC-000019 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Network security is the protection of telecommunications networks and the data they carry from unauthorised access, from damage, and from disruption. It includes encryption, which scrambles data so that only the intended recipient can read it, authentication, which checks that a user is who they claim to be, and firewalls, which block unwanted traffic. Network security matters because networks carry personal data, financial transactions, and government communications, and a failure can lead to theft, to fraud, or to the disruption of essential services. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Network security began with the need to protect military communications, and the first ciphers were used in ancient times, though the modern field began with the work of Claude Shannon on information theory in 1948 and with the development of public-key cryptography by Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman in 1976, which let two people who had never met share a secret. The first computer virus, called Creeper, appeared in the early 1970s, and the first worm, called Morris, spread across the early internet in 1988, infecting thousands of computers. In the 1990s, the growth of the internet and of online commerce made security essential, and the Secure Sockets Layer, or SSL, protocol, developed by Netscape in 1994, let browsers and servers communicate securely, becoming Transport Layer Security, or TLS, in 1999. In the 2000s and 2010s, attacks grew in scale, from the theft of credit card numbers to ransomware, which encrypts a victim's data and demands payment, and governments began to treat network security as a matter of national security, with laws and agencies to protect critical infrastructure. Network security has also been shaped by law. The Computer Misuse Act 1990 in the United Kingdom makes it a crime to access a computer without authorisation, to access it with intent to commit a further offence, and to impair its operation, and similar laws exist in other countries, including the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act in the United States, passed in 1986. The General Data Protection Regulation, or GDPR, which came into force in the European Union in 2018, requires organisations to protect personal data and to report breaches, and it can fine them up to 4 percent of their worldwide turnover, which has made security a board-level issue, not only a technical one.

Key concepts: Encryption is the process of scrambling data so that only someone with the key can read it. Authentication is the process of checking that a user or a device is who it claims to be, for example by a password or by a fingerprint. A firewall is a device or program that blocks unwanted network traffic, while allowing wanted traffic through. Malware is malicious software, including viruses, worms, and ransomware, designed to damage or to steal. A denial-of-service attack is an attack that overwhelms a network or a server with traffic, so that legitimate users cannot use it. A virtual private network, or VPN, is a secure tunnel through a public network, which encrypts the data that pass through it. A zero-day vulnerability is a flaw in software that is unknown to the maker, so there is no fix, and it is valuable to attackers, who can exploit it before it is patched. A patch is a fix for a flaw, issued by the maker, which users should install promptly, because attackers exploit known flaws quickly. Social engineering is the manipulation of people, rather than of technology, to gain access, for example by a phishing email that tricks a person into revealing a password, and it is one of the most common attacks, because people are often the weakest link. A botnet is a network of infected computers, controlled by an attacker, used to send spam, to steal data, or to launch a denial-of-service attack, and the Mirai botnet in 2016 used cameras and routers to attack websites.

How it works: Network security works in layers. Encryption protects data in transit: when a browser connects to a website using HTTPS, the data are encrypted with TLS, so that an eavesdropper on the network cannot read them. Authentication protects access: a user proves who they are with a password, with a second factor, such as a code sent to their phone, or with a biometric, such as a fingerprint. A firewall protects the boundary: it inspects traffic and blocks what does not match its rules, such as traffic from a known malicious address. Intrusion detection looks for signs of an attack, such as unusual traffic, and alerts the operator. The security of a network depends on all of these layers, and on the people who use it, because the weakest link is often a person who clicks on a malicious link or who uses a weak password, which is why training and awareness are as important as technology. Network security also depends on the supply chain. A network is built from hardware and software from many suppliers, and a flaw in one supplier's product can affect the whole network, which is why governments are concerned about the security of the equipment used in critical networks, such as 5G, and why some countries have banned equipment from certain suppliers, on security grounds, a decision that is both technical and political. The security of the supply chain is now a major part of network security, and it is why operators must know where their equipment comes from and whether it can be trusted.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. A password of 8 characters, chosen from 95 possible characters, has 95 to the power of 8 possible combinations, which is about 6,600 trillion, a number so large that it would take a computer trying a billion passwords a second about 200 years to try them all, though in practice attackers use dictionaries and stolen passwords, so a long, unique password is essential. The Morris worm of 1988 infected about 6,000 computers, which was about 10 percent of the internet at the time, while a modern ransomware attack can affect hundreds of organisations at once, and the cost of cybercrime worldwide is estimated in the trillions of dollars a year. The TLS protocol, which secures the Web, is used by more than 90 percent of web traffic, and the shift to HTTPS, which encrypts the connection, was driven by browsers warning users about insecure sites. A second worked example uses the cost of a breach. The average cost of a data breach worldwide is estimated at about 4.5 million dollars, according to industry reports, and the cost includes the loss of data, the fines, the legal costs, and the loss of trust, which can be larger than the direct costs. A ransomware attack on a hospital can cost lives, not only money, because it can disrupt care, which is why hospitals are a target, and why their security is a matter of public safety, not only of business. The numbers show that security is not a cost to be minimised but an investment to be made, because the cost of a failure is far larger than the cost of prevention.

Real applications: Network security is used to protect personal data, to protect financial transactions, and to protect critical infrastructure, such as power grids, hospitals, and transport systems, which depend on networks. It is also used by governments to protect their communications and to defend against attacks from other states, which is why network security is a matter of national security as well as of personal safety. The study of network security is therefore the study of how to build a network that can be trusted, and how to respond when that trust is broken, which is a continuing task, because attackers keep finding new ways in, and defenders keep finding new ways to keep them out. Network security is also a matter of international cooperation, because attackers operate across borders, and a network in one country can be attacked from another, so countries share information, they cooperate on investigations, and they set standards together, through bodies such as the ITU and the IETF. The cooperation is imperfect, because countries have different laws and different interests, but it is essential, because no country can secure its networks alone, and the internet is a global system that needs global rules.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is encryption unbreakable? Modern encryption, such as AES with a 256-bit key, is considered unbreakable in practice, because trying all possible keys would take longer than the age of the universe, though it depends on the key being kept secret and on the implementation being correct. Can a firewall stop all attacks? No, a firewall blocks traffic that does not match its rules, but it cannot stop an attack that comes through an allowed channel, such as a malicious email, or an attack by an insider, which is why security needs several layers. Is a VPN enough to be safe? A VPN encrypts the traffic between the user and the VPN server, but it does not protect against malware, against phishing, or against a compromised server, so it is one tool among many, not a complete solution. Why do passwords matter? Because a weak or reused password is the most common way attackers gain access, and a strong, unique password, with a second factor, is the simplest and most effective protection a person can use. Is security the same as privacy? No, security is about protecting data from unauthorised access, while privacy is about controlling who can collect and use data, and the two are related but distinct: a system can be secure but not private, if it collects data lawfully but uses it in ways the user does not expect, and it can be private but not secure, if it does not collect data but is vulnerable to attack. Can a network be 100 percent secure? No, no network is completely secure, because new vulnerabilities are found, because people make mistakes, and because attackers are creative, so security is a matter of managing risk, not of eliminating it, and the goal is to make an attack hard enough and expensive enough that it is not worth the attacker's effort. Should I use a password manager? Yes, a password manager lets a person use a different, strong password for each site, without having to remember them all, and it is one of the best ways to improve personal security, along with two-factor authentication.

5G and Beyond

ID: JAH-TELC-000020 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: 5G is the fifth generation of mobile network technology, which brings higher speeds, lower latency, and the capacity to connect many more devices than 4G. It uses new radio frequencies, including millimetre-wave bands, new antenna techniques, such as massive MIMO, which uses many antennas to serve many users at once, and a new core network, which is software-based and can be sliced into virtual networks for different uses. 5G is the basis for new applications, from remote surgery to self-driving cars, though many of those applications are still being developed. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: 5G was standardised by the 3rd Generation Partnership Project, or 3GPP, in Release 15, published in 2018, and the first commercial 5G networks were launched in South Korea and the United States in 2019. The United Kingdom's first 5G networks were launched in 2019, and by the mid-2020s 5G was available in most cities in advanced economies, though rural coverage was still limited. The development of 5G was driven by the growth of mobile data, which doubles every few years, and by the need to connect the Internet of Things, which will add billions of devices to the network. Research on 6G, the next generation, began in the late 2010s, with the first standards expected in the late 2020s and the first networks in the 2030s, and it is expected to bring still higher speeds, lower latency, and new uses, such as holographic communications and the integration of sensing and communications. 5G has also raised questions about health and about the environment. The radio waves used by 5G are non-ionising, and the scientific consensus, based on many studies, is that they do not cause harm at the levels used, though the subject is studied continuously, and the networks are regulated for safety, with limits on the power that a base station can transmit. The environmental impact of 5G is also studied: the network needs more base stations, which use more energy, but it can also save energy, by letting devices sleep when they are not needed, and by enabling smart grids and smart transport, which reduce emissions overall, so the net effect is debated and depends on how the network is built and used.

Key concepts: Massive MIMO, or multiple-input multiple-output, is a technique that uses many antennas at a base station to serve many users at once, by directing a beam to each user. Beamforming is the technique of directing a radio beam toward a specific user, rather than radiating in all directions, which increases the signal strength and reduces interference. Network slicing is the technique of dividing a physical network into virtual networks, each with its own performance, so that one slice can serve emergency services with low latency while another serves video streaming with high bandwidth. Latency is the delay in a network, and 5G aims for a latency of about 1 millisecond, compared with about 30 to 50 milliseconds for 4G. Millimetre-wave is the name for radio frequencies above 24 gigahertz, which carry a great deal of data but travel only a short distance. A small cell is a small base station, used in 5G to provide coverage in a small area, such as a street or a building, because the higher frequencies used by 5G travel a shorter distance. A private 5G network is a network built for a single organisation, such as a factory or a port, which gives it control over its performance and its security, and it is a growing market, because it lets a firm use 5G without relying on the public network. Edge computing is the processing of data close to where it is produced, rather than in a distant data centre, which reduces latency, and it is used with 5G for applications that need instant response, such as remote control of machines and augmented reality.

How it works: 5G works by using a wider range of frequencies and by using the spectrum more efficiently. A 5G base station uses massive MIMO, with dozens or hundreds of antennas, to serve many users at once, each with a beam directed at them, which increases the capacity of the cell. It uses millimetre-wave frequencies, where there is a great deal of unused spectrum, to provide very high speeds in dense areas, such as stadiums and city centres, and it uses lower frequencies for wider coverage, to reach rural areas. The core network is software-based, which means it can be updated and reconfigured quickly, and it can be sliced, so that a factory can have its own private 5G network, with guaranteed performance, separate from the public network. The result is a network that is faster, more responsive, and more flexible than 4G, though it needs many more base stations, because the higher frequencies travel a shorter distance. 5G also depends on the fibre network, because each base station must be connected by fibre to the core network, to carry the traffic it receives, and the rollout of 5G therefore depends on the rollout of fibre, which is why the two are often built together. The cost of building 5G is high, because it needs many more base stations than 4G, and the return is uncertain, because many of the applications that will use 5G are still being developed, which is why operators have been cautious about investing, and why governments have offered support, in the form of spectrum, of planning permission, and of funding for rural coverage.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. 4G can deliver speeds of about 100 megabits per second, while 5G can deliver over 1,000 megabits per second in ideal conditions, a rise of more than 10 times, and in laboratory tests 5G has reached more than 10,000 megabits per second. The latency of 4G is about 30 to 50 milliseconds, while 5G aims for about 1 millisecond, a reduction of more than 30 times, which matters for applications that need instant response, such as remote control of machines. A 5G cell can in theory connect a million devices per square kilometre, compared with about 100,000 for 4G, which is why 5G is designed for the Internet of Things. The first 5G networks were launched in 2019, and by 2024 there were more than 1.5 billion 5G subscriptions worldwide, though that is still a minority of all mobile subscriptions. A second worked example uses the number of base stations. A 4G network in the United Kingdom has about 30,000 base stations, while a 5G network, using higher frequencies, may need two or three times as many, or about 60,000 to 90,000, to provide the same coverage, because the higher frequencies travel a shorter distance. The cost of each base station is tens of thousands of pounds, so the total cost is billions, which is why the rollout is gradual, starting in cities, where the demand is highest, and reaching rural areas later, often with government support, to ensure that the benefits of 5G are shared.

Real applications: 5G is used for faster mobile broadband, for fixed wireless access, which brings broadband to homes without a cable, and for industrial applications, such as connecting robots in a factory, monitoring equipment, and controlling vehicles. It is also used for the Internet of Things, connecting sensors, meters, and devices, and for public services, such as remote health monitoring and smart city systems. The study of 5G is therefore the study of how a mobile network can become a general-purpose platform, not only for phones but for machines, for vehicles, and for the whole economy, and how the next generation, 6G, will extend that platform further. 5G is also a matter of geopolitics. The equipment used in 5G networks is made by a small number of companies, including Huawei of China, Ericsson of Sweden, and Nokia of Finland, and the choice of supplier has become a political issue, because of concerns about security and about dependence on a foreign supplier. Several countries, including the United Kingdom and the United States, have restricted the use of Huawei equipment in their 5G networks, on security grounds, a decision that has raised costs and delayed the rollout, and it shows how telecommunications, which was once a technical subject, has become a matter of national security and of international politics.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is 5G dangerous? No, 5G uses radio waves in the same range as earlier generations and as Wi-Fi, and the scientific consensus, based on many studies, is that it does not cause harm at the levels used, though the subject is studied continuously and the networks are regulated for safety. Does 5G replace 4G? Not immediately: 5G networks are built alongside 4G, and phones use 4G where 5G is not available, and 4G will remain in use for many years, especially in rural areas. Is 5G only about faster phones? No, its most important uses may be in industry, in the Internet of Things, and in services that need low latency, such as remote control and autonomous vehicles, rather than in faster downloads for phones. What comes after 5G? 6G, which is in research, is expected in the 2030s, with higher speeds, lower latency, and new capabilities, such as the integration of sensing, so that the network can detect objects as well as communicate with them. Is 6G already being built? No, 6G is in research, with the first standards expected in the late 2020s and the first networks in the 2030s, and it is expected to use even higher frequencies, including terahertz, and to integrate sensing, so that the network can detect objects, as well as communicate, which could be used for autonomous vehicles, for health monitoring, and for the metaverse, though the applications are still speculative. Does 5G use more energy? A 5G base station uses more energy than a 4G one, but it carries far more data, so the energy per bit is lower, and the network can be more efficient overall, though the total energy use may still rise, because the demand for data continues to grow. Will 5G replace Wi-Fi? No, 5G and Wi-Fi are complementary: 5G provides wide-area coverage, while Wi-Fi provides high-speed coverage indoors, and a phone will use both, switching between them as needed.

The Internet of Things

ID: JAH-TELC-000021 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: The Internet of Things, or IoT, is the network of physical objects, such as sensors, appliances, vehicles, and machines, which are connected to the internet and can collect and exchange data. It lets a thermostat learn a household's routine, a factory monitor its machines, a farmer monitor soil moisture, and a city monitor traffic and air quality, all without a person being present. The IoT is one of the fastest-growing areas of telecommunications, because the cost of sensors and of connectivity has fallen, and because the data they produce are valuable. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The term Internet of Things was coined by Kevin Ashton in 1999, when he was working on radio-frequency identification, or RFID, tags, which let objects be identified by radio. In the 2000s, sensors became cheaper and smaller, and wireless networks, including Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and mobile networks, let them connect to the internet. In the 2010s, the number of connected devices grew rapidly, and by the early 2020s there were more connected devices than people, with estimates of more than 15 billion devices, including phones, computers, and sensors. The growth has been driven by the fall in the cost of sensors, by the spread of mobile and Wi-Fi networks, and by the value of the data, which are used to improve efficiency, to save energy, and to provide new services. The next stage, driven by 5G, is expected to connect billions more devices, from vehicles to industrial machines. The IoT has also raised questions about standards. There are many competing standards for IoT, including LoRaWAN, Sigfox, NB-IoT, and Zigbee, each with its own strengths, and the lack of a single standard has slowed the growth of the IoT, because a device that uses one standard cannot talk to a device that uses another. The industry is working toward common standards, and the Matter standard, launched in 2022, aims to let smart home devices from different makers work together, though it is still early, and the fragmentation remains a problem, which is why the choice of standard is a key decision for anyone building an IoT system.

Key concepts: A sensor is a device that measures a physical quantity, such as temperature, humidity, or motion, and turns it into data. An actuator is a device that acts on the physical world, such as a motor or a valve, in response to data. RFID, or radio-frequency identification, is a technology that lets an object be identified by a radio tag, used in shops, in logistics, and in passports. A smart device is a device that can connect to a network and can be controlled remotely, such as a smart thermostat or a smart light. A low-power wide-area network, or LPWAN, is a network designed for IoT devices, which sends small amounts of data over long distances using very little power, so that a sensor can run on a battery for years. A gateway is a device that connects IoT devices to the internet, translating between the protocol used by the devices and the protocol used by the internet, so that a sensor that uses a low-power protocol can be reached from the Web. A digital twin is a virtual model of a physical object, such as a machine or a building, which is updated with data from sensors, so that it can be used to predict when the object will need maintenance, or to test changes without affecting the real object. A smart city is a city that uses IoT sensors and data to manage traffic, lighting, waste, and energy, with the aim of making the city more efficient and more liveable, though the benefits and the privacy implications are debated.

How it works: The IoT works by sensing, by communicating, and by acting. A sensor measures a quantity, such as the temperature in a room, and sends the data by a wireless link, such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, or a low-power network, to a server, which stores and analyses it. The server may send a command back, for example to turn on the heating, which is carried out by an actuator. The data are often analysed in the cloud, where machine learning can find patterns, such as when a machine is about to fail, so that it can be repaired before it breaks, a practice called predictive maintenance. The system is designed to use little power, because many sensors run on batteries and must last for years, so they send small amounts of data, infrequently, and they sleep between transmissions, which is why low-power networks were developed. The IoT also has a history of security failures. The Mirai botnet in 2016 infected hundreds of thousands of cameras and routers, which had default passwords, and used them to attack websites, taking them offline, which showed that IoT devices, if they are not secured, can be a threat to the whole internet, not only to their owners. The response has been new laws, such as the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act in the United Kingdom, passed in 2022, which bans default passwords and requires devices to be updateable, and similar laws in other countries, which are an attempt to make the IoT secure by design, rather than by accident.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. There are more than 15 billion connected devices in the world, and the number is expected to grow to more than 25 billion by 2030, according to industry estimates, though the exact number depends on how devices are counted. A sensor on a low-power network can run on a single battery for up to 10 years, sending a few bytes of data a day, while a sensor on Wi-Fi, which uses more power, may need to be recharged every few weeks. A smart meter, which measures electricity use, sends a reading every 30 minutes, or 48 readings a day, which is 17,520 readings a year, and the data are used to bill the customer and to manage the grid. A factory with 1,000 sensors, each sending 100 bytes a minute, produces 100,000 bytes a minute, or about 144 megabytes a day, a modest amount of data, but one that grows quickly with the number of sensors. A second worked example uses the data from a smart city. A city with 10,000 sensors, each sending 1 kilobyte of data an hour, produces 10 megabytes an hour, or 240 megabytes a day, a modest amount, but a city with 1 million sensors produces 1 gigabyte an hour, or 24 gigabytes a day, which is a large amount, and it needs a network and a data centre to store and analyse it. The data are used to manage traffic, to detect leaks, and to monitor air quality, and the value of the data is in the insights, not in the volume, which is why the IoT is as much about analytics as it is about connectivity.

Real applications: The IoT is used in homes, for heating, lighting, and security, in industry, for monitoring machines and for predictive maintenance, in agriculture, for monitoring soil and crops, in cities, for traffic, for lighting, and for air quality, and in health, for monitoring patients remotely. It is also used in logistics, to track goods, and in energy, to manage the grid. The study of the IoT is therefore the study of how the physical world is being connected to the internet, and how the data from that connection are used to make things more efficient, safer, and more convenient, though it also raises questions about privacy and security, because a connected device can be hacked, and the data it collects can reveal a great deal about a person. The IoT is also used in health. A wearable device can monitor a patient's heart rate, blood pressure, and activity, and send the data to a doctor, who can intervene if something is wrong, without the patient needing to visit a hospital. The remote monitoring of patients, called telehealth, grew rapidly during the pandemic, and it is now a major part of health care, though it raises questions about privacy, about the accuracy of the data, and about who is responsible if the system fails, which are being addressed by regulators and by the medical profession.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the IoT the same as the internet? No, the IoT is a part of the internet, made of physical objects that are connected to it, while the internet also carries the Web, email, and video, which are not physical objects. Are IoT devices secure? Many early IoT devices had poor security, with default passwords and no updates, and they were used in attacks, such as the Mirai botnet in 2016, which used cameras and routers to attack websites, so security is now a major concern, and new rules require IoT devices to have unique passwords and to be updateable. Does the IoT invade privacy? A connected device can collect data about a person's habits, their home, and their health, so privacy is a real concern, and the data should be protected by law and by design, with clear rules about what is collected and why. Will the IoT replace people? It automates some tasks, such as meter reading and monitoring, but it also creates new jobs, in installing, maintaining, and analysing the data, and its net effect on employment is still being studied. Is the IoT only for homes? No, the IoT is used in industry, in agriculture, in cities, and in health, and the home is only one of its uses, though it is the most visible, because smart speakers and smart thermostats are in many homes. Does the IoT need 5G? The IoT can use Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and low-power networks, as well as 5G, and the choice depends on the application: a sensor that sends a few bytes a day can use a low-power network, while a vehicle that needs instant response needs 5G, so the IoT uses many networks, not only one. Can IoT devices be hacked? Yes, many early devices had poor security, and they were hacked, which is why new laws require them to be secure, and why users should change default passwords and update their devices.

A History of Telecommunications

ID: JAH-TELC-000022 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: The history of telecommunications is the story of how people learned to send information over a distance, from smoke signals and drums to the telegraph, the telephone, radio, television, and the internet. It is a story of new technologies, each of which made communication faster, cheaper, and more widely available, and each of which changed society in ways that were not predicted. The history matters because it shows how a technology that began as a curiosity became an essential part of life, and how the network we use today was built, layer by layer, over almost 200 years. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: The earliest telecommunications were visual and audible: smoke signals, drums, and beacons, used for thousands of years to send simple messages over a distance. The optical telegraph, invented by Claude Chappe in France in 1792, used towers with movable arms to send messages across the country, and it was the first telecommunications network. The electric telegraph, built by Samuel Morse in the United States and by Cooke and Wheatstone in Britain in the 1830s and 1840s, sent messages as electrical pulses along wires, and the first transatlantic cable was laid in 1866. The telephone, patented by Bell in 1876, let people speak, and the first exchanges opened in 1878. Radio, developed by Marconi in the 1890s, sent signals without wires, and the first transatlantic radio signal was sent in 1901. Television began in the 1930s, satellite communications in 1962, and the internet in 1969, with the ARPANET, and the mobile phone in 1973, with the first call by Martin Cooper. In the 1990s, the internet became public, and in the 2000s mobile phones and broadband brought it to billions of people. The history of telecommunications has also been shaped by war. The telegraph was used in the Crimean War and in the American Civil War, to coordinate armies, and the telephone was used in the First World War, to connect headquarters to the front, though the lines were often cut by shelling, and runners and pigeons were still used. Radio was used in the Second World War, for radar, for navigation, and for propaganda, and the breaking of the Enigma code, used by Germany to encrypt its radio messages, was a major achievement, led by Alan Turing at Bletchley Park in the United Kingdom, which shortened the war. The internet was funded by the United States military, through the ARPANET, and the Global Positioning System was developed by the military, though it is now used by civilians. War has therefore been a driver of telecommunications, as it has been of many technologies, though the same technologies have also been used for peace, for commerce, and for personal communication.

Key concepts: The optical telegraph was a system of towers with movable arms, used to send messages by sight, which was the first long-distance telecommunications network. The electric telegraph was a system that sent messages as electrical pulses along wires, using Morse code, which was the first electrical telecommunications network. The telephone network was a network of wires and exchanges that let people speak, which was the first voice network. The radio network was a network that sent signals by radio waves, which was the first wireless network. The internet is a network of networks, which connects computers and devices, and it is the current form of telecommunications, built on all the networks that came before it. A code is a system for representing information, such as Morse code, which represents letters as dots and dashes, and a cipher is a system for scrambling information, so that only the intended recipient can read it, such as the Enigma machine, which the Germans used in the Second World War. A codebreaker is a person who tries to read a cipher without the key, and the work at Bletchley Park, where Turing and his colleagues broke Enigma, is one of the most famous examples, and it is estimated to have shortened the war by two years. A SIGINT station is a station that listens to radio signals, to gather intelligence, and the largest, such as Menwith Hill in the United Kingdom, are still in use, though their work is secret.

How it works: The history of telecommunications advances by a repeating pattern. A new technology is invented, often for a narrow purpose, and it is then adopted for wider uses, which its inventors did not predict. The telegraph was invented for railways, but it was used for news and for business. The telephone was invented for business, but it was used for personal calls. Radio was invented for ships, but it was used for broadcasting. The internet was invented for research, but it was used for commerce and for social media. Each technology also created new industries, new jobs, and new social habits, and each was regulated, because the spectrum and the networks are shared resources, and because the power of the new technology raised questions about privacy, about monopoly, and about access. The history of telecommunications is also the history of its regulation, from the first telegraph acts, which licensed operators, to the first radio acts, which allocated frequencies, to the modern regulation of the internet, which is still being debated. The regulation has always been a balance between the interests of the operator, who wants to make a profit, the interests of the user, who wants a cheap and reliable service, and the interests of the state, which wants to use the network for its own purposes, including security and universal service. The balance has shifted over time, from state monopoly to private competition, and from national regulation to international cooperation, and it continues to shift, as new technologies appear.

Worked examples: Take the numbers of the history. The optical telegraph, built in 1792, could send a message across France in a few hours, while the electric telegraph, built in the 1840s, could send it in minutes, and the internet, built in the 1960s, can send it in a fraction of a second. The first telephone call was made in 1876, and there are now more than 8 billion mobile subscriptions, a rise from one call to billions in less than 150 years. The first television broadcast was in 1936, and there are now more than 1.6 billion television households. The first ARPANET message was sent in 1969, and there are now more than 5 billion internet users, a rise from a handful of computers to billions in about 55 years. The speed of change is the most striking feature of the history, and it is why the subject is still changing today. A second worked example uses the speed of the telegraph. A message sent by telegraph in 1866 took minutes to cross the Atlantic, while a message sent by ship took 10 days, a difference of a factor of more than 10,000, which is why the cable was celebrated as a wonder. A message sent by the internet today takes a fraction of a second to cross the Atlantic, a further improvement of a factor of more than 10,000, which shows how the speed of communication has increased, from days to minutes to fractions of a second, in about 150 years, and how each improvement has changed the world, by making distance less important.

Real applications: The history of telecommunications is useful because it shows how technology and society shape each other. It teaches that a new technology is not only a technical change but also a social one, and that its effects depend on how it is used, how it is regulated, and who has access to it. It also preserves the contributions of many inventors, engineers, and operators, whose work built the network we use today, and it reminds us that the network is not a natural fact but a human achievement, built and maintained by people, and that it can be changed, improved, and extended, as it has been for almost 200 years. The history of telecommunications is also a reminder that technology is not neutral. A technology can be used for good or for ill, for communication or for surveillance, for freedom or for control, and the history shows both: the telegraph was used to coordinate armies and to send news, the radio was used for propaganda and for education, and the internet is used for commerce and for misinformation. The study of the history is therefore also a study of the responsibility that comes with a powerful technology, and of the need to govern it wisely, so that it serves the public good, not only the interests of the few.

Common questions and misconceptions: Was the telegraph the first telecommunications? No, smoke signals, drums, and beacons were used for thousands of years, and the optical telegraph of 1792 was the first network, but the electric telegraph of the 1840s was the first electrical network, and it is usually taken as the start of modern telecommunications. Did one person invent the telephone? No, Bell patented it, but Gray, Meucci, and others contributed, and the invention is now seen as the work of several people. Is the history of telecommunications finished? No, new technologies, such as 6G, quantum communications, and satellite internet, are being developed, and the history will continue to be written. Why study the history of a technology? Because it shows how the technology was built, how it was used, and how it changed society, which is the best guide to judging new technologies and to using them well. Was the internet invented for war? It was funded by the military, through ARPA, but its purpose was research, to let computers share resources, and it was built by academics, not by soldiers, though the military's interest in a resilient network shaped its design, which is why it is decentralised and can survive failures. Did the telephone change war? Yes, it let commanders coordinate over long distances, though it was vulnerable to being cut, and it was supplemented by radio, which could not be cut, and by runners, who could go where wires could not. Is the history of telecommunications the history of progress? In the sense that communication has become faster, cheaper, and more widely available, yes, but the history also includes failures, monopolies, and misuse, which is why it is a history to be studied critically, not only celebrated.

Telecommunications Regulation

ID: JAH-TELC-000023 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Telecommunications regulation is the set of rules set by governments and by international bodies to manage telecommunications networks, to allocate the spectrum, to ensure competition, to protect consumers, and to provide universal service, so that everyone can access basic communications. Regulation matters because telecommunications networks are essential, because they are natural monopolies in some parts, and because the spectrum is a shared resource that must be managed. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Regulation began with the telegraph, when governments licensed operators and set rates, and it grew with the telephone, when governments regulated the monopoly telephone companies, such as AT&T in the United States, which was regulated as a monopoly until it was broken up in 1984. The International Telecommunication Union, founded in 1865, has regulated the international use of the spectrum and the interconnection of networks since the 19th century. In the 1980s and 1990s, many countries privatised their telephone companies and introduced competition, creating independent regulators, such as Ofcom in the United Kingdom, founded in 2003, and the Federal Communications Commission in the United States, founded in 1934. In the 2000s and 2010s, regulation extended to the internet, with debates about net neutrality, the principle that internet providers should treat all traffic equally, and about the regulation of large online platforms, which are now subject to new laws in the European Union and in the United Kingdom. Telecommunications regulation has also had to deal with the convergence of media. In the past, telephone, television, and the internet were separate, with separate regulators, but they now use the same networks and the same devices, so a phone can be a television, a television can be a computer, and a computer can be a telephone, which is called convergence. Regulators have had to adapt, by merging, as Ofcom did in 2003, when it brought together the regulators for telecommunications, for broadcasting, and for radio, and by developing new rules for the converged world, such as the rules for video on demand, which are different from the rules for broadcast television, though the content is the same. Convergence is a continuing challenge for regulation, because the old categories no longer fit the new reality.

Key concepts: Universal service is the principle that everyone should have access to basic telecommunications, at an affordable price, even in remote areas where it is not profitable to provide it, and it is usually funded by a levy on operators or by the government. Net neutrality is the principle that internet providers should treat all data equally, without favouring some services over others, and it is a subject of regulation in many countries. Spectrum licensing is the granting of a licence to use a frequency band, usually by auction, for a period of years, with conditions on coverage and on use. Competition policy is the set of rules that prevent a firm from abusing its market power, for example by blocking a merger that would reduce competition. Consumer protection is the set of rules that protect users, for example by requiring clear contracts, by limiting the length of contracts, and by providing a way to complain. A converged regulator is a regulator that covers several sectors, such as telecommunications and broadcasting, which is the model in the United Kingdom, where Ofcom covers both, and in many other countries. A universal service obligation is a requirement that an operator provide a service to everyone, even where it is not profitable, such as providing broadband to rural areas, and it is funded by the operator or by the government. A price cap is a limit on the price an operator can charge, set by the regulator, to prevent a monopoly from charging too much, and it is often linked to inflation minus an efficiency factor, so that the operator must become more efficient over time. A market review is a study by a regulator of a market, to decide whether it is competitive, and if it is not, to impose remedies, such as requiring the dominant firm to share its network.

How it works: Regulation works by setting rules and by enforcing them. A regulator, such as Ofcom, allocates spectrum by auction, so that the frequencies go to the operator that values them most, and it sets conditions, such as a requirement to cover a certain share of the population within a certain time. It sets rules for interconnection, so that a customer of one network can call a customer of another, and it sets the prices that one network can charge another for carrying its traffic, called termination rates. It protects consumers by requiring operators to be transparent about prices, to let customers switch easily, and to handle complaints, and it can fine operators that break the rules. It also promotes competition by preventing a dominant firm from abusing its position, for example by refusing to let rivals use its network, and it can order a firm to share its infrastructure, such as its ducts and poles, with rivals, to make it easier for them to build their own networks. Regulation also has to deal with the global nature of telecommunications. A call from London to New York crosses several countries, and the internet crosses all of them, so regulation cannot be purely national, and international cooperation is needed, through the ITU, through the European Union, and through bilateral agreements. The cooperation is imperfect, because countries have different laws and different interests, but it is essential, because a network that stops at a border is of limited use, and the global network needs global rules, even if they are hard to agree.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. Ofcom, the United Kingdom's regulator, was founded in 2003, and it regulates telecommunications, broadcasting, and postal services, with a budget of about 150 million pounds a year, funded by fees from the companies it regulates. The Federal Communications Commission in the United States was founded in 1934, and it regulates interstate communications, including radio, television, and the internet. The ITU, founded in 1865, has 193 member states, and it holds World Radiocommunication Conferences every few years to allocate spectrum. The first spectrum auctions, held in the United States in the 1990s, raised billions of dollars, and the United Kingdom's 3G auction in 2000 raised 22.5 billion pounds, a record at the time, which showed how valuable spectrum is and how much governments could raise by selling it. A second worked example uses the cost of universal service. Providing broadband to a remote village can cost tens of thousands of pounds per household, because the fibre must be laid over a long distance for few users, while providing it to a household in a city costs hundreds of pounds, because the fibre is shared among many users. The universal service obligation, which requires the operator to serve the village, is therefore expensive, and it is funded by a levy on all users, or by the government, so that the cost is shared, and the village is not left behind. The calculation shows why universal service is a matter of policy, not only of economics, because the market alone would not serve the village, and the decision to serve it is a political one, about fairness and about inclusion.

Real applications: Telecommunications regulation is used to ensure that networks work for everyone, that competition keeps prices down and quality up, and that the spectrum is used efficiently. It is also used to protect national security, by requiring operators to cooperate with law enforcement and to protect their networks from attack, and to promote social goals, such as universal service and access for disabled people. The study of regulation is therefore the study of how a society governs an essential infrastructure, balancing the interests of operators, of consumers, and of the public, and it is a matter of law, of economics, and of politics, as well as of technology. Regulation is also used to protect children. The rules for broadcasting, which prevent programmes unsuitable for children from being shown before the watershed, have been extended to video on demand and to the internet, where new laws require platforms to protect children from harmful content, to verify ages, and to remove illegal material. The protection of children online is one of the most difficult challenges for regulation, because the internet is global and the content is vast, and the balance between protection and freedom of expression is hard to strike, which is why the laws are controversial and are still being tested.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is regulation the same as ownership? No, regulation sets the rules for private companies, while ownership means the government runs the network itself, which was the case in many countries before privatisation in the 1980s and 1990s. Does regulation slow innovation? It can, if the rules are too rigid, but it can also promote innovation, by ensuring competition, by opening networks to new entrants, and by setting standards that let new products work together. What is net neutrality? It is the principle that internet providers should treat all traffic equally, and it is debated because providers argue that they should be able to manage traffic and to charge for priority, while supporters argue that without it, providers could favour their own services and harm competition. Who regulates the internet? No single body regulates the whole internet, because it is global, but national regulators regulate access providers, and international bodies, such as the ITU and ICANN, coordinate the technical resources, such as domain names and addresses. Is regulation the same in every country? No, regulation differs, because countries have different histories, different markets, and different values, though they share the same principles, of competition, of universal service, and of consumer protection, and they learn from each other, through the ITU and through other bodies. Does regulation protect consumers? It aims to, by requiring transparency, by limiting prices where there is a monopoly, and by providing a way to complain, though its effectiveness depends on the regulator's powers and on its willingness to use them, and consumers must also protect themselves, by comparing prices and by understanding their rights. Can regulation keep up with technology? It is a continuing challenge, because technology changes faster than law, and regulators must be flexible, using principles rather than detailed rules, and updating them as the technology changes, which is why the best regulation is adaptive, not rigid.

Telecommunications in Everyday Life

ID: JAH-TELC-000024 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Telecommunications in everyday life is the use of phones, the internet, radio, and television in daily routines: waking to an alarm on a phone, checking the news, working from home on a video call, paying by phone in a shop, and watching a film in the evening. Telecommunications has become so woven into life that it is easy to forget how recent it is, and how much it has changed the way people live, work, and relate to each other. This record covers public record and standards only.

History: Everyday telecommunications has changed rapidly in living memory. In the 1970s, most homes had a single fixed telephone, in the hallway, and a call was a planned event. In the 1980s, the first mobile phones appeared, but they were expensive and heavy, and only a few people had them. In the 1990s, the internet arrived in homes, first by dial-up, which tied up the telephone line, and then by broadband, which was always on. In the 2000s, smartphones brought the internet into the pocket, and social media let people share their lives with friends around the world. In the 2010s and 2020s, video calls became normal, first for work and then for family, and during the pandemic of 2020, telecommunications became the way most people worked, learned, and socialised, when they could not meet in person. The change, from a single phone in the hallway to a computer in every pocket, has taken about 50 years. Telecommunications in everyday life has also changed the way people work. Remote work, which was rare before 2020, became normal during the pandemic, when millions of people worked from home using video calls, email, and cloud services, and it has remained common, with many people working from home part of the week, a pattern called hybrid work. The change has affected cities, which have fewer commuters, offices, which are used differently, and homes, which must have a space to work and a fast internet connection. The study of remote work is now a major field, and it is used by firms to decide how much office space they need, by governments to plan transport, and by workers to decide where to live, because a person who can work from home can live farther from the office, which has affected house prices in cities and in the countryside.

Key concepts: A smartphone is a mobile phone that is also a computer, with a touchscreen, a camera, and an internet connection, which can run apps for communication, for banking, for navigation, and for entertainment. A video call is a call in which the participants can see each other, using a camera and a screen, over the internet. A messaging app is an app that lets people send text, pictures, and voice messages over the internet, such as WhatsApp, which has more than 2 billion users. Contactless payment is a payment made by holding a card or a phone near a reader, using a short-range radio link, which is faster than inserting a card and entering a PIN. Remote work is work done from home or from another place, using telecommunications to connect to colleagues and to the office. A hybrid worker is a person who works partly from home and partly from the office, which is now the most common pattern for office workers in the United Kingdom. A digital nomad is a person who works remotely while travelling, using the internet to connect to their employer from anywhere in the world. A video conference is a meeting held by video call, with participants in different places, which has replaced many face-to-face meetings, though it has also been criticised for fatigue, called Zoom fatigue, because it requires more concentration than a face-to-face meeting. A cloud service is a service delivered over the internet, such as storage or software, which lets a worker access their files and programs from any device, anywhere.

How it works: Everyday telecommunications works by the networks described in this database: a phone connects by radio to a base station, which connects by fibre to the internet, which carries the data to the other person's phone, which may be on the other side of the world. A video call uses a camera and a microphone to capture sound and pictures, compresses them, sends them as packets over the internet, and decompresses them at the other end, all in real time, with a delay of a fraction of a second. A contactless payment uses a short-range radio link, called near-field communication, or NFC, which works over a few centimetres, to send the card's details to the reader, which then contacts the bank over the internet to approve the payment. The whole system depends on the networks being reliable, fast, and secure, which is why they are built with redundancy and with encryption. Everyday telecommunications also includes the use of phones for health. A person can book a doctor's appointment online, can have a video consultation, can order a prescription, and can monitor their health with a wearable device, all through telecommunications, which has made health care more accessible, especially for people in remote areas, though it has also raised questions about privacy and about the quality of remote care, which are being studied by health services and by regulators. The use of telecommunications in health, called telehealth, grew rapidly during the pandemic, and it is now a permanent part of the health system, though it is not a replacement for face-to-face care, but a complement to it.

Worked examples: Take the numbers. The average person in the United Kingdom spends about 4 hours a day on their phone, according to surveys, and checks it dozens of times a day. WhatsApp, the most widely used messaging app, has more than 2 billion users, who send more than 100 billion messages a day. A video call at high definition uses about 1 to 2 gigabytes of data per hour, so a person who works from home on video calls for 4 hours a day uses about 4 to 8 gigabytes a day, or about 100 to 200 gigabytes a month. Contactless payments, first introduced in the United Kingdom in 2007, now account for more than half of all card payments, and the limit for a contactless payment in the United Kingdom is 100 pounds, raised from 30 pounds in 2021. These numbers show how deeply telecommunications is woven into daily life, and how quickly it has changed. A second worked example uses the time saved by remote work. A worker who commutes for an hour each way, five days a week, spends 10 hours a week commuting, or about 500 hours a year, assuming 50 working weeks, which is more than 20 days a year spent travelling. If that worker works from home two days a week, they save 4 hours a week, or about 200 hours a year, which is more than 8 days, time that can be spent working, resting, or with family. The calculation shows why remote work is valued by workers, and why firms that offer it can attract staff, though the time saved must be weighed against the loss of face-to-face contact, which is valuable for collaboration and for learning.

Real applications: Telecommunications in everyday life is used for communication, for work, for shopping, for banking, for learning, and for entertainment, and it has made many things easier, faster, and cheaper. It has also raised new questions: about screen time, about privacy, about the effects of social media on mental health, and about the digital divide, the gap between those who have access to fast internet and those who do not. The study of telecommunications in everyday life is therefore not only technical but also social, and it is the study of how a technology changes the way people live, and how people adapt to it, for better and for worse. Telecommunications in everyday life is also a matter of inclusion. A person without internet access, or without a smartphone, is at a disadvantage, because so many services are online, from banking to government to shopping, and the digital divide, the gap between those who have access and those who do not, is a major social issue. Governments aim to close the divide, by extending broadband, by providing devices, and by teaching digital skills, and charities and community groups run programmes to help people get online, because access to telecommunications is now seen as essential, like access to electricity or to water, and a person without it is excluded from much of modern life.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is it possible to live without telecommunications? It is possible, but it is increasingly difficult, because so many services, from banking to government, are now online, and because so much of social life happens through phones and the internet, so a person without access is at a disadvantage. Does telecommunications make people lonelier? The evidence is mixed: it lets people stay in touch with friends and family over long distances, but it can also replace face-to-face contact, and heavy use of social media is associated with worse mental health in some studies, though the relationship is complex and not fully understood. Is the digital divide real? Yes, in the United Kingdom about 10 percent of households do not have internet access at home, and in poor countries the share is far higher, and the gap affects education, employment, and access to services, which is why governments aim to extend broadband to everyone. Will telecommunications keep changing? Yes, new technologies, such as 6G, satellite internet, and the Internet of Things, will continue to change how people communicate, and the everyday life of the future will be different again, in ways that are hard to predict. Is remote work here to stay? The evidence suggests yes, though the pattern is still settling, with most office workers now working from home part of the week, and firms adapting their offices and their management to the new pattern, though some firms have called workers back to the office, and the debate continues, about productivity, about collaboration, and about the future of cities. Does the phone make us less social? The evidence is mixed: phones let people stay in touch over long distances, but they can also distract from face-to-face conversation, and the advice is to use them mindfully, to put them away at meals and in conversation, and to use them to arrange meeting in person, not only to replace it. Will everyday telecommunications keep changing? Yes, new technologies, from augmented reality to the Internet of Things, will continue to change daily life, and the phone in the pocket today will seem as old-fashioned in 20 years as the phone in the hallway seems today.
