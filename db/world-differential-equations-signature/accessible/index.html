What Differential Equations Are

A differential equation is an equation that involves a function and its derivatives: it says how a quantity changes, and its solution is the quantity's history. The equation dy/dt = ky says that y grows in proportion to itself, and its solution is the exponential. Differential equations are the language in which the laws of nature are written: motion, heat, waves, and populations are all differential equations.

The equation dy/dt = 0.02y, with y(0) = 1,000, has the solution y = 1,000 e^(0.02t): after 10 years, y = 1,000 times e^0.2 = 1,221. The equation d^2y/dt^2 = -y, with y(0) = 1 and y'(0) = 0, has the solution y = cos(t): it oscillates with period 2pi. A cooling cup of coffee obeys Newton's law of cooling: the temperature difference from the room decays exponentially, with a half-life of about 10 minutes. A spring obeys m d^2x/dt^2 = -kx, whose solution is a sine wave with frequency sqrt(k/m).

First-Order Equations

A first-order differential equation involves only the first derivative: dy/dx = f(x, y). It says how y changes with x, and its solution is a function whose derivative matches. First-order equations are solved by separation of variables, by integrating factors, or by numerical methods. They model growth, decay, cooling, and mixing: the simplest laws of change. The general solution is a family of curves, one for each value of the constant, and the initial condition picks one.

The equation dy/dx = 2x has the solution y = x^2 + C: differentiating x^2 + C gives 2x. With y(0) = 1, C = 1, so y = x^2 + 1. The equation dy/dt = -0.1y, with y(0) = 100, has the solution y = 100 e^(-0.1t): after 10 seconds, y = 100 e^(-1) = 36.8. The equation dy/dx + 2y = 4 has the integrating factor e^(2x): multiplying gives d/dx (e^(2x) y) = 4 e^(2x), so e^(2x) y = 2 e^(2x) + C, and y = 2 + C e^(-2x). The equation dy/dx = y/x separates to dy/y = dx/x, giving ln|y| = ln|x| + C, so y = Cx.

Separable Equations

A separable equation is a first-order equation that can be written as g(y) dy = h(x) dx: the variables can be separated, one on each side. It is solved by integrating both sides, and the constant of integration is fixed by the initial condition. Separable equations are the simplest differential equations, and they model growth, decay, and the simplest laws of change.

The equation dy/dx = y separates to dy/y = dx, giving ln|y| = x + C, so y = A e^x, where A is a constant. With y(0) = 3, A = 3, so y = 3 e^x. The equation dy/dx = 2xy separates to dy/y = 2x dx, giving ln|y| = x^2 + C, so y = A e^(x^2). The equation dy/dt = -ky, with y(0) = 100 and k = 0.1, has the solution y = 100 e^(-0.1t): after 10 seconds, y = 36.8. The equation dy/dx = 1/(1 + x^2) has the solution y = arctan(x) + C, because the derivative of arctan(x) is 1/(1 + x^2).

Linear First-Order Equations

A linear first-order equation is dy/dx + P(x) y = Q(x): it is linear in y and its derivative. It is solved by the integrating factor, mu(x) = e to the integral of P(x): multiplying by mu turns the left side into the derivative of mu y, and integrating gives the solution. Linear first-order equations model cooling, mixing, circuits, and the simplest systems with an input.

The equation dy/dx + 2y = 4 has P(x) = 2, so the integrating factor is e^(2x): multiplying gives d/dx (e^(2x) y) = 4 e^(2x), so e^(2x) y = 2 e^(2x) + C, and y = 2 + C e^(-2x). With y(0) = 0, C = -2, so y = 2 - 2 e^(-2x): as x grows, y approaches 2. The equation dy/dx + y = x has the integrating factor e^x: the solution is y = x - 1 + C e^(-x). A tank with 100 litres of water and 10 kg of salt, with fresh water flowing in at 5 litres per minute and the mixture flowing out at the same rate, has the salt satisfying dy/dt = -0.05y, so y = 10 e^(-0.05t): after 20 minutes, y = 10 e^(-1) = 3.68 kg.

Exact Equations

An exact equation is a first-order equation written as M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy = 0, where the equation is the derivative of a function: it is exact when dM/dy = dN/dx, which says that M and N are the partial derivatives of the same function. The solution is that function, set equal to a constant. Exact equations are the generalisation of separable equations, and they are the equations that come from a potential.

The equation (2x + y) dx + (x + 2y) dy = 0 is exact: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1, so they are equal. The potential is x^2 + xy + y^2: its partial derivative with respect to x is 2x + y, and with respect to y is x + 2y. The solution is x^2 + xy + y^2 = C. The equation y dx + x dy = 0 is exact: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1, and the potential is xy: the solution is xy = C. The equation (x + y) dx + x dy = 0 is not exact: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1 — wait, it is exact too, because dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1. A non-exact example is y dx + dy = 0: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 0, so it is not exact.

Second-Order Linear Equations

A second-order linear equation is a y'' + b y' + c y = f(x): it involves the second derivative, and it is linear in y and its derivatives. Its solution is the sum of the homogeneous solution, from the characteristic equation, and a particular solution. Second-order equations model springs, circuits, and vibrations: the simplest systems that oscillate. The order is 2, so the general solution has two arbitrary constants, fixed by two initial conditions.

The equation y'' + y = 0 has the characteristic equation r^2 + 1 = 0, with roots r = i and r = -i, so the homogeneous solution is y = A cos(x) + B sin(x). With y(0) = 1 and y'(0) = 0, A = 1 and B = 0, so y = cos(x). The equation y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0 has the characteristic equation r^2 - 3r + 2 = 0, with roots r = 1 and r = 2, so the solution is y = A e^x + B e^(2x). The equation y'' + 2y' + y = 0 has the characteristic equation (r + 1)^2 = 0, with a repeated root r = -1, so the solution is y = (A + Bx) e^(-x). A spring with mass 1 kg and spring constant 4 N/m has frequency sqrt(4/1) = 2 rad/s, and period pi seconds.

Homogeneous Equations

A homogeneous linear equation is one with no forcing term: a y'' + b y' + c y = 0. Its solutions form a vector space: the sum of two solutions is a solution, and a multiple of a solution is a solution. The general solution is a combination of two independent solutions, and the dimension of the solution space is the order of the equation. Homogeneous equations are the equations of free motion: the spring with no force, and the circuit with no input.

The equation y'' + y = 0 has the fundamental set cos(x) and sin(x): the general solution is y = A cos(x) + B sin(x), and the Wronskian is cos(x) cos(x) + sin(x) sin(x) = 1, which is non-zero, so the solutions are independent. The equation y'' - y = 0 has the fundamental set e^x and e^(-x): the general solution is y = A e^x + B e^(-x). The equation y'' = 0 has the fundamental set 1 and x: the general solution is y = A + Bx. A spring with no force obeys the homogeneous equation m x'' + kx = 0, whose solution is a sine wave with frequency sqrt(k/m).

Characteristic Equations

The characteristic equation is the polynomial obtained by guessing a solution of the form e^(rx): for a y'' + b y' + c y = 0, it is a r^2 + b r + c = 0. Its roots decide the form of the solution: distinct real roots give exponentials, complex roots give sines and cosines, and a repeated root gives an extra factor of x. The characteristic equation is the key to solving a linear equation with constant coefficients.

The equation y'' - 3y' + 2y = 0 has the characteristic equation r^2 - 3r + 2 = 0, with discriminant 9 - 8 = 1, and roots r = 1 and r = 2: the solution is y = A e^x + B e^(2x). The equation y'' + 4y = 0 has the characteristic equation r^2 + 4 = 0, with roots r = 2i and r = -2i: the solution is y = A cos(2x) + B sin(2x). The equation y'' - 2y' + y = 0 has the characteristic equation (r - 1)^2 = 0, with a repeated root r = 1: the solution is y = (A + Bx) e^x. The equation y'' + y' + y = 0 has the characteristic equation r^2 + r + 1 = 0, with discriminant 1 - 4 = -3, and complex roots r = (-1 ± i sqrt(3))/2.

Nonhomogeneous Equations

A nonhomogeneous linear equation is a y'' + b y' + c y = f(x): it has a forcing term f(x), and its solution is the sum of the homogeneous solution and a particular solution. The particular solution is found by the method of undetermined coefficients, when f(x) is a polynomial, exponential, sine, or cosine, or by variation of parameters, in general. Nonhomogeneous equations are the equations of forced motion: the spring with a force, and the circuit with an input.

The equation y'' + y = 2 has the homogeneous solution A cos(x) + B sin(x), and the particular solution y = 2, because the second derivative of 2 is 0, and 0 + 2 = 2: the general solution is y = A cos(x) + B sin(x) + 2. The equation y'' + y = e^x has the particular solution y = e^x/2, because the second derivative of e^x/2 is e^x/2, and e^x/2 + e^x/2 = e^x. The equation y'' + y = sin(x) has the particular solution y = -x cos(x)/2, because sin(x) is a homogeneous solution, so the guess must be multiplied by x: this is resonance, and the amplitude grows with x. A spring forced at its natural frequency resonates: the amplitude grows linearly with time, and the spring can break.

The Method of Undetermined Coefficients

The method of undetermined coefficients finds a particular solution by guessing its form: if the forcing term is a polynomial, guess a polynomial; if it is an exponential, guess an exponential; if it is a sine or cosine, guess a combination of sines and cosines. The coefficients are found by substituting the guess into the equation. If the guess is a homogeneous solution, multiply it by x: that is the resonance case, and the amplitude grows.

The equation y'' + y = 2x has the trial solution y = Ax + B: substituting gives 0 + Ax + B = 2x, so A = 2 and B = 0, and the particular solution is y = 2x. The equation y'' + y = e^x has the trial solution y = A e^x: substituting gives A e^x + A e^x = e^x, so A = 1/2, and the particular solution is y = e^x/2. The equation y'' + y = sin(x) has the trial solution y = A cos(x) + B sin(x), but that is a homogeneous solution, so multiply by x: y = Ax cos(x) + Bx sin(x), and substituting gives the particular solution y = -x cos(x)/2. The equation y'' - y = 3 has the trial solution y = A: substituting gives -A = 3, so A = -3, and the particular solution is y = -3.

Variation of Parameters

Variation of parameters finds a particular solution by letting the constants in the homogeneous solution vary: if the homogeneous solution is y = A y1 + B y2, the particular solution is y = u1(x) y1 + u2(x) y2, where u1 and u2 are functions found by solving a system. It works for any forcing term, unlike undetermined coefficients, but it is more work: it needs integration, and the integrals may be hard.

The equation y'' + y = tan(x) has the homogeneous solutions cos(x) and sin(x), with Wronskian 1. Variation of parameters gives u1' = -sin(x) tan(x) and u2' = cos(x) tan(x), so u1 = sin(x) - ln|sec(x) + tan(x)| and u2 = -cos(x), and the particular solution is y = -cos(x) ln|sec(x) + tan(x)|. The equation y'' + y = 1/x has the particular solution found by variation of parameters: the integrals are not elementary, but the method gives them. The equation y'' - y = e^x has the homogeneous solutions e^x and e^(-x): undetermined coefficients fails, because e^x is a homogeneous solution, but variation of parameters gives the particular solution y = x e^x/2.

Laplace Transforms

The Laplace transform turns a differential equation into an algebraic equation: it maps a function f(t) to F(s) = the integral from 0 to infinity of e^(-st) f(t) dt. Differentiation becomes multiplication by s, so a differential equation becomes an equation in s, which is solved by algebra, and the inverse transform gives the solution. The Laplace transform is the standard tool for initial-value problems, and for systems with a sudden input.

The Laplace transform of 1 is 1/s, and of e^(at) is 1/(s - a). The transform of y' is sY(s) - y(0), and of y'' is s^2 Y(s) - s y(0) - y'(0). The equation y' + y = 0, with y(0) = 1, transforms to sY - 1 + Y = 0, so Y = 1/(s + 1), and the inverse transform is y = e^(-t). The equation y'' + y = 0, with y(0) = 1 and y'(0) = 0, transforms to s^2 Y - s + Y = 0, so Y = s/(s^2 + 1), and the inverse transform is y = cos(t). The Heaviside step function has transform 1/s: it models a switch turned on at t = 0.

Systems of Differential Equations

A system of differential equations is a set of equations, each for the derivative of one variable, in terms of all the variables: dx/dt = f(x, y) and dy/dt = g(x, y). The system is solved as a whole: the variables evolve together, and the solution is a curve in the phase plane. Systems model the predator and the prey, the chemical reaction, and the electrical circuit: the systems where several quantities change together.

The predator-prey system dx/dt = x - xy and dy/dt = -y + xy has fixed points at (0, 0) and (1, 1): at (0, 0), both populations are zero, and at (1, 1), they are in balance. The Jacobian at (1, 1) is [[0, -1], [1, 0]], with eigenvalues i and -i: the fixed point is a centre, and the populations oscillate. The system dx/dt = -x, dy/dt = -2y has the fixed point (0, 0), with eigenvalues -1 and -2: both negative, so the fixed point is stable, and the system decays to zero. The system dx/dt = x, dy/dt = -y has the fixed point (0, 0), with eigenvalues 1 and -1: it is a saddle, stable in one direction and unstable in the other.

Phase Planes

A phase plane is the plane of the variables of a system: the solution is a curve in it, and the direction field shows the flow at every point. The phase portrait is the picture of the flow: the fixed points, the stable and unstable directions, and the closed orbits. The phase plane is the qualitative picture of a system: it shows what the system does, without solving it.

The system dx/dt = -x, dy/dt = -2y has the fixed point (0, 0), a stable node: all trajectories flow into it, along the axes. The system dx/dt = x, dy/dt = -y has the fixed point (0, 0), a saddle: trajectories approach along the y-axis and leave along the x-axis, and the axes are the separatrices. The predator-prey system has a centre at (1, 1): the trajectories are closed orbits around it, and the populations oscillate. The Van der Pol oscillator has a limit cycle: all trajectories spiral toward it, and the system settles into a periodic motion, with a fixed amplitude.

Numerical Methods for Differential Equations

Numerical methods for differential equations approximate the solution step by step: Euler's method takes a step along the tangent, and Runge-Kutta takes several steps and averages them. The error shrinks as the step shrinks, and the method is judged by its accuracy, its speed, and its stability. Numerical methods are how most differential equations are solved: the weather, the aircraft, and the bridge are all numerical solutions.

Euler's method for dy/dt = y, with y(0) = 1 and step 0.1, gives after 10 steps y = 2.5937, while the exact answer e is 2.7183: the error is 0.12. With step 0.05, after 20 steps, y = 2.6533, and the error is 0.065: halving the step halves the error, because Euler's method is first order. The fourth-order Runge-Kutta method with step 0.1 gives y = 2.7183 after 10 steps, correct to 4 decimal places: the error is proportional to h^4, so it is much smaller. The finite difference method solves the heat equation on a grid: the temperature at each grid point is updated from its neighbours, step by step.

Partial Differential Equations

A partial differential equation involves partial derivatives: it describes a function of several variables, like time and space. The heat equation, the wave equation, and Laplace's equation are the three classic partial differential equations, and they describe diffusion, waves, and steady states. Partial differential equations are the equations of fields: the temperature, the displacement, and the potential are all fields, and their equations are partial.

The heat equation u_t = u_xx, with u(0, t) = u(1, t) = 0 and u(x, 0) = sin(pi x), has the solution u = e^(-pi^2 t) sin(pi x): the sine wave decays exponentially, with rate pi^2. The wave equation u_tt = u_xx, with a string fixed at both ends, has the solution u = sin(pi x) cos(pi t): the string vibrates at frequency pi. Laplace's equation u_xx + u_yy = 0, with u = 0 on three sides of a square and u = 1 on the fourth, has a solution that is smooth inside, with the maximum on the boundary. A hot spot in a rod spreads by diffusion: its width grows as the square root of time.

The Heat Equation

The heat equation says that the rate of change of temperature equals the diffusivity times the second spatial derivative: u_t = k u_xx. It describes how heat spreads by diffusion: a hot spot spreads out, and its width grows as the square root of time. The heat equation is the model of diffusion: it describes not only heat, but the spread of a chemical, the diffusion of a population, and the smoothing of an image.

The heat equation u_t = u_xx, on a rod from 0 to 1 with ends held at 0, and initial temperature sin(pi x), has the solution u = e^(-pi^2 t) sin(pi x): at t = 0.1, the factor is e^(-0.987) = 0.373, so the temperature is 37 percent of its initial value. The initial temperature sin(2 pi x) decays as e^(-4 pi^2 t): it decays four times as fast, because the rate is proportional to n^2. A hot spot of width 1 cm in steel spreads to a width of about sqrt(2 times the diffusivity times the time): for steel, with diffusivity about 0.0001 m^2/s, it spreads to 1 cm in about 0.5 seconds. The steady state of a rod with ends at 0 and 100 degrees is the straight line u = 100x.

The Wave Equation

The wave equation says that the acceleration of a disturbance equals the wave speed squared times its curvature: u_tt = c^2 u_xx. It describes sound, light, water, and earthquakes: every wave in nature. Its solutions are travelling waves, and they are found by d'Alembert's formula: the solution is the average of the initial shape, shifted left and right. The wave equation is the most used partial differential equation in physics.

A guitar string of length 0.65 m, fixed at both ends, vibrates at frequencies that are multiples of the fundamental: the fundamental is v/(2L), where v is the wave speed. For a wave speed of 400 m/s, the fundamental is 400/(2 times 0.65) = 308 Hz, and the harmonics are 616, 924, and 1,232 Hz. The speed of sound in air is about 343 m/s: a note of 440 Hz has a wavelength of 343/440 = 0.78 m. An earthquake wave travels at about 5,000 m/s through rock: a wave from 100 km away arrives after 20 seconds. D'Alembert's formula says that a plucked string's shape splits into two waves, travelling left and right, each at half the height.

Laplace's Equation

Laplace's equation says that the sum of the second derivatives is zero: u_xx + u_yy = 0. It describes steady states: the potential in empty space, the temperature when it no longer changes, and the velocity potential of an irrotational flow. Its solutions are harmonic functions, and they have a remarkable property: the maximum is always on the boundary, never inside. Laplace's equation is the elliptic equation, and it is the equation of equilibrium.

Laplace's equation on a square, with u = 0 on three sides and u = 1 on the fourth, has a solution that is smooth inside, with values between 0 and 1, and the maximum, 1, on the fourth side, by the maximum principle. The function u = x^2 - y^2 satisfies Laplace's equation: u_xx = 2 and u_yy = -2, so their sum is 0. The function u = ln(r), where r is the distance from the origin, satisfies Laplace's equation away from the origin: it is the potential of a point charge in two dimensions. The steady-state temperature in a rod with ends at 0 and 100 degrees is the straight line u = 100x, which satisfies u_xx = 0.

Boundary Value Problems

A boundary value problem specifies a differential equation and the values on the boundary of the domain: the temperature at the ends of a rod, or the displacement at the ends of a string. The Dirichlet problem specifies the value; the Neumann problem specifies the derivative. Boundary value problems are the problems of steady states and of structures: they are solved by separation of variables, by Green's functions, or numerically.

The boundary value problem u_xx = 0 on the interval [0, 1], with u(0) = 0 and u(1) = 100, has the solution u = 100x: the straight line. The problem u_xx + u = 0, with u(0) = 0 and u(1) = 0, has the non-zero solutions u = sin(n pi x) when the eigenvalue is n^2 pi^2: the eigenvalues are pi^2, 4 pi^2, 9 pi^2, and so on. The Dirichlet problem for Laplace's equation on a square, with u = 0 on three sides and u = 1 on the fourth, has a unique solution, smooth inside. The Neumann problem for Laplace's equation has a solution only when the total flux through the boundary is zero: otherwise, the heat would accumulate.

Differential Equations in Physics

Differential equations in physics are the laws of nature: Newton's second law is a differential equation, Maxwell's equations are partial differential equations, and Schrödinger's equation is a partial differential equation. The solutions are the predictions: the orbit of a planet, the field of a charge, and the state of an atom. Physics is differential equations, and the equations are the theory.

A pendulum of length 1 metre has a period of 2pi sqrt(1/9.8) = 2.01 seconds, from the differential equation of the pendulum. Maxwell's equations predict that light travels at 3 x 10^8 m/s: the speed is 1/sqrt(epsilon_0 mu_0), where epsilon_0 and mu_0 are the constants of electricity and magnetism. Schrödinger's equation for the hydrogen atom gives the energy levels -13.6/n^2 eV: the first level is -13.6 eV, and the second is -3.4 eV. Einstein's equations predict that light bends near the Sun by 1.75 arcseconds: the prediction was confirmed in 1919, and it made Einstein famous.

Differential Equations in Biology

Differential equations in biology are the mathematics of living things: the equations of populations, epidemics, and the spread of a drug. The predator-prey system is a pair of differential equations; the SIR model is three; and the Hodgkin-Huxley equations are four. Biology is becoming a mathematical science, and the differential equation is its language. The equations are fitted to data, and the parameters are estimated from measurements, with uncertainty.

A population of bacteria doubles every 20 minutes: after 2 hours, 6 doublings, it is 2^6 = 64 times as large. The logistic equation with r = 0.5 and K = 1,000, starting from 10, grows to 500 in about 9.2 time units: the solution is y = K/(1 + A e^(-rt)), with A = 99, so y = 500 when e^(-0.5t) = 1/99, giving t = ln(99)/0.5 = 9.19. The SIR model predicts that an epidemic with R0 = 3 will infect about 94 percent of the population if nothing is done. The Hodgkin-Huxley equations describe the nerve impulse: the voltage rises by 100 mV in about 1 ms, and the impulse travels at about 100 m/s in a myelinated axon.

A History of Differential Equations

Differential equations have no single founder: they were invented by Newton and Leibniz in the 1670s, to describe motion, and they have grown ever since. The 18th century solved the equations of mechanics; the 19th solved the equations of heat and electricity; and the 20th made numerical solution routine. Today differential equations are the language of physics, engineering, and biology, and they are the most used mathematics in science.

The timeline: Newton and Leibniz, 1670s; d'Alembert's wave equation, 1747; Fourier's heat equation, 1822; Maxwell's equations, 1865; Poincaré's qualitative theory, 1890s; the first computers, 1940s; the numerical methods, 1960s. The span from Newton to the first computer is about 270 years. The subject's tools are old, but the subject itself is new: it was the computer that made numerical solution routine, and it is the computer that keeps the subject growing, through the weather, the climate, and the genome.

Differential Equations in Engineering

Differential equations in engineering are the mathematics that designs and builds: the equations of structures, fluids, circuits, and control. An engineer uses differential equations to predict whether a design will work, before it is built, and to make it better, cheaper, and safer. Engineering is differential equations with a deadline. The engineer solves the equations before building, and tests the design after, and the two must agree.

A steel beam 10 metres long, carrying a load of 1,000 N at its centre, deflects by about 1 mm, from the formula PL^3/(48EI): with P = 1,000 N, L = 10 m, E = 200 GPa, and I = 0.0001 m^4, the deflection is 0.00104 m. A control system keeps a room at 20 degrees: the thermostat measures the temperature, compares it with the target, and switches the heater on or off — that is feedback, and the system is a differential equation with a switch. An RC circuit with resistance 1,000 ohms and capacitance 0.001 F has a time constant of 1 second: the voltage decays as e^(-t), and after 1 second it is 37 percent of its initial value. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed in 1940 because the wind forced it at its natural frequency: it was resonance, and the amplitude grew until the bridge broke.
