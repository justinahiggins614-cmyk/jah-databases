Mount Vesuvius

ID: JAH-VOLNS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a side-by-side comparison of the AD 79, 1631, and 1944 eruptions as a teaching ladder, so a reader can see how one volcano produces very different kinds of eruption, and a worked evacuation-planning exercise built from the red-zone figures.

Definition: Mount Vesuvius is a stratovolcano in Campania, Italy, rising about 1,281 metres above the Bay of Naples. It is one of the few active volcanoes on the mainland of Europe and the only one on mainland Europe to have erupted in the last hundred years. A volcano is an opening in the Earth's crust where molten rock, ash, and gas escape, and Vesuvius is famous because its eruption in AD 79 buried the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum. Today the volcano is quiet, but it is closely watched, because about three million people live in the area around it.

History: Vesuvius was known to the Romans as a fertile mountain with vineyards on its slopes, and few people in AD 79 understood that it was a volcano at all. On 24 August AD 79, or possibly in October of that year (the exact date is disputed among scholars, because the surviving letter describing it gives a date that may have been miscopied), the mountain erupted with enormous force. A column of ash rose kilometres into the sky, and pyroclastic surges of hot gas and rock swept down the slopes, burying Pompeii under metres of ash and Herculaneum under volcanic mud and rock. The Roman writer Pliny the Younger watched from across the bay and described the cloud as shaped like a pine tree; his uncle, Pliny the Elder, sailed toward the eruption to help and died there. After AD 79 the volcano erupted many times, including a destructive eruption in 1631 that killed thousands, and eruptions in 1906 and 1944. The 1944 eruption, the most recent, destroyed two villages and damaged Allied aircraft at a nearby airfield during the Second World War. Since 1944 Vesuvius has been quiet, but the Vesuvius Observatory, founded in 1841 and the oldest volcanological observatory in the world, monitors it continuously.

Key concepts: A stratovolcano, also called a composite volcano, is built up over many eruptions from alternating layers of lava, ash, and rock, and it typically has steep sides and a cone shape. A Plinian eruption is a very powerful explosive eruption named after Pliny the Younger; it throws a tall column of ash and gas high into the atmosphere. A pyroclastic flow is a fast-moving current of hot gas and volcanic rock that races down a volcano's slopes and destroys almost everything in its path; a pyroclastic surge is a thinner, more dilute version of the same thing. Tephra is the general name for rock fragments thrown out by an eruption, from fine ash to large blocks. A magma chamber is a pool of molten rock beneath the volcano that feeds eruptions. Vesuvius sits inside the older, partly destroyed cone of Monte Somma, so the pair is often called the Somma-Vesuvius complex.

How it works: Vesuvius exists because of plate tectonics. The African tectonic plate is slowly sliding, or subducting, beneath the Eurasian plate under Italy, and as the sinking plate heats up, water and other materials are driven out of it and lower the melting point of the rock above. Molten rock, or magma, forms and rises through the crust, collecting in a chamber a few kilometres below the surface. The magma under Vesuvius is rich in silica, which makes it thick and sticky, and it holds a large amount of dissolved gas. As more magma arrives, pressure builds. When the pressure becomes too great, the gas bubbles out of the magma suddenly, fragmenting it into ash and rock, and the eruption begins. Because the magma is thick, it does not flow easily as lava; instead it explodes, producing the ash columns and pyroclastic flows that make Vesuvius dangerous. Between eruptions the chamber slowly refills, which is why long quiet periods can end in large eruptions.

Worked examples: The AD 79 eruption is the classic example of a Plinian eruption: the ash column rose an estimated 30 kilometres or more, ash fell as far away as North Africa, and the cities at the foot of the volcano were buried so completely that they were forgotten for more than a thousand years. The 1631 eruption shows the other face of Vesuvius: after centuries of quiet, it erupted violently, sending lava flows and mudflows down its slopes and killing an estimated 3,000 to 6,000 people. The 1906 eruption threw out so much ash that the roof of a market in Naples collapsed, killing more than a hundred people. The 1944 eruption was smaller but still destroyed the villages of San Sebastiano al Vesuvio and Massa di Somma and dropped ash on the town of Terzigno, while lava fountains lit the night sky. Each of these eruptions followed a different pattern, which is why volcanologists study all of them.

Real applications: Vesuvius matters first as a lesson in volcanic hazard. Because millions of people live within a few kilometres of the crater, Italy has drawn up detailed evacuation plans, including a "red zone" of about 600,000 to 700,000 people who would be moved first if the volcano showed signs of a large eruption, and scientists practise watching for the small earthquakes and ground swelling that often precede eruptions. Second, the volcano is a laboratory for volcanology: the observatory on its slopes has been measuring it since 1841, and its long record helps scientists understand how stratovolcanoes behave. Third, the AD 79 eruption preserved Pompeii and Herculaneum as extraordinary archaeological sites, giving historians a detailed picture of Roman daily life. Finally, the rich volcanic soil around the volcano supports vineyards and farms, and the volcano itself draws visitors from around the world.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is Vesuvius extinct? No. It is dormant, meaning it is not erupting now but is expected to erupt again; the last eruption was in 1944, which is a short pause by volcanic standards. Will it erupt again soon? Nobody knows when; scientists watch for warning signs such as earthquakes and ground deformation, and a large eruption would probably be preceded by weeks or months of unrest. Why do people live so close to a dangerous volcano? The soil is fertile, the location is historic, and Naples is a major city that grew up beside the mountain long before modern volcanology existed. Was Pompeii destroyed by lava? No. Pompeii was buried mainly by falling ash and then by pyroclastic surges of hot gas and fine rock; Herculaneum was buried by hotter, denser flows. Is Vesuvius the most dangerous volcano in the world? It is often described as one of the most dangerous because of the number of people living close to it, but danger depends on both the volcano and the people exposed.

Mount Etna

ID: JAH-VOLNS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a lava-flow diversion case study comparing the 1669 and 1991-1993 eruptions, plus a simple model a reader can use to estimate how far a slow lava flow travels in a day.

Definition: Mount Etna is an active stratovolcano on the east coast of Sicily, Italy, and at about 3,357 metres it is the highest active volcano in Europe. Its height changes slightly after each eruption as lava and ash add to the summit, so published heights differ by a few metres from year to year. Etna is one of the most active volcanoes in the world: it erupts frequently, often several times in a single year, producing lava flows, ash falls, and spectacular fountains of molten rock. Unlike Vesuvius, whose eruptions are usually explosive, Etna's eruptions are often effusive, meaning lava flows out relatively gently, though it can also explode. About a million people live on its fertile slopes.

History: Etna has been erupting for a very long time: geologists date its activity back around 500,000 years, and the present cone has been growing for roughly the last 35,000 years. Greek and Roman writers described it as a place of fire and myth. The poet Hesiod and the playwright Aeschylus wrote about it, and the Roman poet Virgil described its eruptions; the Greeks said the monster Typhon was trapped beneath it, and the forge of the god Hephaestus was placed there. A great eruption in 1669 sent lava flows that reached the sea at Catania, destroying part of the city and killing an estimated 10,000 to 20,000 people, though the death toll is uncertain. Eruptions in the 19th and 20th centuries repeatedly threatened villages on the slopes, and in 1991-1993 a long eruption was diverted by engineers who built earth barriers and even used explosives to redirect lava away from the town of Zafferana Etnea. In 2013 UNESCO added Mount Etna to the World Heritage List. Etna continues to erupt regularly; notable recent activity includes powerful paroxysms in 2021 and ash emissions in 2023 and 2024.

Key concepts: An effusive eruption is one in which lava flows out of the volcano rather than exploding; Etna's frequent effusive eruptions are the reason its slopes are covered in dark lava fields. A paroxysm is a short, intense burst of activity in which lava fountains shoot hundreds of metres into the air, often lasting only hours. Lava is molten rock on the surface, and it cools into two common forms: smooth, ropy pahoehoe and rough, jagged aa, both of which are found on Etna. A flank eruption happens when lava breaks out of the side of the volcano rather than from the summit crater, which is common on Etna and is what threatened Catania in 1669. A summit crater is the opening at the top of the volcano; Etna has four, and their number and shape change with eruptions. Volcanic ash is fine rock fragments that can drift on the wind, close airports, and damage crops.

How it works: Etna sits near the boundary where the African plate meets the Eurasian plate, but its exact cause is debated among geologists: it is not a simple subduction volcano like Vesuvius, and many scientists think it is fed by a deep hot spot or by a tear in the subducting plate that lets mantle material rise. Whatever the deep cause, magma rises through a network of cracks and chambers beneath the mountain and reaches the surface at the summit craters or through fissures on the flanks. Etna's magma is less rich in silica than Vesuvius's, so it is runnier and its gas escapes more easily, which is why its eruptions are often flows of lava rather than explosions. The volcano grows because each eruption adds new lava and ash to the cone, and it also spreads sideways under its own weight, which opens new cracks on its flanks. Instruments on the mountain measure tiny earthquakes, ground swelling, and gas release, and these signals usually increase in the days before an eruption.

Worked examples: The 1669 eruption is the most destructive in Etna's recorded history: lava poured from a fissure on the south flank, flowed for weeks, buried villages, and reached the sea at Catania, where it built new land into the harbour; the city walls were partly overrun. The 1991-1993 eruption lasted 473 days, the longest of the 20th century on Etna, and threatened Zafferana Etnea until engineers diverted the flow. In February 2021 a series of paroxysms sent lava fountains about 1,000 metres high and dropped ash on Catania, closing its airport. The 2001 and 2002-2003 flank eruptions opened fissures on the south and north-east sides and damaged tourist facilities near the summit. Smaller examples happen every year: a brief summit eruption may last a night, cover the snow with black ash, and close the airport for a day, then stop.

Real applications: Etna is a natural laboratory for volcanology because it erupts so often: scientists can test instruments and theories against real eruptions many times in a career, which is rare for a large volcano. Its monitoring system, run by Italy's National Institute of Geophysics and Volcanology, is among the most advanced in the world, and the techniques developed there are used on volcanoes elsewhere. The volcano's slopes are intensively farmed — wine, pistachios, citrus, and chestnuts grow in the mineral-rich volcanic soil — and the area's agriculture depends on the very eruptions that threaten it. Etna is also a major tourist destination, with cable cars, guided walks, and ski slopes, and its UNESCO listing recognises both its geology and its cultural history. Finally, engineers have learned from Etna how to divert lava flows with barriers and channels, a skill that may save towns elsewhere.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is Etna the most active volcano in the world? It is among the most active large volcanoes, erupting many times per decade, but smaller volcanoes such as Stromboli and Kilauea erupt more continuously. Why is Etna less deadly than Vesuvius? Its runny lava usually moves slowly enough for people to escape, and its eruptions are usually smaller and less explosive; the danger comes mainly from flank eruptions near towns and from ash. Does Etna's height keep changing? Yes, by a few metres: each eruption adds material to the summit, and collapses or explosions can lower it, so different sources give slightly different heights. Can eruptions be stopped? No, but lava flows can sometimes be diverted with earth barriers, as in 1991-1993. Is it safe to visit? The summit area is often closed during eruptions and can be dangerous because of gas and falling rock; guided tours operate when conditions allow, and visitors are expected to follow official advice.

Krakatoa

ID: JAH-VOLNS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a timeline exercise that places the 1883 eruption, the birth of Anak Krakatau, and the 2018 collapse on one scale, plus a comparison of Krakatoa's VEI 6 with Tambora's VEI 7 so readers can feel the size of the scale.

Definition: Krakatoa, also spelled Krakatau, is a volcanic island in the Sunda Strait between the Indonesian islands of Java and Sumatra. It is famous for the eruption of 27 August 1883, one of the most violent volcanic events in recorded history, which destroyed most of the island, killed more than 36,000 people, and was heard thousands of kilometres away. The 1883 eruption was so powerful that it threw ash high into the atmosphere and cooled the climate around the world for several years. The volcano did not disappear: in 1927 a new island, Anak Krakatau, meaning "child of Krakatoa", began to rise from the sea in the same place, and it remains active today.

History: Krakatoa had erupted before 1883, with records of activity in the 17th and 18th centuries, but by the early 1880s it had been quiet for about two centuries. In May 1883 it woke up with steam and ash eruptions, and over the summer the activity grew. On the morning of 27 August 1883 the volcano exploded in a series of blasts, the largest at about 10:02 local time; it is often described as the loudest sound in modern history, heard in Mauritius, about 4,800 kilometres away, and on Rodrigues Island, about 4,800 kilometres in the other direction. The eruption destroyed about two-thirds of the island, threw an estimated 20 cubic kilometres of rock into the air, and triggered tsunamis up to about 35 metres high that struck the coasts of Java and Sumatra, causing most of the deaths. Fine ash and sulfur in the atmosphere spread around the globe, producing vivid red sunsets for years and lowering average temperatures by roughly 1 degree Celsius. In 1927 eruptions began again at the same spot, and Anak Krakatau grew out of the sea. It erupted repeatedly through the 20th and 21st centuries, and on 22 December 2018 part of its flank collapsed into the sea, causing a tsunami that killed more than 400 people.

Key concepts: A caldera is a large bowl-shaped hollow left when a volcano collapses into its own emptied magma chamber; the 1883 eruption left a caldera under the sea where the island had been. A tsunami is a series of large sea waves caused by a sudden displacement of water, in this case by the collapse of the volcano and the eruption itself. A volcanic winter is a period of global cooling caused by sulfur gases and fine ash from a very large eruption reflecting sunlight; Krakatoa's eruption is a classic example. The Volcanic Explosivity Index, or VEI, is a scale from 0 to 8 that rates eruptions by the volume of material thrown out; the 1883 Krakatoa eruption is rated VEI 6. A pyroclastic flow, a fast current of hot gas and rock, swept across the sea surface during the 1883 eruption and is thought to have reached nearby islands. An island arc is a chain of volcanoes formed where one tectonic plate slides beneath another; Krakatoa is part of the Indonesian island arc.

How it works: Krakatoa sits above the Sunda subduction zone, where the Indo-Australian plate slides beneath the Eurasian plate. As the sinking plate descends, water is driven out of it, which lowers the melting point of the rock above and creates magma. The magma collects in a chamber beneath the volcano; because it is rich in silica, it is thick and traps gas, so pressure builds until the volcano explodes. In 1883 the eruption was so violent because a huge volume of gas-rich magma was released almost at once, and sea water pouring into the hot crater may have added to the explosions, though scientists still debate how much of the blast was caused by magma meeting seawater. The collapse of the island into the emptied chamber displaced the sea and made the tsunamis. Anak Krakatau grows by the same process: magma rises at the same spot, erupts on the sea floor, and piles up new rock until it breaks the surface.

Worked examples: The sound of the 27 August 1883 explosion was heard across the Indian Ocean, and barometers around the world recorded the pressure wave circling the globe several times. The tsunamis that followed destroyed more than 160 villages on the coasts of Java and Sumatra and threw a steamship, the Berouw, nearly two kilometres inland. Ships reported pumice rafts, floating masses of volcanic rock, drifting for months afterwards. The global effects included red sunsets so vivid that painters recorded them — the artist Edvard Munch may have been influenced by the blood-red skies of 1883 when he later painted "The Scream", though this is a popular suggestion rather than a proven fact — and a drop in temperatures that produced cold summers in the years that followed. Anak Krakatau's growth is itself an example: from nothing in 1927 it rose to more than 300 metres high before the 2018 collapse, which cut its height roughly in half.

Real applications: Krakatoa is the textbook case for studying very large explosive eruptions and their global effects, and it is used to calibrate models of how volcanic sulfur cools the climate. It also taught the world about volcanic tsunamis: before 1883, few people understood that a volcano could cause a sea wave, and the disaster led to the first systematic study of tsunamis. Today Anak Krakatau is monitored closely by Indonesian scientists because it sits in one of the world's busiest shipping straits and near densely populated coasts; the 2018 flank collapse showed that even a small eruption can be deadly if part of the volcano slides into the sea. The volcano also supports research into how new land is colonised by plants and animals, since Anak Krakatau rose from the sea as bare rock and scientists have watched life arrive there over decades.

Common questions and misconceptions: Was Krakatoa the loudest sound ever? It is usually described as the loudest sound in recorded history, estimated at around 310 decibels near the source, though that figure is an estimate made from barometer readings, not a direct measurement. Did the eruption really cool the whole world? Yes, measurably: average global temperatures fell by roughly 1 degree Celsius for a few years, and weather records show cold summers and crop failures in some regions. Is Krakatoa gone? The original island is mostly gone, but Anak Krakatau, its child, grows in the same place and is one of Indonesia's most watched volcanoes. Could it erupt like 1883 again? A repeat on the same scale is possible in principle but is not expected soon; Anak Krakatau is far smaller than the 1883 volcano, and large eruptions need centuries of magma to accumulate. Why do people still live nearby? The coasts of Java and Sumatra are among the most densely populated places on Earth, and the fertile volcanic soil and fishing grounds draw people despite the risk.

Mount St. Helens

ID: JAH-VOLNS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a before-and-after comparison exercise using the height and blast-zone figures, and a short study of how the 1980 monitoring record became the template for modern eruption forecasting.

Definition: Mount St. Helens is an active stratovolcano in the Cascade Range of Washington State, in the north-western United States. Before 1980 it was a graceful, snow-capped cone about 2,950 metres high, often called the Mount Fuji of America; the eruption of 18 May 1980 blew off its top, leaving a horseshoe-shaped crater and a mountain 2,549 metres high. The 1980 eruption was the most destructive volcanic event in the history of the United States: it killed 57 people, flattened about 600 square kilometres of forest, and threw ash across much of the country. Since then the volcano has been slowly rebuilding a lava dome inside its crater, and it remains one of the most closely studied volcanoes in the world.

History: Mount St. Helens is a young volcano, about 40,000 years old, and Native American peoples of the region knew it as Lawetlat'la and told stories of its eruptions. European settlers recorded eruptions in the 19th century, including activity in 1800-1801 and 1831-1857, and then the mountain was quiet for more than a century. In March 1980 earthquakes began under the mountain, and a bulge grew on its north flank at a rate of about 1.5 metres per day as magma pushed in. On 18 May 1980 a magnitude 5.1 earthquake shook the mountain, the bulge collapsed in the largest landslide in recorded history, and the sudden release of pressure let the magma explode sideways in a lateral blast that flattened forests up to 30 kilometres away. Ash rose about 19 kilometres into the sky and fell as far as Montana. Fifty-seven people died, including the volcanologist David Johnston, who was watching from an observation post, and the elderly lodge owner Harry Truman, who refused to leave. The volcano erupted again in 2004-2008, slowly building a new lava dome, and scientists continue to watch it closely.

Key concepts: A lateral blast is an eruption that explodes sideways rather than upward, caused when a landslide removes the side of a volcano and suddenly releases the pressure on gas-rich magma; the 1980 blast was the first to be studied in detail. A debris avalanche is a huge landslide of rock and debris, and the 1980 avalanche, about 2.8 cubic kilometres of material, was the largest in recorded history. A lava dome is a mound of thick, slow-moving lava that piles up around a vent instead of flowing away; new domes have been growing inside the crater since 1980. A lahar is a destructive mudflow of volcanic ash, rock, and water that races down river valleys; lahars from the 1980 eruption buried roads and bridges. A blast zone is the area devastated by the sideways explosion, about 600 square kilometres in 1980. A volcanic crater is the bowl-shaped opening at the top of a volcano; the 1980 eruption left a crater about 2 kilometres wide.

How it works: Mount St. Helens sits above the Cascadia subduction zone, where the small Juan de Fuca plate slides beneath the North American plate. As the plate sinks, water is released, the rock above melts, and magma rises into a chamber beneath the volcano. In the months before May 1980, magma pushed into the mountain and deformed its north flank into a bulge. The earthquake on 18 May triggered the landslide, and with the weight of the flank suddenly gone, the pressurised magma exploded outward in a blast that moved at hundreds of kilometres per hour and flattened trees like matchsticks. The blast was followed by a vertical column of ash and by pyroclastic flows that poured into the valleys. Because the eruption removed the top of the mountain, the volcano's shape changed completely, and the crater it left became a natural laboratory: scientists have watched new domes grow, plants and animals return, and the mountain slowly rebuild itself.

Worked examples: The lateral blast is the defining example of the 1980 eruption: it swept over ridges and valleys for about 30 kilometres, knocked down some 4.7 billion board feet of timber, and left a grey landscape of bare trunks pointing away from the mountain. Spirit Lake, a popular holiday lake north of the volcano, was buried under the landslide debris and its water was thrown up onto the hillside; the lake is still full of floating logs today. Ash from the eruption fell on Yakima, Washington, turning day into night at midday, and fine ash reached the east coast of the United States within days. The 2004-2008 eruption was a quiet contrast: no explosion, just a slow extrusion of lava that built a dome inside the crater, giving scientists a chance to watch a dome grow in real time. The recovery of the land is another example: within a few years, plants and animals began returning to the blast zone, and ecologists have studied that recovery ever since.

Real applications: Mount St. Helens changed volcanology. The monitoring that preceded the eruption — earthquakes, ground swelling, gas measurements — proved that eruptions can be forecast, and the techniques developed there are now used around the world; the United States Geological Survey's Cascades Volcano Observatory was founded because of it. The eruption also changed how governments handle volcanic risk: evacuation zones, hazard maps, and public warnings are now standard practice. For ecologists, the blast zone is a rare controlled experiment in how life returns to a devastated landscape, and the Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument, established in 1982, protects the area for research and for visitors. The volcano is also a teaching case: almost every school textbook on volcanoes uses the 1980 eruption to explain lateral blasts, and the story of David Johnston, who radioed "Vancouver! Vancouver! This is it!" moments before he died, is part of the science's history.

Common questions and misconceptions: Was the 1980 eruption predicted? In part, yes. Scientists knew an eruption was coming and had set up an exclusion zone, which saved many lives, but the sideways blast was larger than expected and reached beyond the zone, which is why 57 people died. Is the volcano still active? Yes. It erupted from 2004 to 2008, and small earthquakes continue; scientists expect it to erupt again, though they cannot say when. Will it rebuild its cone? Probably, over centuries: new lava domes are already growing inside the crater, and if eruptions continue the mountain may regain a cone shape. Why did so many trees survive as bare trunks? The blast snapped trees near the volcano but only scorched and stripped them farther away, leaving a forest of grey trunks that slowly rotted. Can I visit? Yes. The Johnston Ridge Observatory and other viewpoints are open to the public, though the crater itself is closed to climbing.

Definition: Mount Pinatubo is a volcano on the island of Luzon in the Philippines, about 90 kilometres north-west of Manila. The eruption threw about 20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide into the atmosphere, cooled the planet by about half a degree Celsius, and forced the evacuation of tens of thousands of people. Pinatubo is famous not only for its size but because it was the first large eruption to be successfully forecast: scientists warned of it in time, and the evacuation saved thousands of lives.

History: Pinatubo had been quiet for about 500 years before 1991, and it was so heavily forested that few people thought of it as a volcano at all. In March 1991 small earthquakes began under the mountain, and in April a small steam eruption opened vents on its side. Scientists from the Philippine Institute of Volcanology and Seismology and the United States Geological Survey set up monitoring stations and studied the deposits of old eruptions, which showed that Pinatubo had produced very large eruptions in the past. Definition: Stromboli is a small volcanic island in the Aeolian Islands, north of Sicily, Italy, and it is one of the most continuously active volcanoes on Earth. Definition: The Yellowstone Caldera is a vast volcanic crater in Yellowstone National Park, in Wyoming in the western United States, and it is the largest active volcanic system in North America. Definition: Lake Toba is a great lake on the island of Sumatra in Indonesia, about 100 kilometres long and 30 kilometres wide, and it fills the caldera left by the largest volcanic eruption of the last 25 million years. About 74,000 years ago, the Toba volcano erupted with a force that threw out an estimated 2,800 cubic kilometres of rock and ash, covered much of South-East Asia in ash, and may have cooled the climate of the whole planet for years. In the middle of the lake stands Samosir Island, a resurgent dome of rock pushed up by magma beneath the caldera. The lake is the largest volcanic lake in the world, and the Batak people who live around it have a rich culture of villages, houses, and music.

History: Toba's great eruption happened about 74,000 years ago, during the last Ice Age, when early humans were already living in Africa and Asia. The eruption is dated by ash layers found across Asia and by ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica, which show a spike in sulfur from the same period. The ash fell across South-East Asia and as far away as India and the Arabian Sea, and the sulfur in the atmosphere is thought to have caused a volcanic winter that lasted for years. In the 1990s, scientists proposed the "Toba catastrophe theory": that the eruption caused a population bottleneck in early humans, reducing the human population to a few thousand people. That theory is debated: some genetic evidence supports a bottleneck around that time, but other evidence suggests the human population did not fall so sharply, and the debate continues. Toba has erupted on a smaller scale since then, most recently about 1,200 years ago, and the caldera is still active: the ground beneath it moves, and hot springs bubble around the lake. In 2020 UNESCO recognised the Toba Caldera as a Global Geopark.

Key concepts: A caldera is a large hollow left when a volcano collapses into its emptied chamber; Toba's caldera is about 100 kilometres long and 30 kilometres wide, one of the largest on Earth.
