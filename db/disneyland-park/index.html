Main Street, U.S.A. Rank 1 · simple · land · beginner

Main Street, U.S.A. is Disneyland's entrance land, a turn-of-the-century American small-town street that runs from the train station to the central hub, and it sets the park's scale and mood before any ride. Its buildings are shops, a cinema, a fire station, and food counters dressed in Victorian and Edwardian detail, with upper-story windows carrying the names of the park's early designers as fictional businesses, a quiet tribute guests can read from the street. The street's vehicles, a horse-drawn streetcar, a fire engine, and an omnibus, carry guests slowly toward the castle, and its music, ragtime and barbershop, follows them. Main Street is also the park's parade route and its fireworks viewing street, which means it fills, empties, and fills again across the day, and its covered arcades give a rainy-day path beside the main walk. No ride defines Main Street; the street itself is the attraction, a place to arrive, to shop, to eat, and to leave by, waving at the castle.

Adventureland Rank 2 · simple · land · beginner

Adventureland is Disneyland's jungle land, entered through a carved gateway beside the hub, where dense planting, drums, and torchlight replace Main Street's order with the look and sound of distant rivers and temples. Its rides carry guests into that world: a jungle river cruise past animatronic animals and ruins, a tiki-room show of singing birds and flowers, and an archaeology adventure through a temple of traps and chambers, each built to the same dense, green standard. The land's food follows its story, with tropical flavors and open-air counters, and its shops sell explorer gear and curios. Adventureland's hidden character is its layering: paths fork into shaded courts, a treehouse once climbed above the paths, and the jungle's edge hides the neighboring lands completely, so the land feels larger and wilder than its acreage. It is the park's most atmospheric land after dark, when torches and low light turn its planting into silhouette.

Lands Areas

Frontierland Rank 3 · simple · land · beginner

Frontierland is Disneyland's American West land, a timber-and-stone district of a mining mountain, a river, and a fort, built around the park's largest thrill mountain. Its mountain coaster races through a runaway mine, its riverboats circle the Rivers of America, and its shooting gallery and crafts shops carry the frontier story at walking pace. The land's character comes from its materials: rough timber, stone, and desert planting, with a desert town's main street that feels worked-in rather than new. Frontierland also holds the park's river crossing, where rafts carry guests to Tom Sawyer Island, a wooded island of caves, bridges, and forts that is itself a record in this database. At night the mountain's silhouette and the river's reflections give Frontierland the park's most romantic skyline, and its food counters serve hearty frontier fare to match.

Fantasyland Rank 4 · simple · land · beginner

Fantasyland is Disneyland's storybook land, the district behind Sleeping Beauty Castle where fairy tales become rides, and it is the park's most visited land for families with young children. Its rides carry guests through classic tales in dark-ride cars, on a spinning teacup, on a carousel of painted horses, and on a gentle canal boat past miniature storybook scenes, while its castle walkthrough tells its own tale in dioramas above the drawbridge. The land's architecture is storybook by design: crooked roofs, bright color, and a village square that reads as a fairytale town, with a theatre for stage shows and a small train for the youngest riders. Fantasyland's queues are its challenge, because its rides are short and beloved, and its mornings belong to families who arrive at opening for the dark rides before the crowds build.

Tomorrowland Rank 5 · simple · land · beginner

Tomorrowland is Disneyland's future land, a district of smooth white forms, rockets, and starfields that has been rebuilt more often than any other land, because the future keeps arriving and dating it. Its current anchors are its indoor space coaster, its launched space ride, its astro-orbiter spinner, and its submarine voyage through a lagoon, alongside a theatre for 3-D shows and a monorail station that carries guests above the park. Tomorrowland's history is written in its replacements: a people-moving transit system once circled the land, a rocket-jet spinner once crowned its entrance, and a carousel theatre once carried a show about progress through the decades. The land's character today is retro-future, a 1950s vision of tomorrow kept alive beside modern space rides, and its nighttime lighting, cool blue and white, makes it the park's most futuristic district after dark.

New Orleans Square Rank 6 · compounded · land · intermediate

New Orleans Square is Disneyland's smallest and most detailed land, a French Quarter district of wrought iron, gas lamps, courtyards, and jazz, opened in 1966 as the first new land added after the park's opening. Its two anchor rides are among the park's most beloved: a pirate boat voyage through caverns and a battle, and a haunted house tour through a mansion of ghosts, both dark rides that reward repeat visits with details missed the first time. The land's food and shops follow its story, with French Quarter flavors and a famous beignet counter, and its courtyards and balconies give it the park's most adult, evening character. New Orleans Square's compact scale is its strength: every balcony, lamp, and sign is placed, and its train station, the only one inside a land rather than at the entrance, lets guests arrive by rail directly into its streets.

Critter Country Rank 7 · compounded · land · intermediate

Critter Country is Disneyland's smallest land, a wooded bayou district at the park's northern edge, originally opened as Bear Country in 1972 and renamed as its animal characters changed. Its anchor is its log-flume mountain, a long boat ride through a briar-patch story that ends in a steep drop into a splash pool, and its smaller rides are a gentle spinning ride and a canoe voyage on the Rivers of America. The land's character is woodland: timber buildings, shaded paths, and a quiet, creek-side feeling that contrasts with the busier lands beside it, and its food counters serve bayou and woodland flavors. Critter Country's scale makes it a half-day land for most guests, visited for the flume and the canoes and then left for the larger lands, but its shaded paths make it one of the park's best hot-afternoon refuges.

Mickey's Toontown Rank 8 · compounded · land · intermediate

Mickey's Toontown, opened in 1993, is Disneyland's cartoon land, a district built as the town where cartoon characters live, with houses guests can enter, a downtown of bent and bright buildings, and a gag-filled main street. Its rides are family-scaled: a cartoon coaster through a gag factory, a gentle car ride through a cartoon countryside, and a trolley tour of the town, while its houses, Mickey's and Minnie's among them, are walk-through attractions in their own right. Toontown's character is its interactivity: doors honk, mailboxes talk, and sidewalks carry cartoon jokes, so the land rewards guests who touch, open, and explore rather than only queue and ride. Its 2023 rebuild added a green park and a new family ride, keeping the cartoon town's spirit while giving it the shade and seating it previously lacked.

Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge Rank 9 · compounded · land · intermediate

Star Wars: Galaxy's Edge, opened in 2019, is Disneyland's largest single-land expansion, a fourteen-acre district built as the planet Batuu's Black Spire Outpost, a remote trading port on the edge of the galaxy. Its two anchor rides are a falcon-piloting simulator in which guests crew a starship, and a dark ride through a star destroyer in which guests join a resistance escape, both among the most technically ambitious rides in any Disney park. The land's character is its immersion: no Disney signage, no familiar park music, and a marketplace of original shops, food, and a cantina, all built to the outpost's story, with a full-size falcon as its centerpiece. Galaxy's Edge rewards exploration over queuing: its alleys, its droid workshop, and its lightsaber workshop are experiences in themselves, and its evenings, lit by the outpost's lamps, are its most atmospheric hours.

Tom Sawyer Island and Rivers of America Rank 10 · compounded · land · intermediate

Tom Sawyer Island and the Rivers of America form Disneyland's river heart: a wooded island reached only by raft, circled by riverboats and a sailing ship, and built as a place for children to explore rather than to queue. Mine Train Through Nature's Wonderland (past) Rank 11 · complex · past · advanced

Mine Train Through Nature's Wonderland ran in Frontierland from 1960 to 1977, a slow train ride through a miniature wilderness of desert, forest, and waterfalls, with animatronic animals, geysers, and a rainbow cavern of colored pools. Its trains wound past beaver dams, bear dens, and a living desert of cactus and rock, and its narration taught guests the names of the landscapes they passed, making it as much a nature lesson as a ride. The ride's scale was its charm: a full wilderness in miniature, built to be watched slowly from an open train, and its rainbow caverns, glowing pools of colored water, remain among the most fondly remembered scenes in Disneyland history. It closed in 1977 to make way for the runaway-mine coaster that now occupies its mountain, and its wilderness theme lives on in the frontier land's planting and in the name of the rainbow ridge district that followed it.

PeopleMover (past) Rank 12 · complex · past · advanced

The PeopleMover ran in Tomorrowland from 1967 to 1995, a slow elevated transit ride that carried guests around the land on a continuously moving track, past ride buildings, through show scenes, and above the streets, at walking pace and without a queue in the usual sense. Its charm was its viewpoint: guests boarded a moving walkway onto open cars and saw Tomorrowland from above, gliding through the carousel theatre building, past the space coaster's exterior, and over the submarine lagoon, with a narration that explained the land below. The PeopleMover embodied a 1960s idea of the future, that cities would move people on quiet elevated transit rather than in cars, and its closure in 1995, after years of declining reliability, left its elevated track standing for years as a visible memory. Its successor, a faster rocket ride on the same track, ran briefly and closed, and the track's fate remains a subject of guest speculation.

Skyway (past) Rank 13 · complex · past · advanced

The Skyway ran at Disneyland from 1956 to 1994, a gondola lift that carried guests between Fantasyland and Tomorrowland in open buckets, high above the park, with views of the castle, the mountain, and the lands below. Its two stations, a storybook chalet in Fantasyland and a futuristic terminal in Tomorrowland, were themselves themed buildings, and its buckets, four-seat gondolas with no doors, gave the park's best aerial view for nearly forty years. The Skyway's charm was its slowness: a gentle crossing above the rooftops, with the park spread below like a map, and its closure in 1994, driven by maintenance cost and by new safety standards for open gondolas, removed the park's only aerial transit. Its Fantasyland station building stood for years afterwards, and its Tomorrowland station's footprint remains legible to guests who know where to look, though neither is a guest attraction today.

Carousel of Progress (past, at Disneyland) Rank 14 · complex · past · advanced

The Carousel of Progress played at Disneyland from 1967 to 1973, a rotating theatre show that carried its audience around a central stage through four scenes of American home life, from the age of gas lamps to a then-futuristic world of automated kitchens. Its song about a great big beautiful tomorrow became one of the park's most quoted tunes, and its structure, a theatre that moved while its stage stood still, was itself a World's Fair innovation, brought from New York after the 1964 fair. The show's idea was progress as a domestic story: each scene showed the same family with new appliances, new conveniences, and the same jokes about the future arriving faster than expected. It closed at Disneyland in 1973 and moved to Florida, where it still plays at Magic Kingdom, so its Disneyland past is also a Florida present, and its theatre building in Tomorrowland later housed other shows.

America Sings (past) Rank 15 · complex · past · advanced

America Sings played in Tomorrowland's carousel theatre from 1974 to 1988, a rotating-theatre show of animatronic animals singing American popular songs from different eras, from the age of the pioneers to the age of rock and roll. Its structure reused the Carousel of Progress theatre, with the audience rotating around a central stage while animal figures, geese, frogs, and foxes among them, performed songs matched to each era's music. The show's charm was its casting: animals as singers gave every era a comic, gentle treatment, and its finale gathered the whole cast for a patriotic medley. It closed in 1988, and many of its figures were reused in the park's new critter-country flume, where guests can still see some of the same animal performers in a new story, a rare case of a closed show living on inside a ride.

Rainbow Ridge and Mine Train Area (past) Rank 16 · complex · past · advanced

Rainbow Ridge was the colorful mining-town district that grew around Mine Train Through Nature's Wonderland in Frontierland, a cluster of false-front buildings, a rainbow-colored ridge, and a cavern entrance that gave the wilderness train its most photographed backdrop. Its buildings were scaled to the train, small and bright, with a general store, a saloon, and a mine office that read as a frontier town glimpsed from a passing train, and its rainbow caverns, glowing pools beneath the ridge, were the ride's finale. When the mine train closed in 1977, Rainbow Ridge lost its reason, and its buildings were gradually removed or absorbed into the runaway-mine coaster's mountain, though its name survived in the rainbow-colored rockwork that still edges the frontier land. Rainbow Ridge matters as a lesson in themed design: a district built for a ride's viewpoint, not for walking, and therefore fragile when the ride closed, unlike the walkable lands that survived around it.
