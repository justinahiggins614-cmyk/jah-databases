WHO Surgical Safety Checklist

The World Health Organization checklist has three pauses: before anesthesia, before incision, and before leaving the OR. The team confirms identity, site, antibiotics, and counts aloud. Hospitals using it cut surgical deaths measurably.

The Time-Out Ritual

Right before the first cut, everything stops: the surgeon states the patient's name, the procedure, and the site, and the team agrees. This single pause prevents wrong-site surgery. It is required by accreditation everywhere.

Sponge and Instrument Counts

Scrub nurses count every sponge, needle, and instrument before the case and again before closure. Counts must match exactly — a missing sponge means an X-ray before the patient leaves the room. Retained objects are a 'never event.'

General Anesthesia

General anesthesia renders the patient unconscious, pain-free, and still using IV drugs and inhaled gases. The anesthesiologist manages the airway, breathing, and blood pressure throughout. Waking happens in PACU as the drugs wear off.

Regional Anesthesia

Spinals, epidurals, and nerve blocks numb only the surgical region while the patient stays awake or lightly sedated. Recovery is faster with less nausea and less opioid need. Hips, knees, and C-sections commonly use regional techniques.

Sterile Field Technique

Everything touching the surgical site must be sterile: gowns, gloves, drapes, and instruments. The scrub nurse guards the field, and anything contaminated is removed immediately. One break in sterility can seed a deep infection.

Electrosurgery

Electrosurgical units cut tissue and seal blood vessels with electric current, reducing bleeding dramatically. The surgeon switches between cut and coagulation modes with a foot pedal. A grounding pad protects the patient from burns.

Minimally Invasive Laparoscopy

Laparoscopy operates through small incisions using a camera and long instruments, instead of one large open cut. Patients have less pain, smaller scars, and faster discharge. Gallbladders, hernias, and many gynecologic cases are done this way.

The post-anesthesia care unit watches patients wake up: breathing, blood pressure, pain, nausea, and bleeding. Nurses use the Aldrete score to judge readiness for discharge. Most patients spend one to two hours here.

Aldrete Discharge Score

The Aldrete score rates activity, breathing, circulation, consciousness, and oxygen — 0 to 2 points each. A score of 9 or 10 means safe discharge from PACU. It turns 'ready to go' into an objective number.

Surgical Site Infection Prevention

Infection prevention starts before the incision: antibiotics within 60 minutes, clipping (not shaving) hair, skin prep with chlorhexidine, and sterile technique. Post-op, wounds are watched for redness, drainage, and fever.

Patient Positioning

Patients are positioned — supine, prone, lateral, lithotomy — to expose the surgical site while protecting nerves and pressure points. Padding guards elbows, heels, and eyes. Poor positioning can injure nerves that take months to heal.

Specimen Handling

Tissue removed in surgery goes to pathology in labeled containers, sometimes rushed as a 'frozen section' for a diagnosis while the patient is still asleep. Correct labeling ties every specimen to the right patient and site — a mix-up is catastrophic.

OR Turnover

Between cases, the room is cleaned, trash removed, surfaces disinfected, and fresh sterile setups brought in. Fast, thorough turnover keeps the schedule moving without cutting corners. Average turnover runs 20 to 30 minutes.

Block Scheduling

OR time is scheduled in blocks assigned to surgical services — orthopedics gets two rooms on Mondays, for example. Unused block time is released to others. Good scheduling fills rooms without running past midnight.

Wound Closure Techniques

Surgeons close wounds in layers: deep sutures for strength, skin sutures or staples for the surface, sometimes glue for small cuts. The method balances strength, healing, and scarring. Drains may be left to prevent fluid buildup.
