JAH-R20-PSSIM-000001 — Public data: the Sun and the eight planets — masses and orbits

This public record supplies the real data the solar-system collider simulations build on. The Sun holds 99.86 percent of the system's mass (1.989 x 10^30 kg). The planets, in order: Mercury (3.30 x 10^23 kg, 47.4 km/s orbital speed), Venus (4.87 x 10^24 kg, 35.0 km/s), Earth (5.97 x 10^24 kg, 29.8 km/s), Mars (6.42 x 10^23 kg, 24.1 km/s), Jupiter (1.90 x 10^27 kg, 13.1 km/s), Saturn (5.68 x 10^26 kg, 9.7 km/s), Uranus (8.68 x 10^25 kg, 6.8 km/s), Neptune (1.02 x 10^26 kg, 5.4 km/s). Orbital speeds fall with distance exactly as Kepler's laws require.

These are the inputs the simulation database treats as fixed public facts: any scenario's masses and velocities must match this table or state its deviation. Escape velocities — 11.2 km/s for Earth, 59.5 km/s for Jupiter, 617.5 km/s for the Sun — set the characteristic speeds of infall scenarios. The asteroid belt (between Mars and Jupiter) and the Kuiper belt (beyond Neptune) supply the small-body populations; the Oort cloud, hypothesized at up to 100,000 AU, supplies long-period comets.

Data provenance: planetary masses and orbits from NASA/JPL ephemerides and the IAU; these are among the most precisely known numbers in science (Earth's mass to ~0.01 percent). The simulation layer may vary them only as labeled hypotheticals.

JAH-R20-PSSIM-000002 — Public data: the observed collision record — craters and airbursts

The solar system has a public collision record written in craters. Earth's: Chicxulub (~180 km, 66 million years ago, linked to the end-Cretaceous extinction), Sudbury (~250 km, 1.85 billion years), Vredefort (~300 km, 2 billion years, the largest verified). The Moon's far side and Mercury's surface are saturated with craters — a preserved record of the Late Heavy Bombardment ~4 billion years ago. Mars carries Hellas Basin (~2,300 km); Jupiter's moon Callisto is crater-saturated.

Observed in real time: Comet Shoemaker-Levy 9 struck Jupiter in July 1994 — 21 fragments, the largest releasing an estimated 6 million megatons, leaving Earth-sized scars in the cloud tops for months. The Chelyabinsk airburst of 15 February 2013 (a ~20-metre asteroid, ~500 kilotons, 1,500 injuries from broken glass) was recorded by hundreds of dashcams and became the best-documented impact event in history. The 1908 Tunguska event flattened 2,150 square kilometres of Siberian forest with no crater.

This record is what the simulations are checked against: any impact-scaling law must reproduce these events' energies from their sizes and speeds. The database keeps the observed record (this public edition) strictly separate from hypothetical scenarios (the simulation edition).

JAH-R20-PSSIM-000003 — Public data: small bodies — asteroids, comets, and near-Earth objects

The simulation's impactor populations are public data. As of 2026, over 1.3 million asteroids have catalogued orbits (Minor Planet Center), most in the main belt between Mars and Jupiter. Near-Earth objects (NEOs): over 35,000 catalogued, of which more than 2,300 are classified potentially hazardous (PHAs) — larger than ~140 metres and passing within 0.05 AU of Earth's orbit. NASA's planetary-defense surveys have found over 90 percent of the kilometre-class NEOs; the 140-metre completeness is the ongoing survey goal, pursued by the NEO Surveyor space telescope.

Comets: the public record includes Halley (76-year period, last 1986, next 2061), Hale-Bopp (1997, the great comet), and the sungrazing Kreutz family continuously observed disintegrating by the SOHO spacecraft — real-time disruption data the simulations use. Interstellar visitors 1I/'Oumuamua (2017) and 2I/Borisov (2019) proved bodies cross between stars.

DART (Double Asteroid Redirection Test, 2022) gave the first full-scale momentum-transfer measurement: NASA's impactor struck Dimorphos and shortened its orbit around Didymos by 33 minutes — public proof that kinetic impactors can deflect asteroids, and the calibration point for any deflection scenario. This edition records the populations; the simulation edition flies the hypothetical missions.

JAH-R20-PSSIM-000004 — Public data: moons, rings, and tidal systems

The simulations' tidal and disruption scenarios rest on public satellite data. Notable systems: Earth's Moon (3,474 km diameter, receding 3.8 cm/year — measured by lunar laser ranging since 1969); Mars's Phobos (spiraling inward, predicted to disrupt into a ring in ~40 million years); Jupiter's 95 known moons including the four Galilean moons — Io (the most volcanically active body known, tidally heated), Europa (subsurface ocean, prime astrobiology target), Ganymede (largest moon, own magnetic field), Callisto (ancient cratered surface); Saturn's 146 moons including Titan (thick nitrogen atmosphere, methane lakes) and Enceladus (cryovolcanic plumes feeding the E ring); Neptune's Triton (captured Kuiper object, retrograde orbit, nitrogen geysers).

Rings: Saturn's (the archetype, mostly water ice, ~280,000 km across but ~10 metres thick), plus Jupiter, Uranus, and Neptune ring systems — and the public 2023-2026 confirmation work on rings around the dwarf planet Quaoar beyond the classical Roche limit, challenging ring-formation models. The Roche limit itself (~2.44 planetary radii for fluid bodies) is the public formula every disruption scenario uses.

Tidal heating is public, measured physics: Io's volcanism, Europa's ocean, Enceladus's plumes are all powered by orbital eccentricity pumping. The simulation edition extrapolates these with the same equations; this edition holds the measured values.
