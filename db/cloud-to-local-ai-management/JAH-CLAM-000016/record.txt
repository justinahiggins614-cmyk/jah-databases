DESCRIPTIONS
Local machines use power and make heat, so the manager budgets both: how much power each job may use, and what happens when a machine gets too hot. The budget is set per machine, because a small laptop and a big desktop are not the same. A job that would push a machine past its power limit is moved to a machine with room, or to the cloud, or it waits. Heat is watched the same way: when a machine reports it is hot, the manager slows its jobs or moves them, and tells the user. The budget is checked monthly against the real power use, and when a machine keeps running hot, the manager's placement rules are changed so the machine is given less work. The Energy and Heat Budget stage works through 4 parts: Power limits; Heat watch; Job moves; Monthly check. Each part is named, tested, and filed, so the work can be checked by a stranger. This record specifies the stage completely: what it is for, how it is done, how it is tested, what it costs in time and attention, and what can go wrong. The specification is written for a builder who has never done this stage before, and for a reviewer who must judge whether it was done properly. Inside the Cloud-to-Local AI Management Portal, Energy and Heat Budget sits between the stages that come before it and the stages that come after it, and it must fit both: what it receives must be in the shape it expects, and what it hands on must be in the shape the next stage expects. WORKFLOW
Problem: The problem Energy and Heat Budget solves is simple to state and hard to do well: Counting the power local machines use and keeping them cool. Without this stage, the work goes wrong in a particular way. The failure shows up late, when it is expensive to fix, and it shows up as a surprise, because nothing was recorded that would have warned anyone. The stage exists to move the failure earlier, where it is cheap, and to make it visible, where it can be fixed. The problem is not that people are careless; it is that a job with many parts and many hands needs a fixed shape, or the parts drift apart. Energy and Heat Budget is that fixed shape for this part of the work.
Solution: The worked solution runs in 5 steps, in order. step 1: Set a power limit per machine; step 2: Move jobs that would break the limit; step 3: Slow or move jobs when a machine is hot; step 4: Tell the user when heat moves a job; step 5: Check the budget monthly. Each step has a visible result: something written, something checked, or something handed on, so the stage can be stopped and inspected at any point. The steps are done by the person or program named in the record, and each step is checked before the next begins, because a mistake carried forward is a mistake multiplied. The parts of the stage are: Power limits; Heat watch; Job moves; Monthly check. When a step fails, the worker stops, fixes the failure, and re-runs the step; the stage never skips a failed step and hopes the next one covers it. The whole method is written so that two workers following it would produce the same result, and a reviewer following it could find where a result came from.
Answer: The answer this stage delivers is a finished piece of work that the next stage can use without asking questions: the Energy and Heat Budget is complete, checked, and filed. The checks that prove it are: Every machine has a power limit; Hot machines get less work; Heat moves are reported. When those checks pass, the stage is done, and the proof is the record itself — the checks, the dates, and the names. The figures that describe this stage are: budget_checks = 12; heat_moves = 7; machines_budgeted = 18; power_limit_watts = 65. Those numbers are the record's own checkable figures, and the demo in this record recomputes them from the figures alone, so any reader can verify them without trusting the record's word. A stage whose checks pass is a stage the factory will stand behind.
