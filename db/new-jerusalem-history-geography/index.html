Ancient Jerusalem occupied a narrow, defensible ridge in the Judean hills, roughly 750 to 800 metres above sea level, with the Mount of Olives across the Kidron Valley to the east rising slightly higher. The stadion (plural stadia) is the unit behind Revelation 21:16's 12,000 stadia and behind many ancient distance statements. Science proof: stadion lengths are established by measuring surviving racecourses (stadia) and by comparing ancient distance statements with surveyed ground distances. The spread of results is the evidence for variation, not a failure of measurement.

Geology Beneath Jerusalem

Engineering view: Engineering view: geology is rock units in order, with thickness and character. Jerusalem sits on Cretaceous limestone, dolomite, chalk, and marl, and the building stone of the city — the local limestone known as Jerusalem stone — comes from those units, which is why the city's walls and buildings share a colour.

The Judean hills are built of Cretaceous sedimentary rocks, laid down in ancient seas and later uplifted and folded. The harder limestone and dolomite form ridges and building stone; the softer chalk and marl form slopes and valleys and are quarried for lime. Karst processes — water dissolving limestone — formed caves, including the large Zedekiah's Cave quarry under the Old City, and the Gihon Spring issues where a perched aquifer meets the valley side. Faulting and folding tilt the beds, so a tunnel like Hezekiah's passes through rock of varying hardness, which the Siloam inscription's account of meeting teams reflects in its own way. The record gives that sequence as public geological fact.

Science proof: rock units are mapped by geological survey, dated by fossils and by stratigraphic position, and quarried stone is identified by petrology. Karst dissolution is a measured chemical process: slightly acidic rainwater dissolves calcium carbonate over time.
