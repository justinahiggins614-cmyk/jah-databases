What Milk Is Rank 1 · simple · fundamentals · beginner

Milk is the fluid mammals produce to feed their young, and the milk people drink most often comes from cows, goats, or sheep. Whole cow milk is about 87 percent water, with the rest made up of lactose (milk sugar) at about 4.8 percent, fat at about 3.6 percent, protein at about 3.3 percent, and minerals at about 0.7 percent. The protein divides into casein, about 80 percent of the protein, which forms soft curds and gives milk its white color, and whey, about 20 percent, which stays liquid when milk curdles. The fat travels in tiny globules wrapped in a membrane, and the minerals include calcium, potassium, and phosphorus. Skim milk removes almost all the fat while keeping most of the protein, lactose, and minerals, and cream is the fat-rich layer that rises from whole milk. Milk tastes mildly sweet because of its lactose, and it feels rich because of its fat and protein together.

Composition

Water 87%, lactose 4.8%, fat 3.6%, protein 3.3% (about 80% casein, 20% whey), minerals 0.7%.

Method

Measure by weight: 1,000 grams of whole milk holds about 870 grams of water, 48 grams of lactose, 36 grams of fat, 33 grams of protein, and 7 grams of minerals.

Storage

Fresh milk keeps refrigerated at 2 to 4 degrees Celsius and is used within a few days of opening.

What Honey Is Rank 2 · simple · fundamentals · beginner

Honey is the sweet food honeybees make from flower nectar. Foraging bees collect nectar, carry it back to the hive, and pass it between bees while enzymes break the nectar sugars down; the bees then fan the stored nectar until most of the water has evaporated. Finished honey is about 38.5 percent fructose and 31 percent glucose, with about 17.1 percent water, about 9 percent other sugars, and the rest enzymes, minerals, acids, and other compounds. Because so much of honey is sugar and so little is water, honey has low water activity, around 0.6, which is why microbes struggle to grow in it and why honey keeps for a very long time when it is kept dry and sealed. Honey varies in color and flavor with the flowers the bees visited, from pale clover honey to dark buckwheat honey, and raw honey may still hold pollen grains and tiny wax particles.

Composition

Fructose 38.5%, glucose 31%, water 17.1%, other sugars 9%, enzymes, minerals, acids, and other compounds 4.4%.

Method

Bees reduce nectar water by fanning stored nectar in the comb until the water content falls near 17 percent.

Storage

Honey keeps for years sealed and dry at room temperature; it crystallizes over time, which is natural and reversible.

    print("%s: %.1f%% = %.1f g" % (name, pct, grams))

print("Water activity: about 0.6 (low, which limits microbial growth)")

What Beeswax Is Rank 3 · simple · fundamentals · beginner

Beeswax is the wax worker honeybees secrete from glands on their abdomens and shape into the comb that holds honey, pollen, and brood. Chemically it is mostly esters, about 70 percent, with hydrocarbons around 15 percent and free fatty acids around 14 percent, plus small amounts of other compounds. Pure beeswax melts between about 62 and 64 degrees Celsius, which is why it softens in a warm room but holds its shape at ordinary temperatures, and it burns with a warm golden color. Food-grade beeswax carries the food additive code E901, and only food-grade wax is used where wax touches food; wax that is not food-grade is kept away from food entirely. Fresh wax is pale and nearly white, and it turns deeper yellow and then brown as it ages in the hive and picks up pollen and propolis.

Composition

Esters about 71%, hydrocarbons about 15%, free fatty acids about 14%.

Method

Bees secrete wax flakes, chew them soft, and build comb from them; people harvest wax as cappings and old comb.

Safety

Food contact uses food-grade beeswax only (E901). Non-food-grade wax is never used for food.

Milking Without Death Rank 4 · simple · production · beginner

Milking is the act of drawing milk from a dairy animal, and it does not involve slaughter: the animal is milked, fed, and milked again. On a small farm the milker washes the udder, checks the first milk, and milks by hand or with a small machine, usually twice a day, while the animal stands calmly and keeps eating. A healthy dairy cow gives roughly 20 to 30 litres of milk a day at peak production, a goat gives roughly 2 to 4 litres, and a sheep gives roughly 1 to 2 litres, though breeds, feed, and season move these numbers a good deal. Milk is strained, cooled quickly to slow bacterial growth, and stored cold. The plain fact is that milking is a harvest from a living animal, like shearing wool or gathering eggs, and the animal stays alive through the whole process.

Method

Wash the udder, check the first milk, milk twice daily by hand or machine, strain, and cool quickly to 2 to 4 degrees Celsius.

Safety

Cool milk promptly and keep it refrigerated; milk from a sick animal is not used.

Storage

Fresh milk keeps refrigerated at 2 to 4 degrees Celsius and is used within a few days of opening.

Beekeeping Harvest Without Death Rank 5 · simple · production · beginner

A beekeeper harvests honey by taking frames of capped honey from the hive, removing the wax caps, and spinning the honey out in an extractor, while leaving the bees alive and leaving enough honey in the hive for the colony to eat. Careful beekeepers work slowly, use smoke gently to calm the bees, brush bees off frames rather than shaking them hard, and never take the colony's whole food store. The plain facts must be stated honestly: careful handling greatly reduces harm, but commercial production does not involve zero bee deaths, and some bees may still be lost even in a careful harvest. The harvest itself does not require killing the colony, and a well-kept colony goes on working after the honey is taken, which is why beekeeping is described as a harvest from a living colony rather than a slaughter.

Method

Take capped frames, brush bees off gently, uncap, spin in an extractor, return wet frames for the bees to clean, and leave a food reserve in the hive.

Safety

Work slowly with gentle smoke; wear a veil and gloves; keep the hive entrance clear so bees can return.

Storage

Extracted honey is strained, settled, and stored sealed and dry at room temperature.

Working demo program

Wax Rendering from Cappings and Old Comb Rank 6 · compounded · production · intermediate

Wax rendering is how beekeepers turn cappings, the thin wax lids cut off honey frames at harvest, and old comb into clean blocks of beeswax. The cappings and comb are melted gently in water, often in a solar wax melter or a pot kept below boiling, and the melted wax rises to the top while honey, propolis, and debris sink or stay in the water. After cooling, the solid wax cake is lifted off, scraped clean underneath, and melted again through a fine cloth filter if a cleaner block is wanted. Old dark comb gives darker wax than fresh cappings, and repeated filtering lightens it. The wax is then poured into molds and cooled slowly so it does not crack. Rendering uses no slaughter-derived ingredient at any step, and it turns what would be hive waste into candles, wraps, balms, and coatings.

Method

Melt cappings and old comb gently in water, lift the cooled wax cake, scrape it clean, filter through cloth on a second melt, and mold with slow cooling.

Safety

Never leave melting wax unattended and never melt wax over an open flame; wax is flammable, so use a water bath or solar melter.

Storage

Store wax blocks cool and dry, away from strong smells, which wax absorbs.

Pasteurization and Milk Safety Rank 7 · compounded · safety · intermediate

Pasteurization is the gentle heating of milk to kill harmful microbes without boiling the milk or changing its character much. The two standard methods are HTST, short-time heating to at least 72 degrees Celsius for at least 15 seconds, and batch pasteurization, heating to at least 63 degrees Celsius for at least 30 minutes, followed in both cases by rapid cooling. Pasteurization greatly lowers the risk from milk, which is why public health guidance favors pasteurized milk, while raw milk carries a higher risk because it has not been heated. Pasteurized milk still needs refrigeration, still spoils after some days, and still carries an allergy risk for people allergic to milk protein, because heating does not remove the proteins that cause allergy. The rule is simple: heat it right, cool it fast, keep it cold.

Method

HTST: at least 72 degrees Celsius for at least 15 seconds. Batch: at least 63 degrees Celsius for at least 30 minutes. Cool rapidly after heating.

Safety

Pasteurization greatly lowers microbial risk but does not remove milk allergy risk. Raw milk carries a higher risk because it has not been heated. Keep pasteurized milk refrigerated.

Storage

Refrigerate pasteurized milk at 2 to 4 degrees Celsius and use within a few days of opening.

Working demo program

Milk Allergy and Lactose, Plainly Rank 8 · compounded · safety · intermediate

Milk allergy and lactose intolerance are two different things, and confusing them causes real trouble. Milk allergy is an immune response to milk proteins, chiefly casein and whey, and it can cause serious reactions, so people with milk allergy avoid milk and milk products entirely and read labels for casein, whey, and lactalbumin. Lactose intolerance is different: the body makes too little lactase, the enzyme that splits lactose, so undigested lactose ferments in the gut and causes discomfort, but it is not an immune reaction and it is not an allergy. People with lactose intolerance often tolerate small amounts of milk, yogurt with live cultures, and hard cheeses, which hold less lactose, and lactose-free milk has its lactose already split for them. Nothing in this record tells any individual what condition they have; a person who suspects either condition asks a clinician for testing rather than guessing.

Safety

Milk allergy is an immune response to milk proteins (casein, whey) and requires avoiding milk products. Lactose intolerance is low lactase, not an allergy. Anyone who suspects either condition asks a clinician for testing.

Method

Read labels for casein, whey, and lactalbumin. Lactose-free milk has its lactose already split; it still holds milk protein.

Storage

Lactose-free milk keeps under the same refrigeration rules as ordinary milk.

Honey and Infants Under 12 Months Rank 9 · compounded · safety · intermediate

Honey must not be given to infants under 12 months old. The rule exists because honey can carry spores of the bacterium that causes infant botulism, a rare but serious illness, and an infant's gut is not yet mature enough to keep those spores from growing. Older children and adults, whose guts are mature, handle the same spores without trouble, which is why the rule stops at 12 months rather than lasting for life. The rule covers all honey, including raw honey, pasteurized honey, and honey mixed into foods, drinks, and pacifier dips, because heating honey does not reliably destroy the spores. After the first birthday, honey may be given as an ordinary food. This record states the rule plainly and does not soften it: no honey under 12 months, no exceptions for small amounts.

Safety

MANDATORY RULE: no honey for infants under 12 months, in any form, including honey in foods, drinks, and pacifier dips. Honey can carry spores that cause infant botulism, and an infant's gut is not yet mature enough to resist them. After the first birthday, honey may be given as an ordinary food.

Method

Check the child's age in months before any honey is served: under 12 means no honey, 12 or older means the rule no longer applies.

Storage

Keep honey sealed and out of reach of small children, and label honey jars with the under-12-months rule.

Working demo program

Storage of Milk, Honey, and Wax Rank 10 · compounded · storage · intermediate

Milk, honey, and wax each store differently, and getting storage right is what keeps them good. Fresh milk stays refrigerated at 2 to 4 degrees Celsius, is used within about 5 to 7 days of opening, and freezes for about 3 months, though freezing changes its texture. UHT milk keeps unopened for months at room temperature and, once opened, follows the same refrigeration rule as fresh milk. Honey keeps for years sealed and dry at room temperature, because its low water activity resists spoilage, and it crystallizes over time, which is natural; crystallized honey reliquifies in a warm water bath. Beeswax keeps for years cool and dry, away from strong smells, which wax absorbs, and away from heat, which softens it. The three together cover the whole suite pantry: cold and short for milk, dry and long for honey, cool and dry for wax.

Storage

Fresh milk: refrigerated 2 to 4 degrees Celsius, 5 to 7 days after opening, about 90 days frozen. Honey: sealed, dry, room temperature, years. Beeswax: cool, dry, away from strong smells and heat, years.

Method

Refrigerate milk promptly after use. Keep honey jars sealed so honey does not absorb water from the air. Keep wax away from heat and strong smells.

Safety

Discard milk that smells sour or has been left warm for hours. Honey that has absorbed water can ferment; keep jars sealed.

The Science of Emulsification Rank 11 · complex · science · advanced

Emulsification is the science of holding oil and water together in a stable mixture, and milk itself is a natural emulsion: fat globules float in water, kept apart by a membrane of proteins and phospholipids. When a cook makes mayonnaise, egg yolk lecithin and mustard coat tiny oil droplets and stop them from merging, which is why mayonnaise holds its shape instead of splitting into oil and water. A vinaigrette is a temporary emulsion: shaking breaks oil into droplets that stay mixed for minutes, then the dressing separates again, while a cream sauce is an emulsion held by starch and milk protein. The key numbers are the ratio of oil to water phase and the amount of emulsifier: too little emulsifier and the mixture splits, too much and it turns heavy. Heat, acid, and salt all shift the balance, which is why a cream sauce splits when it is boiled hard and why lemon juice helps mayonnaise hold.

Composition

Milk is a natural oil-in-water emulsion: fat globules in water, stabilized by a membrane of proteins and phospholipids.

Method

Add oil slowly to the water phase while whisking, with lecithin or mustard as emulsifier; keep the mixture cool and do not boil a cream emulsion hard.

Safety

Mayonnaise made with raw yolk keeps refrigerated and is used quickly; keep cream sauces refrigerated after cooking.

The Science of Fermentation Rank 12 · complex · science · advanced

Fermentation is the work of microbes turning sugars into acids, alcohol, and gases, and it is how milk becomes yogurt and kefir and how honey becomes mead. In yogurt, lactic acid bacteria eat lactose and make lactic acid, which drops the pH, thickens the milk as casein sets, and gives yogurt its tang; in kefir, bacteria and yeasts work together, making a lightly fizzy cultured milk. In mead, yeast eats honey sugars and makes alcohol and carbon dioxide, and the finished strength depends on how much sugar the yeast had and how much is left: a simple estimate multiplies the drop in specific gravity by 131.25 to get alcohol by volume. Fermentation also preserves food, because acid and alcohol slow spoilage microbes, and it builds flavor, because the same microbes make hundreds of aroma compounds. The controls are temperature, time, sugar, and salt, and clean equipment matters, because unwanted microbes compete with the wanted ones.

Composition

Yogurt: lactose converted to lactic acid by lactic acid bacteria, dropping pH and setting casein. Mead: honey sugars converted to alcohol and carbon dioxide by yeast.

Method

Keep cultures warm and steady for yogurt; keep mead vessels clean, airlocked, and cool while yeast works. Record starting and finishing gravity for mead.

Safety

Fermented milk keeps refrigerated after setting. Mead is an alcoholic drink and is kept away from children; honey rules for infants still apply to honey itself.

Working demo program

    return (og - fg) * 131.25

The Science of Gelling Without Gelatin Rank 13 · complex · science · advanced

Gelling is how a liquid is set into a soft solid, and it can be done without gelatin, which is excluded from this suite because it requires animal death. Agar, from seaweed, sets firmly at about 35 to 40 degrees Celsius and holds its set even in a warm room, which is why agar jellies stay firm where gelatin would melt; about 0.8 percent agar by weight, or 8 grams per litre, sets a firm jelly. Pectin, from fruit, needs sugar and acid to set, which is why it suits jams and fruit jellies at about 1 percent by weight. Carrageenan, also from seaweed, sets milk especially well, because it links with milk protein, and about 0.6 percent sets a soft milk jelly. Each gelling agent behaves differently on heating and cooling, so a cook picks the agent by the food: agar for firm molded jellies, pectin for fruit preserves, carrageenan for milk puddings. All three give full, sliceable, spoonable results with no slaughter-derived ingredient.

Composition

Agar: seaweed gelling agent, firm set, holds in a warm room. Pectin: fruit gelling agent, needs sugar and acid. Carrageenan: seaweed gelling agent, sets milk especially well through milk protein.

Method

Agar: dissolve in boiling liquid, pour, cool. Pectin: boil with fruit, sugar, and acid until set. Carrageenan: dissolve in warm milk, cool to set. Gelatin is excluded from this suite.

Storage

Agar jellies keep refrigerated for several days and hold their set at room temperature; pectin preserves keep sealed for months.

The Science of Freezing and Crystallization Rank 14 · complex · science · advanced

Freezing and crystallization are two ways liquids turn solid, and both shape milk and honey. When milk freezes, water turns to ice first and pushes fat, protein, and lactose into the remaining liquid, which is why thawed milk can look grainy and separate; shaking or blending after thawing brings much of it back together. Honey does not freeze solid in an ordinary freezer, because its sugars resist freezing, but it crystallizes: glucose, which dissolves less easily than fructose, forms crystals, and honey with a high glucose-to-water ratio crystallizes fastest. Crystallization is fastest around 14 degrees Celsius and slowest when honey is kept cool and still or warm and moving, and it reverses in a warm water bath, which reliquifies the honey without harming its flavor. Beeswax, by contrast, simply hardens as it cools below its melting range, and it can be remelted and remolded many times.

Composition

Honey glucose-to-water ratio near 1.81 (31 grams glucose to 17.1 grams water per 100 grams) predicts ready crystallization. Milk freezes near -0.5 degrees Celsius.

Method

Freeze milk promptly and shake after thawing. Reliquify crystallized honey in a warm water bath, never by boiling. Cool wax slowly in molds.

Storage

Milk: freezer about 90 days. Honey: sealed, dry, room temperature; crystallization is natural. Wax: cool and dry.

The Science of Maillard Browning and Candy Stages Rank 15 · complex · science · advanced

Maillard browning is the reaction between sugars and proteins under heat that turns bread crusts golden, milk puddings brown on top, and dulce de leche deep amber, and it begins in earnest around 140 to 165 degrees Celsius in low-moisture cooking. It is different from caramelization, which is sugar browning alone at higher heat, and the two often happen together in milk and honey cooking, because milk brings both lactose and protein and honey brings fructose and glucose. Candy stages are the cook's thermometer map for sugar syrups: thread stage near 110 degrees Celsius, soft ball near 115, firm ball near 121, hard ball near 127, soft crack near 138, hard crack near 149, and caramel near 170. A milk caramel cooked to soft ball stays spoonable, while honey candy cooked to hard crack snaps clean, and each stage is a water-content step, because boiling syrup loses water as its temperature climbs. Controlling heat and reading the thermometer is what separates a smooth milk fudge from a grainy one.

Composition

Maillard browning: sugars plus proteins, from about 140 degrees Celsius. Caramelization: sugar alone, at higher heat. Milk brings lactose and protein; honey brings fructose and glucose.

Method

Read the thermometer: thread 110, soft ball 115, firm ball 121, hard ball 127, soft crack 138, hard crack 149, caramel 170 degrees Celsius. Stir milk sweets to prevent scorching.

Safety

Hot sugar syrup causes severe burns; keep children away from the pot and never add water to hot syrup.

Working demo program

STAGES = [(110, "thread"), (115, "soft ball"), (121, "firm ball"), (127, "hard ball"), (138, "soft crack"), (149, "hard crack"), (170, "caramel")]


def stage_for(temp_c):
    found = "below thread"
    for mark, name in STAGES:
        if temp_c >= mark:
            found = name
    return found


print("Candy stage lookup")

    print("%d degrees C: %s stage" % (temp_c, stage_for(temp_c)))
