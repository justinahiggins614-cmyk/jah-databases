What Microbiology Is

Microbiology is the science of the smallest living things: the bacteria, the viruses, the fungi, and the protozoa, too small to see with the eye. The microbiologist studies how they live, grow, and change, and how they affect us: the bread, the disease, and the soil. Microbiology is the science of the invisible: the world too small to see, which shapes the world we can see.

A bacterium is about 1 micrometre long, and a human hair is about 100 micrometres wide: the hair is 100 times as wide as the bacterium. A virus is about 100 nanometres, which is 10 times smaller than the bacterium. A microscope magnifies 400 times, so a 1 micrometre bacterium looks 400 micrometres wide, or 0.4 millimetres. Leeuwenhoek saw the microbes in the 1670s, and microbiology is about 350 years old in 2026. The invisible world is the largest: the microbes are the most numerous living things on Earth.

Bacteria

Bacteria are the single-celled microbes without a nucleus: the smallest free-living things, which divide in two, and live everywhere — in the soil, the water, the air, and the body. Bacteria are the oldest life: they have lived for billions of years, and they are the most numerous living things on Earth. Bacteria are both the helpers and the harms: they make the bread, and they cause the disease, and the microbiologist studies both.

A bacterium divides every 20 minutes, so in 1 hour it doubles 3 times, and 1 becomes 8. In 7 hours, it doubles 21 times, and 1 becomes about 2 million: the growth is exponential. A colony on a plate has about 1 million bacteria, and it is visible as a spot of 1 millimetre. The bacteria are about 1 micrometre long, and a colony of 1 million is 1 millimetre wide: the invisible becomes visible, by the million. Koch proved the bacteria cause disease in the 1880s, and the proof is 140 years old in 2026.

Viruses

A virus is a tiny particle of genes and protein: it has no cell of its own, and it must live inside a cell, where it copies itself. Viruses are the smallest infectious things: they cause the cold, the flu, and many other diseases, and they are also the tools of the laboratory. Viruses are the border of life: they are not quite alive, and not quite not, and the microbiologist studies them, to understand them and to defend against them.

A virus is about 100 nanometres wide, and a bacterium is about 1,000: the virus is 10 times smaller. The electron microscope magnifies 100,000 times, so a 100 nanometre virus looks 10 millimetres wide. A single infected cell can make about 1,000 new viruses, and each infects a new cell: the growth is exponential, like the bacteria. The first virus was found in the 1890s, and virology is about 130 years old in 2026. The virus is the border of life: it copies itself, but only inside a cell.

Fungi

Fungi are the recyclers: they break down the dead, and they return the nutrients to the soil, and they feed the plants. Fungi are the largest living things, and the smallest: the mushroom is the fruit, and the mycelium is the body, which can cover hectares.

A yeast cell divides every 2 hours, so in 1 day it doubles 12 times, and 1 becomes about 4,000. The mycelium of one fungus covers about 9 square kilometres, in Oregon, and it is the largest living thing known. Penicillin was found in 1928, and the antibiotic age is about 98 years old in 2026. The mushroom is the fruit of the fungus: the mycelium is the body, under the ground, and the mushroom is the fruit, above. The fungi are the recyclers: they break down the dead, and the nutrients return to the soil.
