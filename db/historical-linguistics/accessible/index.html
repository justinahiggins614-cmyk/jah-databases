Comparative Method

The comparative method is the reconstructing of languages by comparing them: by finding regular correspondences between related languages, linguists infer the sounds of their common ancestor. English father, Latin pater, and Greek patēr show the correspondence f, p, p, and from it the ancestor's sound is reconstructed. The method is the foundation of historical linguistics. Sound Change

Sound change is the changing of sounds over time: a sound in one generation becomes another sound in the next, regularly and across the whole language. Latin p became Germanic f, so pater became father, and the change applied to every p at the start of a word. Sound change is regular, and its regularity is what lets linguists reconstruct the past. Grimm's Law

Grimm's law is the consonant shift that separates the Germanic languages from the rest of Indo-European: voiceless stops became fricatives, voiced stops became voiceless, and voiced fricatives became stops. Latin pater became English father, Latin ped became foot, and Latin cord became heart. The law is the most famous sound change in linguistics, and it proved that sound change is regular. Proto-Language

A proto-language is the reconstructed ancestor of a language family: the language that the family grew from, rebuilt from its daughters by the comparative method. Proto-Indo-European is the ancestor of English, Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, and hundreds of other languages, and it was spoken some five or six thousand years ago. No text of it survives; everything known about it is reconstructed. Reconstruction

Reconstruction is the rebuilding of a language that left no text: by comparing its daughters, linguists infer the sounds, words, and grammar of the ancestor. The reconstruction is written with asterisks, as *ph₂tḗr for father, to mark that it is inferred, not attested. Reconstruction is the central craft of historical linguistics, and its results are tested by whether they predict the daughters. Analogy

Analogy is the change by copying a pattern: when a regular pattern is extended to a word that did not follow it. English helped was once holp, but the regular -ed pattern replaced it, because speakers copied the pattern of walked and talked. Analogy works against sound change: sound change makes forms irregular, analogy makes them regular again, and the history of a language is the balance between the two. Semantic Change

Grammaticalization

Grammaticalization is the process by which words become grammar: the verb go in I am going to leave is no longer about movement, it marks the future, and the noun body in somebody is no longer a body, it marks a person. Words lose their concrete meaning, become fixed in one position, and end as affixes or auxiliaries. Grammaticalization is the main way new grammar is made. Language contact is what happens when languages meet: their speakers borrow words, mix grammars, and sometimes create new languages. Internal Reconstruction

Internal reconstruction is the reconstructing of a language's past from the language alone, without comparing it with others: by finding the alternations inside it, such as the vowel changes in sing, sang, sung, and inferring the earlier forms that explain them. Glottochronology

Glottochronology is the dating of languages by their shared words: by counting how many words of a basic list two languages still share, and assuming words are replaced at a steady rate, the time since they split is estimated. The method is disputed, because the rate is not steady and borrowing corrupts the count, but it is still used as a rough clock where no other dating exists. Neogrammarian Hypothesis

The neogrammarian hypothesis is the claim that sound change has no exceptions: when a sound changes, it changes in every word where its conditions hold, with no exceptions except by analogy or borrowing. The hypothesis was the battle cry of the neogrammarians in the 1870s, and it made historical linguistics a science, because it made sound change testable and exceptionless by definition. Lexical Diffusion

Lexical diffusion is the spreading of a change word by word: instead of applying to all words at once, the change starts in a few words and spreads through the vocabulary over time. The Great Vowel Shift spread through English words over centuries, and the change can be caught halfway, with some words changed and others not yet. Lexical diffusion is the rival of the neogrammarian view, and the two are now seen as two ways change can spread. Drift

Drift is the changing of languages in the same direction over long periods: related languages, separated for centuries, keep changing the same way, as if pulled by a shared tendency. English and the other Germanic languages all lost their endings and fixed their word order, and the drift is explained by the shared structure they inherited. Drift shows that change is not random: the starting point shapes the direction. Language Death

Language death is the death of a language: when its last speaker dies, or when its speakers all shift to another language, the language ends. Half the world's languages are endangered, and many will die in this century, taking with them the knowledge, the stories, and the ways of seeing that only they carry. Language death is studied, documented, and, where possible, reversed by revival.
