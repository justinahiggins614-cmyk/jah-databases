1. Description

A business-in-a-box generator builds a complete starter kit for a new small business: a one-page plan, a name, a structure, a registration checklist, a startup budget, prices, a marketing plan, a sales funnel, operations, hiring, bookkeeping, tax basics, customer service, a growth plan and a risk register — all in one place, free, in the browser.

Twenty-four full records, ordered simple to working to advanced, each written in human-readable sentences and paragraphs, each carrying a history, a design, a problem, a proposed solution, dependencies, a feasibility label with its reason, a verification path, a science proof, a math proof, a formal statement, an equation, a proof sketch, a runnable demo program whose output is reproduced on the page, and two named specialist AIs.

2. Math proof

Break-even is fixed costs divided by profit per sale: 120 dollars of weekly fixed costs divided by 2 dollars profit per jar is 60 jars a week. Below 60 jars the business loses money; above 60 jars it gains. Profit is earnings minus costs: 200 dollars earned minus 120 dollars spent leaves 80 dollars profit in a week. The startup budget is a sum: 150 plus 120 plus 60 plus 170 equals 500 dollars.

3. Science proof

A starter kit works because a new owner faces too many first decisions at once, and a written kit moves those decisions from memory into a list that can be checked off one by one. Each finished part is visible proof of progress, and visible progress keeps a new owner going. That is why one box holding the plan, the budget, the prices and the checklists helps a new business open sooner and with fewer forgotten steps than memory alone.

What a Business-in-a-Box Kit Is

JAH-BBOX-000001 — A business-in-a-box kit is a complete starter set for a new small business: a plan, a name, a budget, a price list, a marketing plan and a record book, all in one place. The kit in this database holds 24 parts, and part 1 is the box itself.

Math proof: Counting is addition: 24 parts, each counted once, make one complete kit. If 1 part is missing, 24 minus 1 leaves 23 parts, so the kit is incomplete until that part is added.

Science proof: A starter kit works because a new owner faces too many first decisions at once, and a written kit moves those decisions from memory into a list that can be checked off one by one. Each finished part is visible proof of progress, and visible progress keeps a new owner going.

The kit holds 24 parts; part 1 is the box itself

The Business Plan in One Page

JAH-BBOX-000002 — A one-page business plan states what the business sells, who buys it, what it costs to run, and what it must earn, in four short paragraphs. A plan for a market stall selling jam reads: jam for neighbours, 40 jars a week, costs 120 dollars a week, earnings 200 dollars a week.

Math proof: Profit is earnings minus costs: 200 dollars earned minus 120 dollars spent leaves 80 dollars profit in a week.

Science proof: A written plan works because a goal held only in the head changes every time it is remembered, while a goal written down stays still long enough to be measured. Writing the four facts on one page forces the owner to choose, and a choice made can be tested.

Runnable demo program
earnings, costs = 200, 120
print(f"Earnings {earnings} minus costs {costs} leaves {earnings - costs} dollars profit")

Output:

Earnings 200 minus costs 120 leaves 80 dollars profit

Choosing a Business Name

JAH-BBOX-000003 — A good business name is short, easy to say, easy to spell, and free to use, so customers remember it and no one else owns it. The name 'Sunny Jam' passes all four checks: two words, plain spelling, and no other jam seller using it in town.

Math proof: Four checks, each a yes or a no: a name passes only when all four are yes, so 4 out of 4 is a pass and 3 out of 4 is not a pass.

Science proof: A name works as a memory hook: short, plain words are easier to recall and repeat than long or tricky ones, and a customer who can say the name can recommend the business to a neighbour.

Runnable demo program
checks = ['short', 'easy to say', 'easy to spell', 'free to use']
print(f"'Sunny Jam' passes {len(checks)} of {len(checks)} checks")

Output:

'Sunny Jam' passes 4 of 4 checks

Choosing a Business Structure

JAH-BBOX-000004 — A business structure is the legal shape of the business: a sole trader works alone, a partnership shares the work, and a company is a separate legal person. A jam seller working alone starts as a sole trader, and the kit records that choice with its reason. This is general information only, not legal advice.

Math proof: One owner, one share of the profit: 80 dollars profit split 1 way is 80 dollars; split 2 ways between partners is 40 dollars each.

Science proof: A structure works by drawing a clear line around who is responsible for what: when the line is written down before trading starts, later arguments about money and decisions have an answer already agreed.

Runnable demo program
profit, partners = 80, 2

Output:

80 dollars split 2 ways is 40 dollars each

Registration Checklist

JAH-BBOX-000005 — A registration checklist lists every form, fee and licence the business needs before it opens, in the order they must be done. The worked checklist has 6 steps, and step 6, opening the business bank account, comes only after step 1, registering the name.

Math proof: Six steps in order: step 1 first, step 6 last. Finishing 4 of 6 steps means 2 steps remain, because 6 minus 4 is 2.

Science proof: A checklist in order works because some steps depend on earlier ones: a bank account cannot open under a name that is not yet registered, so the order is part of the instruction, not decoration.

steps = ['Register the name', 'Choose the structure', 'Get the licence', 'Register for tax', 'Print the labels', 'Open the bank account']

Startup Budget (Worked Example)

JAH-BBOX-000006 — A startup budget lists everything the business must buy before it opens, with a price beside each item, and adds them up. The worked budget buys a stall table for 150 dollars, jars and labels for 120 dollars, a licence for 60 dollars and ingredients for 170 dollars, a total of 500 dollars.

Math proof: Addition: 150 plus 120 plus 60 plus 170 equals 500 dollars. The budget is 500 dollars, so the owner must have 500 dollars before opening day.

Science proof: A budget works by turning a vague worry about money into a list of named items with prices: once each item has a price, the total is a fact, and a fact can be saved toward with open eyes.

Pricing Your Product or Service

JAH-BBOX-000007 — A price must cover the cost of making the product and leave a profit, or the business loses money on every sale. A jar of jam costs 3 dollars to make and sells for 5 dollars, leaving 2 dollars profit on each jar.

Math proof: Price minus cost equals profit: 5 minus 3 equals 2 dollars per jar. Selling 40 jars gives 40 times 2, or 80 dollars profit.

Science proof: A price works as a signal as well as a sum: too low, and customers doubt the quality; too high, and they walk past. A price built from cost plus a fair profit can be explained to any customer who asks.

Runnable demo program
price, cost, jars = 5, 3, 40
print(f"{jars} jars at {price - cost} dollars profit each: {jars * (price - cost)} dollars profit")

Output:

40 jars at 2 dollars profit each: 80 dollars profit

The Marketing Plan

JAH-BBOX-000008 — A marketing plan says who the customers are, where they can be found, and what message will reach them, week by week. The jam stall's plan reaches 3 places: the Saturday market, the town noticeboard, and word of mouth from happy customers.

Math proof: Three channels, each reaching about 50 people a week: 3 times 50 is 150 people reached every week.

Science proof: A marketing plan works because attention is scarce: a message repeated in the same few places, week after week, is remembered, while a single shout in many places is forgotten by Monday.

Runnable demo program

print(f"{channels} channels x {reach} people = {channels * reach} people reached a week")

Output:

3 channels x 50 people = 150 people reached a week

Building a Sales Funnel

JAH-BBOX-000009 — A sales funnel is the path from stranger to customer: people hear about the business, some visit, some buy, and some come back. Of 100 people who hear about the jam stall, 20 visit, 10 buy, and 5 come back the next week.

Math proof: Conversion is a fraction: 10 buyers out of 100 who heard is 10 over 100, or 10 percent. Of the 10 buyers, 5 return, which is 5 over 10, or 50 percent.

Science proof: A funnel works because buying is a chain of small yeses: each step asks for a little more trust than the last, and measuring each step shows exactly where the chain breaks.

Runnable demo program
heard, buyers = 100, 10
print(f"{buyers} of {heard} heard buy: {100 * buyers // heard}%")

Output:

10 of 100 heard buy: 10%

Bookkeeping Basics

JAH-BBOX-000012 — Bookkeeping is writing down every dollar in and every dollar out, on the day it moves, so the business always knows where it stands. In the worked week, 200 dollars came in and 120 dollars went out, leaving 80 dollars.

Math proof: Balance is money in minus money out: 200 minus 120 is 80 dollars.

Science proof: Bookkeeping works because memory of money fades fast: a dollar written down on the day it moves is a fact, while a dollar remembered a month later is a guess, and guesses cannot be added up.

Runnable demo program
money_in, money_out = 200, 120
print(f"In {money_in}, out {money_out}: balance {money_in - money_out} dollars")

Output:

In 200, out 120: balance 80 dollars

Math proof: Satisfaction is a fraction: 9 happy out of 10 complaints is 90 percent; 8 repeat buyers out of 10 is 80 percent.

Science proof: Good service works because a customer who is heard feels respected, and a fix that follows quickly proves the respect was real: people return to businesses that treat them well after a mistake, sometimes more than to businesses that never made one.

Cash Flow and the Break-Even Point

JAH-BBOX-000017 — Cash flow is money moving in and out over time, and the break-even point is where sales exactly cover costs. The stall's fixed costs are 120 dollars a week and each jar earns 2 dollars profit, so it must sell 60 jars a week to break even.

Math proof: Break-even is fixed costs divided by profit per sale: 120 divided by 2 is 60 jars.

Science proof: A break-even point works as a single clear target: below it the business loses money, above it the business gains, so the owner knows exactly how many sales the week must hold.

Runnable demo program
fixed, profit_per_jar = 120, 2
print(f"Break even: {fixed} / {profit_per_jar} = {fixed // profit_per_jar} jars a week")

Output:

Break even: 120 / 2 = 60 jars a week

Suppliers and Inventory

JAH-BBOX-000018 — Suppliers sell the business what it needs, and inventory is the stock kept on hand so the business never runs out. The stall keeps 100 jars in stock, uses 40 a week, and orders more when 20 remain, so the shelf is never empty.

Math proof: Stock lasts stock divided by weekly use: 100 jars divided by 40 a week is 2 and a half weeks. Reordering at 20 jars leaves half a week of cover.

Science proof: A reorder point works because deliveries take time: ordering when stock is low but not empty means the new stock arrives before the old stock runs out, and the shelf is never empty on market day.

Runnable demo program
stock, weekly_use = 100, 40
print(f"{stock} jars at {weekly_use} a week last {stock / weekly_use} weeks")

Output:

100 jars at 40 a week last 2.5 weeks

Reinvesting Profits

JAH-BBOX-000021 — Reinvesting profit means putting part of what the business earned back into the business, instead of taking it all out. The stall earns 80 dollars profit a week and reinvests half, 40 dollars, in new jars and a bigger sign.

Math proof: Half of 80 dollars is 40 dollars: 80 divided by 2 is 40. After 5 weeks, 5 times 40 is 200 dollars reinvested.

Science proof: Reinvestment works like compound interest in a garden: money put back into the business grows the next harvest, and each bigger harvest leaves more to put back.

Runnable demo program

Reinvest 40 dollars a week; after 5 weeks: 200 dollars

Math proof: A year of weeks is 52 weeks: 40 jars a week times 52 weeks is 2,080 jars. Profit of 80 dollars a week times 52 is 4,160 dollars.

Science proof: A yearly review works because a full year holds every season: a single good month can mislead, but twelve months show the true pattern, including the slow winter weeks.
