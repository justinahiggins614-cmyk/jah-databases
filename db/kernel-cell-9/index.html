JAH-KC9-000129 — Cell 9 (bottom-right) in the Sobel Gx kernel

Cell 9 sits at the bottom-right of the 3-by-3 Sobel Gx kernel, and its value is 1. The Gx kernel estimates the horizontal brightness gradient -- how fast the image gets brighter or darker moving left to right -- while smoothing vertically to calm noise. This cell weights the pixel one row below and one column to the right of the pixel being processed: whatever brightness that neighbor holds is multiplied by 1 and added into the gradient estimate. Because the value is positive, a bright pixel at this position pushes the response upward -- it votes for 'brighter on the right'. As a corner cell it sits on a diagonal neighbor, so it takes part in both the horizontal and the vertical estimates -- corners are the only cells that work both jobs.

The executed demo below convolves the full Sobel Gx kernel over a synthetic vertical step edge (dark 10 on the left, bright 200 on the right) and isolates exactly what this single cell contributes at the edge pixel: 200 out of a total response of 760 (26.3 percent of the edge signal comes through this one cell).

JAH-KC9-000131 — Cell 9 (bottom-right) in the Prewitt kernels

In the Prewitt operator (1970) the bottom-right cell (cell 9) holds 1 in Gx and 1 in Gy. Prewitt is Sobel's plainer sibling: where Sobel doubles the middle row's smoothing weight to 2, Prewitt trusts every row equally with weight 1, so its kernels are uniform. That makes Prewitt simpler and cheaper, but a little more nervous around noise -- the center row gets no extra vote. As a corner cell it sits on a diagonal neighbor, so it takes part in both the horizontal and the vertical estimates -- corners are the only cells that work both jobs.

The executed demo runs both Prewitt kernels over the step edges. In Gx this cell contributes 200 out of a total 570; in Gy it contributes 200 out of 570. Compare with Sobel's 760: Prewitt's edge answer is weaker by exactly the smoothing-weight ratio, because every Prewitt row carries weight 1 where Sobel's middle row carries 2.

JAH-KC9-000132 — Cell 9 (bottom-right) in the Scharr kernels

In the Scharr operator (2000) the bottom-right cell (cell 9) holds 3 in Gx and 3 in Gy. Scharr kept Sobel's shape but re-optimized the numbers for rotational symmetry: its gradient direction estimates stay truer as an edge rotates, which is why libraries reach for Scharr when the angle of the edge matters, not just its strength. The price is louder numbers -- 3 and 10 where Sobel uses 1 and 2 -- so Scharr's raw responses run about four times hotter. As a corner cell it sits on a diagonal neighbor, so it takes part in both the horizontal and the vertical estimates -- corners are the only cells that work both jobs.

The executed demo runs both Scharr kernels over the step edges. In Gx this cell contributes 600 out of a total 3040; in Gy it contributes 600 out of 3040. Against Sobel's 760, Scharr answers 3040: the ratio is exactly 4, the ratio of the positive-side weight sums (3+10+3 = 16 versus 1+2+1 = 4).

JAH-KC9-000133 — Cell 9: horizontal-edge vs vertical-edge response

Cell 9 (bottom-right) does not treat every edge orientation equally. Gx is the vertical-edge finder (it reacts when brightness changes left to right) and Gy is the horizontal-edge finder (it reacts when brightness changes top to bottom). A cell with a nonzero Gx weight takes part in finding vertical edges; a cell with a nonzero Gy weight takes part in finding horizontal ones. Measured at the edge pixel, this cell contributes Gx-on-vertical-edge 200, Gy-on-vertical-edge 200, Gx-on-horizontal-edge 200, and Gy-on-horizontal-edge 200. In plain terms: it sees vertical edges and it sees horizontal edges.

The executed demo prints exactly that four-way table for this cell. Notice the pattern across the grid: the middle column (cells 2, 5, 8) carries Gx weight 0, so it never helps find vertical edges, and the middle row (cells 4, 5, 6) carries Gy weight 0, so it never helps find horizontal edges. Only the four corner cells work both jobs -- one reason corners matter more than their size suggests.

JAH-KC9-000134 — Cell 9: sensitivity experiment -- varying the cell

What happens if cell 9 (bottom-right) is not 1? The sensitivity experiment varies just this one cell of Sobel Gx through -1, 0, 1, 2, 3 and measures two things at the edge pixel: the response on the vertical step edge, and the response on a perfectly flat field (brightness 50 everywhere, no edge at all).

Two real, measured facts come out. First, the edge response moves linearly with the cell: every +1 added to the cell shifts the edge answer by 200, the brightness of the pixel this cell weights -- the cell's influence is exactly its weight times its pixel, no more, no less. Second, touching the cell breaks the kernel's zero-sum balance: on the flat field, where the true answer is 0, the response becomes 50 times the cell's deviation from its designed value. A single changed cell makes empty sky report edges. That is why kernel design is conservative: the numbers are a balanced set, and each one earns its place by keeping flat regions quiet while letting real edges shout. JAH-KC9-000135 — Cell 9: smoothing weight vs differencing weight

Every one of these 3x3 kernels is secretly two 1-D filters multiplied together: a smoothing filter down the rows times a differencing filter across the columns. That is why cell 9 (bottom-right) always factors as smoothing[2] x differencing[2]: sobel 1x1=1; prewitt 1x1=1; scharr 3x1=3. For Gy the idea is transposed: the cell is differencing[row] x smoothing[column].

This factorization is the famous 'trust' interpretation: the smoothing number is somebody's opinion about how much you ought to trust the row of pixels you happen to be standing on. Sobel says the middle row deserves double trust (2); Prewitt says trust every row equally (1); Scharr says the middle row deserves the most trust of all (10, with 3 for the outer rows). Cell 9's own values across the three operators are just that opinion, multiplied by the differencing sign of its column. The executed demo verifies the outer-product decomposition numerically for all three Gx kernels and prints this cell's factorization in each.

# Nine-number kernel lab -- pure-Python 3x3 convolution, zero dependencies.
# Images are 2-D brightness lists; the demo convolves the interior 3x3.
SOBEL_GX = [[-1,0,1],[-2,0,2],[-1,0,1]]
SOBEL_GY = [[-1,-2,-1],[0,0,0],[1,2,1]]
PREWITT_GX = [[-1,0,1],[-1,0,1],[-1,0,1]]
PREWITT_GY = [[-1,-1,-1],[0,0,0],[1,1,1]]
SCHARR_GX = [[-3,0,3],[-10,0,10],[-3,0,3]]
SCHARR_GY = [[-3,-10,-3],[0,0,0],[3,10,3]]
V = [[10,10,10,200,200] for _ in range(5)]   # vertical step edge: dark left, bright right
H = [[10]*5,[10]*5,[10]*5,[200]*5,[200]*5]   # horizontal step edge: dark top, bright bottom
F = [[50]*5 for _ in range(5)]               # flat field: no edges at all
D = [[10,10,10,10,10],[10,10,10,10,200],[10,10,10,200,200],[10,10,200,200,200],[10,200,200,200,200]]  # diagonal edge
def conv(img, k):
    return [[sum(img[y+dy-1][x+dx-1]*k[dy][dx] for dy in range(3) for dx in range(3))
             for x in range(1,4)] for y in range(1,4)]
def show(m):
    for row in m:
        print(" ".join("%8g" % v for v in row))
r, c = 2, 2
k = SOBEL_GX
out = conv(V, k)
print("Sobel Gx convolved over the vertical step edge (interior 3x3 output):")
show(out)
touch = V[1+r][1+c]
contrib = touch * k[r][c]
print("cell value:", k[r][c], "| brightness of the pixel it weights:", touch)
print("this cell contribution at output (2,2):", contrib, "of total", out[1][1])
print("share of edge signal: %.1f %%" % (100.0*contrib/out[1][1]))
