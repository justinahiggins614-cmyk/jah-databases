The design of the Golden Gate Bridge, in plain words, element by element.

The L.L. Design: An Overview

The Golden Gate Bridge design as a whole, in plain words.

What is the design of the Golden Gate Bridge, and how do its parts work together? The bridge is a suspension bridge, which means its roadway hangs from cables that are draped over tall towers and anchored into the ground at each end. This record gives the whole design in plain words: the two towers, the two main cables, the anchorages that hold the pull, the deck that carries the road, the stiffening truss that calms the wind, and the color that makes the bridge visible in fog. Every fact here is documented in the public record.

The Suspension Principle: Two Cables, Two Towers

The suspension principle: how two cables and two towers hold up a roadway.

How does a suspension bridge hold up a road in the air? The answer is the suspension principle, which is simple enough to state in one sentence: the roadway hangs from cables, the cables rest on towers, and the towers stand on the ground while the cable ends are anchored into the ground. This record explains that principle in plain words, with the Golden Gate Bridge as the worked example: two main cables, two towers, and a deck hung between them by vertical cables called suspenders.

The Towers as Signature Forms

The two towers as architectural forms, designed by Irving F. Morrow.

Why do the towers of the Golden Gate Bridge look the way they do? The towers are not plain steel posts: they are stepped, set back in stages as they rise, with Art Deco styling, lighting, and railings designed by consulting architect Irving F. Morrow. This record describes the towers as forms, which means their shape, their height, and the way they were designed to be seen, in plain words, with the documented facts and the architect's name beside them.

The Main Cable: Geometry and Load Path

The main cable: its curve, its wires, and the path the load takes.

What is the main cable, and how does it carry the bridge? Each of the two main cables is a bundle of steel wires spun in place over the towers, sagging in a curve called a catenary, which is the curve a hanging cable makes under its own weight. This record explains the cable's geometry, which means its shape and size, and its load path, which means the route the weight of the bridge takes through the cable to the towers and anchorages.

The Anchorages: Holding the Pull

The anchorages: the concrete blocks that hold the pull of the cables.

What holds the cables from pulling the towers over? The answer is the anchorages, which are huge concrete blocks at each end of the bridge that the main cables are fastened into. This record explains the anchorages in plain words: what they are, how they hold the pull, and why a suspension bridge cannot stand without them. An anchorage is a mass of concrete and steel that resists the cable's pull by its own weight and by its grip on the ground.

The Deck: A Roadway in the Air

The deck: a roadway hung in the air, 90 feet wide.

What is the deck of a suspension bridge, and what does it carry? The deck is the roadway itself: the flat structure that carries the traffic, hung from the main cables by suspenders. This record describes the deck in plain words: its width, its lanes, its sidewalks, and how it was changed over the years, including the replacement of its original concrete surface with a lighter steel deck in the 1980s.

The Stiffening Truss: Calm in the Wind

The stiffening truss: the structure under the deck that calms the wind.

Why does the deck of a suspension bridge not wave in the wind like a rope bridge? The answer is the stiffening truss, which is a deep framework of steel under the deck that makes the deck rigid, so that wind and traffic cannot bend it far. This record explains the stiffening truss in plain words, with the deflection theory of Leon S. Moisseiff, which is the mathematics of how a suspension bridge bends under load and how the truss controls that bending.

International Orange as Signature Color

International Orange: the color of the bridge, chosen by Irving F. Morrow.

Why is the Golden Gate Bridge orange? The color is called International Orange, and it began as the orange-red primer, which is the first coat of paint put on steel to protect it, that covered the steel when it arrived on site. Consulting architect Irving F. Morrow saw the primer color in the fog and chose it as the bridge's permanent color, because it is visible in fog and it suits the hills and the sea. This record tells that story with the documented facts.

The bridge is made of steel, which is iron with a small amount of carbon added to make it stronger, and the steel for this bridge was fabricated, which means cut and shaped into parts, by Bethlehem Steel at five plants, then shipped to the site and erected, which means lifted into place, by the McClintic-Marshall Construction Company. This record follows the steel from the mill to the tower leg.

The net cost 130,000 dollars and saved nineteen men, who called themselves the Half Way to Hell Club. The L.L. Design: An Overview

A suspension bridge works by tension and compression, two plain ideas. Tension is a pulling force: the main cables are pulled tight by the weight of the deck, and they pull back. Compression is a pushing force: the towers are pushed down by the cables passing over them, and the ground pushes back up. The deck hangs from the cables by suspenders, so the deck's weight becomes tension in the cables, compression in the towers, and a pull that the anchorages must hold. Nothing in the design is magic; it is a chain of forces, each part doing one job. The Stiffening Truss: Calm in the Wind

A truss is a framework of triangles: each member is either pulled (tension) or pushed (compression), and triangles do not change shape easily, so the whole framework stays rigid. The wind load, which is the push of the wind, and the live load, which is the weight of the traffic, both try to bend the deck; the truss answers by sharing each push among many members at once.
