What Mathematical Logic Is

ID: JAH-MTLO-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-mathematical-logic#JAH-MTLO-000001 k=63ee789c

Definition: Mathematical logic is the study of reasoning itself: what makes an argument valid, what can be proved, and what can be computed. It has four parts: proof theory, which studies proofs; model theory, which studies what is true in a structure; computability theory, which studies what can be computed; and set theory, which studies the foundations. Logic is the foundation of mathematics, and the theory of computing.

History: Logic began with Aristotle's syllogisms in the 4th century BCE, the first systematic study of valid argument. It was revived by the Stoics, developed by medieval logicians, and transformed by Boole in 1854, who showed that logic is algebra, and by Frege in 1879, who introduced quantifiers. In the 20th century, logic became mathematical: Russell and Whitehead tried to derive mathematics from logic, Gödel proved that no system can prove its own consistency, and Turing defined computation. Today logic is the foundation of computer science, and it is used in verification, in databases, and in artificial intelligence.

Key concepts: The key ideas are proposition, proof, model, computability, set theory. A proposition is a statement that is true or false, but not both. 2 + 2 = 4 is true; 2 + 2 = 5 is false. A proof is a chain of valid steps from the axioms to the theorem: each step follows by a rule of inference. A model is a structure in which a set of sentences is true: the natural numbers are a model of the axioms of arithmetic. Computability is the study of what can be computed: the halting problem is undecidable, and no program can solve it. Set theory is the study of sets: the foundation of mathematics, in which every object is a set, and every theorem is a theorem about sets. Logical Connectives

ID: JAH-MTLO-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-mathematical-logic#JAH-MTLO-000003 k=80db2474

Definition: The logical connectives are AND, OR, NOT, IF-THEN, and IF-AND-ONLY-IF: they combine propositions into compound ones, and their meaning is given by truth tables. Quantifiers

ID: JAH-MTLO-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-mathematical-logic#JAH-MTLO-000004 k=2b593eaa

Definition: Quantifiers say how many: for all (∀) says that a statement holds for every object, and there exists (∃) says that it holds for at least one. The statement ∀x (x + 0 = x) says that adding zero changes nothing, for every number; and ∃x (x^2 = 2) says that there is a number whose square is 2. Quantifiers are the second ingredient of logic, after the connectives, and they are what make first-order logic powerful.

History: Quantifiers were introduced by Frege in 1879, in his Begriffsschrift, which founded modern logic: before Frege, logic could not express for all and there exists. In the 20th century, quantifiers became the standard tool of mathematics: every definition and theorem is written with quantifiers, and the order of quantifiers decides the meaning. In the 20th century, the quantifiers became the standard tool of mathematics, and the order of quantifiers was recognised as decisive. Today quantifiers are taught as the second ingredient of logic, and they are the difference between propositional logic and first-order logic.

Key concepts: The key ideas are universal quantifier, existential quantifier, scope, bound variable, order of quantifiers. The universal quantifier ∀ says for all: ∀x P(x) means that P holds for every x. ∀x (x + 0 = x) says that adding zero changes nothing. The existential quantifier ∃ says there exists: ∃x P(x) means that P holds for at least one x. ∃x (x^2 = 2) says that there is a number whose square is 2. The scope of a quantifier is the part of the formula it applies to: in ∀x (P(x) → Q(x)), the scope is (P(x) → Q(x)). A bound variable is one that is quantified: in ∀x P(x), x is bound, and the formula does not depend on x. A free variable is one that is not quantified. The order of quantifiers matters: ∀x ∃y (x < y) says that for every x there is a larger y, which is true, while ∃y ∀x (x < y) says that there is a y larger than every x, which is false. Worked examples: The statement ∀x (x + 0 = x) is true in the natural numbers: adding zero changes nothing, for every number. ID: JAH-MTLO-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-mathematical-logic#JAH-MTLO-000006 k=315b93ae

Definition: A rule of inference is a rule for deriving a conclusion from premises: modus ponens says that from p and p → q, infer q; modus tollens says that from not q and p → q, infer not p; and universal instantiation says that from ∀x P(x), infer P(a) for any a.
