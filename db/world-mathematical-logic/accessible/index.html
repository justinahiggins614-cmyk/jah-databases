What Mathematical Logic Is

Mathematical logic is the study of reasoning itself: what makes an argument valid, what can be proved, and what can be computed. It has four parts: proof theory, which studies proofs; model theory, which studies what is true in a structure; computability theory, which studies what can be computed; and set theory, which studies the foundations. Logic is the foundation of mathematics, and the theory of computing.

The argument All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal is valid: in every model where the premises are true, the conclusion is true. The argument All men are mortal; Socrates is mortal; therefore Socrates is a man is invalid: there is a model — Socrates the cat, who is mortal but not a man — where the premises are true and the conclusion is false. The statement 2 + 2 = 4 is provable from the axioms of arithmetic, and it is true in the standard model. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that any consistent system strong enough for arithmetic has true statements it cannot prove.

Propositions and Truth Tables

A proposition is a statement that is either true or false. The connectives AND, OR, NOT, IF-THEN, and IF-AND-ONLY-IF combine propositions into compound ones, and a truth table lists every combination of truth values and the result. A tautology is true in every row; a contradiction is false in every row; and a contingency is true in some rows and false in others. Truth tables are the first tool of logic.

The proposition p and q, with p true and q false, is false, because a conjunction needs both parts true. The implication p → q with p true and q false is false; in the other three rows it is true. De Morgan's law says not (p and q) is equivalent to (not p) or (not q): if p is true and q is false, then not (p and q) is true, and (not p) or (not q) is false or true, which is true. The truth table for p → q has 4 rows, and for three variables it has 8 rows. The proposition p or not p is a tautology: it is true in both rows.

Logical Connectives

The logical connectives are AND, OR, NOT, IF-THEN, and IF-AND-ONLY-IF: they combine propositions into compound ones, and their meaning is given by truth tables. AND is true when both parts are true; OR is true when at least one is true; NOT flips the value; IF-THEN is false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent false; and IF-AND-ONLY-IF is true when both parts have the same value. The connectives are the operations of logic, and they are the operations of digital circuits.

The conjunction p and q is true only when both are true: with p true and q false, it is false. The disjunction p or q is true when at least one is true: with p true and q false, it is true. The negation of true is false, and of false is true. The implication p → q is false only when p is true and q is false: with p false, it is true, whatever q is. The biconditional p ↔ q is true when p and q agree: with both true, it is true; with p true and q false, it is false. De Morgan's law says not (p and q) is (not p) or (not q).

Quantifiers

Quantifiers say how many: for all (∀) says that a statement holds for every object, and there exists (∃) says that it holds for at least one. The statement ∀x (x + 0 = x) says that adding zero changes nothing, for every number; and ∃x (x^2 = 2) says that there is a number whose square is 2. Quantifiers are the second ingredient of logic, after the connectives, and they are what make first-order logic powerful.

The statement ∀x (x + 0 = x) is true in the natural numbers: adding zero changes nothing, for every number. The statement ∃x (x^2 = 2) is true in the real numbers, because sqrt(2) is real, but false in the rational numbers, because no rational squares to 2. The statement ∀x ∃y (x < y) is true in the natural numbers: for every x, y = x + 1 is larger. The statement ∃y ∀x (x < y) is false in the natural numbers: there is no largest number. The negation of ∀x P(x) is ∃x not P(x): to disprove a for-all statement, find one counterexample.

Predicates

A predicate is a property or a relation: a function from objects to true or false. Is-even(x) is a predicate on numbers: it is true for 2 and false for 3. Less-than(x, y) is a predicate on pairs: it is true for (1, 2) and false for (2, 1). Predicates, combined with quantifiers and connectives, are the language of first-order logic: every mathematical statement is a predicate applied to objects, quantified and connected.

The predicate Is-even(x) is true for x = 2, 4, and 6, and false for x = 1, 3, and 5. The predicate Less-than(x, y) is true for (1, 2) and (2, 3), and false for (2, 1) and (3, 3). The formula ∀x (Is-even(x) → ∃y (x = 2y)) says that every even number is twice some number: it is true in the natural numbers. The formula ∃x (Is-even(x) and Is-odd(x)) is false: no number is both even and odd. A database table is a predicate: the table Employees(x) is true for the employees, and false for everyone else.

Rules of Inference

A rule of inference is a rule for deriving a conclusion from premises: modus ponens says that from p and p → q, infer q; modus tollens says that from not q and p → q, infer not p; and universal instantiation says that from ∀x P(x), infer P(a) for any a. Rules of inference are the steps of a proof: a proof is a sequence of formulas, each justified by a rule, from the premises to the conclusion.

From the premises p and p → q, modus ponens gives q. From the premises not q and p → q, modus tollens gives not p. From ∀x (x + 0 = x), universal instantiation gives 5 + 0 = 5. From P(2), existential generalisation gives ∃x P(x). The proof that Socrates is mortal: from ∀x (Man(x) → Mortal(x)) and Man(Socrates), universal instantiation gives Man(Socrates) → Mortal(Socrates), and modus ponens gives Mortal(Socrates). The proof has two steps: universal instantiation, then modus ponens, and each step is justified by a rule.

Proof by Contradiction

Proof by contradiction proves a statement by assuming its negation and deriving a contradiction: if assuming not P leads to a falsehood, then P must be true. It is the method of the indirect proof, and it is one of the oldest and most powerful methods in mathematics. The classic example is the proof that sqrt(2) is irrational: assume it is rational, and derive that a fraction is in lowest terms and not in lowest terms at the same time.

The proof that sqrt(2) is irrational: assume sqrt(2) = a/b, with a and b integers in lowest terms. Then 2 = a^2/b^2, so a^2 = 2b^2, and a^2 is even, so a is even, say a = 2c. Then 4c^2 = 2b^2, so b^2 = 2c^2, and b is even too. But a and b were in lowest terms, so they cannot both be even: a contradiction. So the assumption is false, and sqrt(2) is irrational. Euclid's proof that there are infinitely many primes: assume there are finitely many, multiply them all and add 1, and the result has a prime factor not in the list: a contradiction.

Proof by Induction

Proof by induction proves a statement for all natural numbers: prove it for n = 1, then prove that if it holds for n, it holds for n + 1. The base case and the inductive step together prove the statement for all n. Induction is the method of the natural numbers: it is one of Peano's axioms, and it is the most used proof method in discrete mathematics.

The sum 1 + 2 + ... + n equals n(n+1)/2. The base case n = 1 gives 1 = 1 times 2/2, which is true. The inductive step assumes 1 + ... + n = n(n+1)/2, and adds n + 1 to both sides: the left becomes 1 + ... + (n+1), and the right becomes n(n+1)/2 + (n+1) = (n+1)(n+2)/2, which is the formula for n + 1. So the formula holds for all n. The Fibonacci sequence is defined recursively, and its properties are proved by strong induction, because F(n) depends on F(n-1) and F(n-2). The classic false proof that all horses are the same colour fails in the step from n = 1 to n = 2.

Set Theory

Set theory is the study of sets: collections of objects, with the operations of union, intersection, and complement. It is the foundation of mathematics: every mathematical object is a set, and every theorem is a theorem about sets. The axioms of Zermelo-Fraenkel, with the axiom of choice, are the standard foundation, and they are the setting in which all of mathematics is done.

The set {1, 2, 3} has 3 elements and 2^3 = 8 subsets: the empty set, {1}, {2}, {3}, {1, 2}, {1, 3}, {2, 3}, and {1, 2, 3}. The natural numbers have cardinality aleph-null, and the real numbers have a larger cardinality: Cantor's diagonal argument shows that the reals are uncountable. Russell's paradox is the set R of all sets that do not contain themselves: if R contains itself, then it does not, by its definition, and if it does not, then it does, by its definition: a contradiction. The axiom of choice is needed to prove that every vector space has a basis, and that every set can be well-ordered.

Axiomatic Set Theory

Axiomatic set theory is set theory done from axioms: the Zermelo-Fraenkel axioms, with the axiom of choice, are the standard foundation of mathematics. The axioms say which sets exist: the empty set exists, the union of two sets exists, the power set exists, and the infinite set exists. Every theorem of mathematics is proved from these axioms, and the consistency of the axioms cannot be proved from within, by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem.

The natural numbers are built from the empty set: 0 is the empty set, 1 is {0}, 2 is {0, 1}, and n + 1 is n union {n}. The integers are built from the naturals as equivalence classes of pairs, and the rationals from the integers, and the reals from the rationals as Dedekind cuts. The power set of a set with n elements has 2^n elements: the power set of {1, 2, 3} has 8 elements. The continuum hypothesis says that there is no cardinality between aleph-null and the cardinality of the reals: it is independent of ZFC, by Gödel and Cohen.

The Axiom of Choice

The axiom of choice says that for any collection of non-empty sets, there is a function choosing one element from each. It is independent of the other axioms of set theory: it can neither be proved nor disproved from them, by Gödel and Cohen. It is needed for many theorems — every vector space has a basis, every set can be well-ordered — and it has surprising consequences, like the Banach-Tarski paradox, which says that a ball can be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls of the same size.

The axiom of choice is needed to prove that every vector space has a basis: the basis is chosen from the vectors, and the choice needs the axiom when the space is infinite-dimensional. It is needed to prove that every set can be well-ordered: the well-ordering is built by choosing elements one by one. Zorn's lemma, which is equivalent to the axiom, is used to prove that every ring has a maximal ideal, and that every field has an algebraic closure. The Banach-Tarski paradox says that a ball of volume 1 can be cut into 5 pieces and reassembled into two balls of volume 1: the pieces are non-measurable, and the paradox uses the axiom of choice.

Cardinality

Cardinality is the size of a set: the number of elements, for a finite set, and the infinite cardinal, for an infinite set. Two sets have the same cardinality when there is a bijection between them. The natural numbers have cardinality aleph-null, and the real numbers have a larger cardinality: Cantor's diagonal argument shows that the reals are uncountable. Cardinality is the theory of infinite sizes, and it is the most surprising part of set theory.

The natural numbers and the even numbers have the same cardinality: the bijection n -> 2n pairs each natural with an even number. The integers are countable: the bijection lists 0, 1, -1, 2, -2, and so on. The rationals are countable: they can be listed by the diagonals of the grid of fractions. The real numbers are uncountable: Cantor's diagonal argument assumes a listing of the reals between 0 and 1, and builds a real whose nth digit differs from the nth number's nth digit, so it is not in the listing. The power set of the naturals has a larger cardinality than the naturals: Cantor's theorem says that the power set is always larger.

Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems

Gödel's incompleteness theorems say that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic is incomplete: there are true statements it cannot prove, and it cannot prove its own consistency. The first theorem builds a statement that says I am not provable, which is true exactly when it is not provable. The second theorem says that the consistency of the system cannot be proved within the system. The theorems are the most important results in logic, and they limit what mathematics can do.

Peano arithmetic is a formal system for the natural numbers: its axioms are the Peano axioms, and its rules are the rules of first-order logic. The Gödel sentence for Peano arithmetic says I am not provable in Peano arithmetic: it is true, because it is not provable, and it is not provable, because it is true. The second theorem says that Peano arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency: the statement Con(PA), which says that Peano arithmetic is consistent, is not provable in Peano arithmetic. The theorems apply to any system strong enough for arithmetic: ZFC, the foundation of mathematics, is also incomplete, and it cannot prove its own consistency.

The Halting Problem

The halting problem asks whether a given program, on a given input, will stop or run forever. Turing proved in 1936 that there is no program that can answer it for every program and input: the halting problem is undecidable. The proof is by diagonalisation: assume a program H decides halting, build a program that does the opposite of what H says, and derive a contradiction. The halting problem is the most famous undecidable problem, and it is the limit of computation.

A program that adds two numbers halts: it finishes in a few steps. A program that loops forever, while True: pass, does not halt: it runs forever. The halting problem asks for a program that can tell, for every program and input, whether it halts: Turing proved that no such program exists. The proof is by diagonalisation: the program D does the opposite of what H says, and running D on itself gives a contradiction either way. Every undecidable problem is proved undecidable by reducing it to the halting problem: if the problem could be solved, then halting could be solved too.

Computability

Computability is the study of what can be computed: the functions that a program can compute, and the problems that a program can solve. A function is computable when there is a program that computes it; a problem is decidable when there is a program that solves it for every input. The Church-Turing thesis says that the computable functions are exactly the ones computable by a Turing machine, and it is the foundation of computer science.

The function that adds two numbers is computable: a program adds them. The problem of whether a number is prime is decidable: a program tests the divisors. The halting problem is undecidable: no program can decide, for every program and input, whether it halts. The problem of whether a program prints hello is undecidable: it reduces to the halting problem. The Church-Turing thesis says that every computable function is computable by a Turing machine: the thesis is supported by the fact that every model of computation — the lambda calculus, the recursive functions, and the Turing machine — computes the same functions.

Turing Machines

A Turing machine is the model of a computer: an infinite tape, a head that reads and writes, and a table of rules that says what to do in each state. It is the definition of computation: a function is computable when a Turing machine computes it, and a problem is decidable when a Turing machine decides it. The Turing machine is the simplest model of a computer, and it is the most powerful: it can compute anything a real computer can, given enough time and tape.

A Turing machine that adds 1 to a binary number: it moves right to the end, then moves left, flipping 1s to 0s and 0s to 1s, until it finds a 0 to flip to 1, or the start. Adding 1 to 1011 (11) gives 1100 (12): the machine flips the last two 1s to 0s, and the 0 to 1. A universal Turing machine reads the description of another machine and its input, and simulates it: it is a program that runs programs. The busy beaver function is the maximum number of steps a Turing machine with n states can take before halting: for n = 5, it is 47,176,870, and for n = 6, it is more than 10^36,000.

Model Theory

Model theory is the study of the relationship between a formal language and the structures it describes: a model is a structure in which a set of sentences is true, and the theory is the set of sentences true in the model. The completeness theorem says that a sentence is provable exactly when it is true in every model. Model theory is the semantics of logic: it is what truth means, and it is the study of the structures of mathematics.

The natural numbers are a model of Peano arithmetic: the axioms are true in them. The real numbers are a model of the axioms of a complete ordered field: the axioms are true in them. The completeness theorem says that a sentence is provable from the axioms exactly when it is true in every model: for first-order logic, provability and truth coincide. The compactness theorem says that if every finite subset of a theory has a model, then the whole theory has one: it is used to build non-standard models, like the hyperreals, which have infinitesimals. The hyperreals are elementarily equivalent to the reals: they satisfy the same first-order sentences, but they have infinitesimals, which the reals do not.

Proof Theory

Proof theory is the study of proofs themselves: what a proof is, what rules it follows, and what it can do. A proof is a sequence of formulas, each justified by a rule, from the axioms to the theorem. Proof theory studies the structure of proofs: cut elimination, which says that a proof can be transformed into one without cuts; and the Curry-Howard correspondence, which says that proofs are programs and propositions are types. Proof theory is the syntax of logic: it is what a proof is.

A proof of p → p: assume p, then p, so p → p by implication introduction. Cut elimination says that this proof can be transformed into one without cuts: the direct proof. The Curry-Howard correspondence says that the proof of p → p is the program that takes a value of type p and returns it: the identity function. Gentzen proved the consistency of Peano arithmetic in 1936, by cut elimination and ordinal analysis: the proof uses induction up to epsilon-naught. A proof assistant checks a proof by applying the rules: the Coq system has checked the four-colour theorem and the Feit-Thompson theorem.

The Paradoxes: Russell and Others

A paradox is a statement that seems true but leads to a contradiction, or a proof that seems valid but gives a false conclusion. Russell's paradox is the set of all sets that do not contain themselves: it both contains itself and does not. The liar paradox is the sentence This sentence is false: if it is true, it is false, and if it is false, it is true. Paradoxes are the questions of logic: they show the limits of naive reasoning, and they are resolved by distinguishing the levels.

Russell's paradox: let R be the set of all sets that do not contain themselves. If R contains itself, then by its definition it does not contain itself. If R does not contain itself, then by its definition it does contain itself. So R both contains itself and does not: a contradiction. The resolution is the axioms: not every property defines a set, and R is not a set. The liar paradox: This sentence is false. If it is true, then what it says is true, so it is false. If it is false, then what it says is false, so it is true. The resolution is Tarski's hierarchy: truth is defined in the metalanguage, and the liar sentence is not a sentence of the object language.

Intuitionism and Constructivism

Intuitionism is the philosophy that mathematics is a mental construction: a statement is true only when it is proved, and a proof must construct the object it claims to exist. It rejects the law of the excluded middle and proof by contradiction: to prove that something exists, you must construct it. Constructivism is the mathematics built on this philosophy: it is the mathematics of algorithms, and it is the mathematics of computer science.

The classical proof that there are irrational numbers a and b with a^b rational: let a = b = sqrt(2). If sqrt(2)^sqrt(2) is rational, we are done; if it is irrational, let a = sqrt(2)^sqrt(2) and b = sqrt(2), and then a^b = sqrt(2)^2 = 2, which is rational. The proof does not say which case holds, so it is not constructive: it does not construct the pair. A constructive proof would give the pair explicitly: for example, a = sqrt(2) and b = 2 log_2(3), or a simpler pair. The intuitionist rejects the classical proof, because it uses the excluded middle on an undecided statement.

Logic in Computer Science

Logic in computer science is the use of logic to design, verify, and reason about programs: a program is a proof, a type is a proposition, and a specification is a formula. Logic is used in verification, where a program is proved correct; in databases, where a query is a formula; in artificial intelligence, where knowledge is logic; and in programming languages, where the type system is a logic. Computer science is applied logic.

A program that sorts a list: the specification is that the output is a permutation of the input, and it is sorted. Hoare logic proves it: the loop invariant is that the prefix is sorted, and the postcondition is that the whole list is sorted. A database query SELECT * FROM Employees WHERE salary > 50000 is a formula: the answer is the rows that satisfy it. A SAT solver decides whether a formula with a million variables is satisfiable: it is used to verify hardware, and it solves problems that were impossible 20 years ago. The Coq proof assistant checked the four-colour theorem: the proof is 60,000 lines, and it was checked by a computer.

Formal Verification

Formal verification is the proving of a program or a system correct: the specification is written as a formula, the program is written as a proof, and the proof is checked by a computer. It is the most rigorous form of testing: instead of testing some inputs, it proves the program correct for all inputs. Formal verification is used for the systems that must not fail: the flight controller, the medical device, and the cryptographic protocol.

The CompCert compiler is a C compiler proved correct: the proof is 100,000 lines, and it guarantees that the compiled code behaves as the source code specifies. The four-colour theorem was proved by Appel and Haken in 1976, with computer assistance, and it was checked by Coq in 2005: the proof is 60,000 lines, and it was checked by a computer. Model checking verifies a hardware design by exploring its states: a design with a million states is checked in minutes, and the checker finds the bugs that testing misses. The seL4 microkernel was verified in 2009: it is the first operating system kernel proved correct, with 10,000 lines of code and 200,000 lines of proof.

A History of Mathematical Logic

Mathematical logic has no single founder: it is the coming together of Aristotle's syllogisms, Boole's algebra, Frege's quantifiers, and the computability of Turing. The subject took its modern form in the 20th century, when logic became mathematical: Gödel's theorems, Turing's machine, and the foundations of set theory. Today logic is the foundation of computer science, and it is the study of reasoning itself.

The timeline: Aristotle, 4th century BCE; Boole, 1854; Frege, 1879; Russell and Whitehead, 1910; Gödel, 1931; Turing, 1936; Curry-Howard, 1960s; Coq and the four-colour theorem, 2005. The span from Aristotle to Turing is about 2,300 years. The span from Aristotle to Coq is about 2,300 years, and the subject's growth is the story of logic becoming mathematical. The subject's parts are old, but the subject itself is new: it was the 20th century that made logic mathematical, and it is the computer that keeps it growing, through verification and artificial intelligence.

Logic in the Modern World

Logic in the modern world is everywhere: the search engine, the database, the proof assistant, and the artificial intelligence are all logic. The modern world runs on logic that is computed, not just argued: the queries are formulas, the programs are proofs, and the reasoning is deduction. Logic is the invisible infrastructure of modern life, and it is the first mathematics that a computer scientist learns.

A search engine answers a query in a fraction of a second: it looks up the words in an index, ranks the pages, and returns the top ten. A database answers a query by evaluating a formula over the rows: the answer is the rows that satisfy it, and the query is optimised by the logic. A proof assistant checks a proof of 60,000 lines in minutes: the four-colour theorem was checked by Coq in 2005. An artificial intelligence answers a question by reasoning in logic: the knowledge is a set of formulas, and the answer is deduced from them. A flight controller is verified by formal methods: the proof guarantees that it behaves as specified, for all inputs.
