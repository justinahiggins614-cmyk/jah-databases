What a Soundscape Is: Three Layers, Never Blurred JAH-SECO-000001 · simple

A soundscape is everything audible in a place over a period of time, and soundscape ecology studies it as a whole while keeping its three layers distinct. Biophony is the sounds living organisms make: animal calls, songs, and choruses. Geophony is the sounds of the earth itself: wind, rain, thunder, running water, and waves. Anthrophony is the sounds humans make: traffic, machinery, aircraft, music, and voices. The layers are cross-linked, because animals must communicate through geophony and anthrophony, and because each layer shapes the others, but they are never blurred into one undifferentiated noise: every record in this pair states which layer or layers it covers, so a reader can always tell a bird from the wind behind it and from the road beyond that.

Soundscape layers

biophony, geophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: the three-layer framework comes from soundscape ecology as developed by Bernie Krause and colleagues, and it is the standard vocabulary of the field, used in soundscape studies worldwide to separate biological, geophysical, and human sound sources.

Math proof

Math proof: a soundscape with biophony at 55 decibels, geophony at 50 decibels, and anthrophony at 60 decibels has a combined level of 10 x log10(10^5.5 + 10^5 + 10^6) = 61.5 decibels, dominated by anthrophony.

Source

Krause and colleagues, soundscape ecology three-layer framework (as of October 2026).

Biophony: The Living Layer JAH-SECO-000002 · simple

Biophony is the layer of sound produced by living organisms in an ecosystem: bird song at dawn, insect choruses at night, frog calls at a pond, whale song in the ocean, and the rustle and footfall of animals moving. Biophony varies by place, season, and time of day, and it carries ecological information: which species are present, whether they are breeding, and how healthy a habitat is. A rich biophony with many species calling in different frequency bands and time slots suggests a diverse community, while a silent habitat may signal disturbance, even when it looks intact. This record covers biophony alone, and it names the other layers only to keep them distinct: the wind behind the chorus is geophony, and any road noise is anthrophony.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: long-term soundscape recordings show that biophony tracks species presence and breeding activity, and that habitats with documented high biodiversity produce richer, more structured biophony than degraded habitats.

Math proof

Math proof: if 6 bird species, 4 frog species, and 10 insect species call at a site, the calling community totals 6 + 4 + 10 = 20 species.

Source

Geophony: The Earth's Own Sounds JAH-SECO-000003 · simple

Geophony is the layer of sound produced by non-living natural processes: wind moving through trees, rain on leaves, thunder rolling across a plain, streams running over rock, waves breaking on a shore, and the deep groan of moving ice. Geophony shapes the other layers: animals evolved to call in frequency bands and at times when geophony is quieter, and a soundscape's geophony tells a listener about weather, terrain, and water even with eyes closed. Geophony is also the baseline against which change is measured: a stream that stops flowing changes a valley's soundscape before any animal leaves. This record covers geophony alone, kept distinct from the biophony of animals that live in the wind and rain and from the anthrophony of human machines.

Soundscape layers

geophony

Science proof

Science proof: meteorological and hydrological studies confirm that geophony tracks weather and water flow, and soundscape recordings show that animal calling shifts in timing and frequency to avoid the loudest geophony, such as wind and surf.

Math proof

Math proof: a stream sound at 45 decibels and wind at 48 decibels combine to 10 x log10(10^4.5 + 10^4.8) = 49.8 decibels.

Source

Anthrophony: The Human-Made Layer JAH-SECO-000004 · simple

Anthrophony is the layer of sound humans make: road traffic, aircraft, construction machinery, sirens, music, and voices, including the low-frequency rumble of engines and the broadband roar of highways. Anthrophony is the youngest layer in evolutionary terms, and it is also the loudest in many modern soundscapes, masking animal calls, disrupting breeding choruses, and changing how animals use a habitat. Unlike geophony, anthrophony follows human schedules: rush hours, flight paths, and work shifts, so its pattern in a soundscape reveals human activity as clearly as biophony reveals animal activity. This record covers anthrophony alone, kept distinct from the biophony it masks and the geophony it overlays.

Soundscape layers

anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: traffic-noise studies document reduced breeding success and altered calling behaviour in birds and frogs near busy roads, and quiet-area studies show recovery of biophony when anthrophony is reduced.

Math proof

Math proof: traffic at 70 decibels against a natural background of 40 decibels exceeds it by 70 - 40 = 30 decibels, a thousandfold increase in sound intensity.

Source

Traffic-noise and wildlife studies; urban soundscape literature (as of October 2026).

The Acoustic Niche Hypothesis JAH-SECO-000005 · simple

The acoustic niche hypothesis, proposed by Bernie Krause, states that in a healthy ecosystem, species evolve to vocalise in their own acoustic niches, distinct frequency bands and time slots, so that their calls do not mask each other, much as species divide food resources. A dawn chorus is the classic illustration: different birds sing at different pitches and in different minutes, insects occupy other bands at night, and frogs take yet other slots, so the whole community can be heard at once. When a habitat is degraded and species are lost, the acoustic niches empty and the soundscape thins, which is why soundscape recordings can reveal ecological damage. This record's primary layer is biophony, the community of callers dividing the acoustic space, while geophony and anthrophony are the competing sounds that niches must also avoid.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: spectrogram studies of intact habitats show species partitioning frequency and time as the hypothesis predicts, and comparisons with degraded habitats show emptied niches, supporting Krause's hypothesis as a working model of soundscape organisation.

Math proof

Math proof: if 5 species each occupy a distinct 1-kilohertz band, the community spans 5 x 1 = 5 kilohertz of acoustic space.

Source

Krause, acoustic niche hypothesis (as of October 2026).

Habitat Soundscape Comparison: Forest, Grassland, and Wetland JAH-SECO-000006 · simple

Different habitats sound different, and comparing their soundscapes is a core soundscape-ecology method. A mature forest soundscape is layered: insects high, birds in the middle, mammals low, with wind in the canopy as geophony. A grassland soundscape is more open: wind dominates geophony, and bird and insect calls carry far. A wetland soundscape is dominated by water geophony and frog and bird biophony, often with the densest choruses of any habitat. The same three layers appear in all three habitats, but their balance differs, and that balance is itself ecological data: a forest whose canopy birds have vanished sounds different from an intact forest even before a surveyor arrives. This record covers all three layers, stated separately for each habitat, never blurred.

Soundscape layers

biophony, geophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: habitat-comparison studies using standardised recordings and soundscape indices consistently distinguish forest, grassland, and wetland soundscapes, and track habitat change through soundscape change.

Math proof

Math proof: a forest layer mix of biophony 58, geophony 48, and anthrophony 40 decibels combines to 10 x log10(10^5.8 + 10^4.8 + 10^4) = 58.5 decibels, dominated by biophony.

The Dawn Chorus: A Daily Soundscape Event JAH-SECO-000007 · compounded

The dawn chorus is the burst of bird song that begins before sunrise and peaks in the first hours of daylight, when many species sing at once to defend territories and attract mates after the night. Singing at dawn is thought to help because sound carries well in the cool, still morning air, and because a male that sings vigorously after a night without food honestly advertises his condition. The chorus follows a sequence: different species start at different light levels, so the chorus builds species by species, and it fades as the day warms and feeding takes over. This record's primary layer is biophony, the birds themselves, while the still morning air is geophony at its quietest and any early traffic is anthrophony intruding on the chorus.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: dawn-chorus studies across temperate regions document species-specific start times tied to light levels and show that chorus intensity predicts territory defence and mating success in the season that follows.

Math proof

Math proof: if 30 males each sing 5 songs in the first hour, the chorus totals 30 x 5 = 150 songs in that hour.

Urban Soundscapes: When Anthrophony Dominates JAH-SECO-000008 · compounded

In cities, anthrophony dominates the soundscape: traffic, construction, aircraft, and sirens form a near-constant background, often loudest at low frequencies, and biophony must fit around it. Urban birds adapt in documented ways: they sing louder, higher, and at quieter times, such as at night or in the early morning before rush hour, and some species shift their song pitch above the traffic rumble. But adaptation has limits, and species that cannot adjust disappear from the noisiest areas, so the urban soundscape is both a record of human activity and a filter on wildlife. This record covers biophony and anthrophony together, kept distinct: the birds are biophony, the traffic is anthrophony, and the story is their interaction, with geophony, such as urban wind and rain, as the third layer in the background.

Soundscape layers

biophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: urban ecology studies document higher-pitched, louder songs in city birds compared with rural members of the same species, and lower diversity in the noisiest city areas, confirming anthrophony as a selective pressure.

Math proof

Math proof: an urban layer mix of biophony 55, geophony 45, and anthrophony 72 decibels combines to 10 x log10(10^5.5 + 10^4.5 + 10^7.2) = 72.1 decibels, dominated by anthrophony.

Freshwater Soundscapes: Ponds, Streams, and Rivers JAH-SECO-000009 · compounded

Freshwater soundscapes combine rich biophony with strong geophony. Ponds host frog choruses and insect stridulation, streams add the continuous geophony of flowing water over rock, and rivers add depth, current, and seasonal flood sounds. Underwater, fish produce grunts, clicks, and drumming sounds, and aquatic insects add their own signals, so a hydrophone in a pond hears a different world from a microphone above it. Freshwater soundscapes are sensitive indicators: pollution, water extraction, and invasive species change them, and their recording is a growing tool in freshwater conservation. This record covers biophony and geophony, kept distinct: the animals are biophony, the water itself is geophony, and anthrophony such as nearby roads or pumps is named separately when present.

Marine Soundscapes: The Ocean's Three Layers JAH-SECO-000010 · compounded

Marine soundscapes contain all three layers in a medium where sound travels far and fast. Biophony includes whale song, dolphin whistles, fish choruses, and the snapping of shrimp, which dominates many shallow reefs. Geophony includes waves, surf, rain on the sea surface, and underwater earthquakes. Anthrophony includes shipping, sonar, seismic surveys, and construction, and it is the layer of greatest concern in marine conservation, because it masks whale communication over large areas. Because water carries low frequencies over great distances, a single ship can raise background levels across a wide area, and marine protected areas are increasingly managed with sound in mind. This record covers all three layers, stated separately, never blurred.

Seasonal Change in Soundscapes JAH-SECO-000011 · compounded

Soundscapes change with the seasons, and those changes are ecological data. Spring brings breeding choruses of birds and frogs, summer brings insect choruses at night, autumn brings migrating flocks and rutting calls, and winter quiets most biophony while geophony, wind, storms, and ice, takes over. Recording the same site through a full year produces a soundscape calendar, and shifts in that calendar, such as earlier spring choruses, are evidence of broader environmental change. This record's primary layers are biophony and geophony, kept distinct: the seasonal arrival and departure of callers is biophony, while the seasonal weather that frames them is geophony, and anthrophony such as seasonal tourism or farming is named separately when it appears.

Soundscape layers

biophony, geophony

Science proof

Science proof: year-round soundscape studies document repeatable seasonal patterns in biophony and geophony, and multi-year studies show shifts in chorus timing that match independent phenology records.

Math proof

Math proof: if a spring chorus runs 60 days and a summer insect chorus runs 90 days, the two together cover 60 + 90 = 150 days of the year.

Soundscape Indices in Practice JAH-SECO-000012 · complex

Soundscape indices such as the Acoustic Complexity Index, the Acoustic Diversity Index, and the Normalised Difference Soundscape Index compress a recording into numbers that can be compared across sites and years. The Normalised Difference Soundscape Index, or NDSI, compares biophony in the 2 to 8 kilohertz band, where many birds and insects call, with anthrophony in the 1 to 2 kilohertz band, where much human noise lies, giving a value from minus one, anthrophony-dominated, to plus one, biophony-dominated. Indices are powerful for large datasets, but they are summaries, not substitutes for listening: a high diversity score can come from a storm as easily as from a chorus. Good practice therefore pairs indices with listening checks and states which layers each index responds to.

Soundscape layers

biophony, geophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: NDSI and related indices were validated in soundscape studies against direct listening and species surveys, showing that they track the biophony-anthrophony balance while also responding to geophony, as their authors document.

Math proof

Math proof: with biophony energy 70 and anthrophony energy 30, NDSI equals (70 - 30) / (70 + 30) = 0.40, a biophony-dominated soundscape.

Source

Soundscape-index literature: ACI, ADI, AEI, NDSI (as of October 2026).

Noise Pollution and Its Effects on Wildlife JAH-SECO-000013 · complex

Noise pollution is anthrophony at levels that harm wildlife: it masks communication, so mates cannot find each other and alarms go unheard; it raises stress hormones; it drives animals from otherwise suitable habitat; and it changes predator-prey dynamics, because prey that cannot hear predators are caught more often. The effects are documented across birds, frogs, whales, and fish, and they occur even when the noise causes no physical injury, because communication itself is the casualty. Mitigation follows from the diagnosis: quieter roads, speed limits, noise barriers, quiet zones around breeding sites, and quieter ships all reduce masking, and soundscape monitoring measures whether they worked. This record covers biophony as the affected layer and anthrophony as the cause, kept distinct, with geophony as the natural background.

Soundscape layers

biophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: controlled noise-exposure experiments and before-and-after studies of mitigation, such as road closures and quiet zones, document both the harm and the recovery when anthrophony is reduced.

Math proof

Math proof: if masking cuts a call's audible range from 100 metres to 25 metres, the audible area falls by a factor of (100 / 25)^2 = 16, because area scales with the square of range.

Restoration Ecology: Soundscapes That Come Back JAH-SECO-000014 · complex

When a habitat is restored, its soundscape is one of the first signs of recovery, and sometimes the earliest: replanted forests gain insect and bird voices before the canopy closes, rewetted wetlands regain frog choruses, and protected reefs regain fish and shrimp sounds. Soundscape monitoring is therefore used to judge restoration success, comparing restored sites with intact reference sites and with unrestored controls. The return of biophony does not by itself prove full recovery, since a few loud generalist species can dominate a still-degraded site, so restoration studies pair soundscape data with species surveys. This record covers all three layers: the returning biophony, the geophony of the restored landscape, and the anthrophony that restoration seeks to reduce.

Soundscape layers

biophony, geophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: restoration soundscape studies document biophony recovery tracking vegetation and species recovery, and comparisons with reference sites validate soundscape indices as restoration indicators.

Math proof

Math proof: if a restored site's biophony index rises from 20 to 60 over 5 years, the average rise is (60 - 20) / 5 = 8.0 index points per year.

Quiet Parks and Cultural Soundscapes JAH-SECO-000015 · complex

Some places are valued precisely for their soundscapes: national parks with natural quiet, recognised by programmes such as the United States National Park Service's Natural Sounds Program, and cultural soundscapes such as historic town squares, markets, and sacred sites, where human sounds are part of the heritage. Natural quiet is now rare and is treated as a resource to protect, because it supports wildlife communication and human restoration alike, and quiet parks are monitored for anthrophony intrusion from aircraft and roads. Cultural soundscapes raise a different question: which human sounds belong, and which are intrusion, a judgement that belongs to the community that lives with the soundscape. This record covers all three layers, with the judgement about anthrophony stated as a community matter, not as a technical fact.

Soundscape layers

biophony, geophony, anthrophony

Science proof

Science proof: park soundscape monitoring documents anthrophony intrusion levels and their effects on visitor experience and wildlife, and cultural soundscape studies document community values attached to particular sounds.

Math proof

Math proof: a park layer mix of biophony 50, geophony 45, and anthrophony 30 decibels combines to 10 x log10(10^5 + 10^4.5 + 10^3) = 51.2 decibels, dominated by biophony.

Source

U.S. National Park Service Natural Sounds Program; cultural soundscape studies (as of October 2026).

TITLE = 'Quiet Parks and Cultural Soundscapes'
import math
BIO = 50
GEO = 45
ANTH = 30
tot = 10 * math.log10(10 ** (BIO / 10.0) + 10 ** (GEO / 10.0) + 10 ** (ANTH / 10.0))
dom = 'biophony' if BIO >= GEO and BIO >= ANTH else ('geophony' if GEO >= ANTH else 'anthrophony')
print('Record: ' + TITLE)
print('Biophony: %.1f decibels' % BIO)
print('Geophony: %.1f decibels' % GEO)
print('Anthrophony: %.1f decibels' % ANTH)
print('Combined soundscape level: %.1f decibels' % tot)
print('Dominant layer: ' + dom)
print('Layers are mixed in arithmetic but kept distinct in description.')
