The records are: Finding a Sound That Is Rising Today; How a Trend Is Measured Honestly; A Chart Anyone Can Read and Check; Discovering Sounds by Mood and Tempo; A Daily Chart of New Sounds; Following a Sound From First Use to Wide Use; A Discovery Page That Welcomes New Listeners; Keeping Charts Free of Bought Plays; A Sound Library Sorted by Feeling; Weekly Roundups of Rising Sounds; Discovery That Respects the Maker; A Chart Archive for a Whole Year; Finding Sounds for a Particular Moment; How a Sound Spreads Between Communities; A Discovery Tool That Works Without an Account; Teaching Listening Through Trend Examples; A Chart That Shows Where a Sound Came From; Discovery Across Many Languages; A Sound That Fits a Short Video; Long-Run Records and a Hall of Fame; A Discovery Feed That Never Repeats Itself; Quality Checks Before a Sound Is Listed; The Discovery Chart as a Public Reference. Without a guide, a newcomer opens the database at a random record and may start with a complex one they are not ready for, while an expert cannot find the one record they came back for. The guide fixes that by naming the order, the ladder rank of each record, and the record a newcomer should read first and the record an expert should read next. Every line in the guide points to the full record, so the guide never replaces the record it lists. Worked example: with 5 groups of 5, the trending audio discovery counts 25 sounds charted, and the demo prints that same total, so a reader can check the arithmetic by running the demo fresh.

Feasibility: research-stage — Public research and working examples exist in this area, but no complete system at the needed scale exists yet; the gap is named on the record.
