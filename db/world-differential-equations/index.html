What Differential Equations Are

ID: JAH-DIFQ-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-differential-equations#JAH-DIFQ-000001 k=abea1109

Definition: A differential equation is an equation that involves a function and its derivatives: it says how a quantity changes, and its solution is the quantity's history. The equation dy/dt = ky says that y grows in proportion to itself, and its solution is the exponential. Differential equations are the language in which the laws of nature are written: motion, heat, waves, and populations are all differential equations.

History: Differential equations were invented by Newton and Leibniz in the 1670s, to describe motion: the equation of a falling body is the first differential equation. In the 18th century, Euler, Bernoulli, and Lagrange solved the equations of mechanics, and in the 19th, Fourier solved the heat equation and Maxwell the equations of electromagnetism. In the 20th century, the computer made numerical solution routine: finite differences, finite elements, and spectral methods solve equations that no one can solve by hand. Today differential equations are solved for weather, aircraft, bridges, epidemics, and the climate.

Key concepts: The key ideas are differential equation, derivative, solution, initial condition, order. A differential equation relates a function to its derivatives: dy/dt = ky says that y grows in proportion to itself. A derivative is the rate of change: dy/dt is how fast y changes with time. The velocity is the derivative of position. A solution is a function that satisfies the equation: the exponential e^(kt) satisfies dy/dt = ky. The initial condition is the value at the start: it picks one solution from the family of solutions. The order of a differential equation is the highest derivative in it: dy/dt = ky is first order, and d^2y/dt^2 = -ky is second order. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, write the equation: what changes, and how fast?. Second, state the initial and boundary conditions. Third, classify it: ordinary or partial, linear or non-linear, first or second order. Fourth, solve it exactly, if the equation allows, or numerically. Fifth, check the solution: does it satisfy the equation and the conditions?. Sixth, verify the units: the derivative of metres with respect to seconds is metres per second. Worked examples: The equation dy/dt = 0.02y, with y(0) = 1,000, has the solution y = 1,000 e^(0.02t): after 10 years, y = 1,000 times e^0.2 = 1,221. The equation d^2y/dt^2 = -y, with y(0) = 1 and y'(0) = 0, has the solution y = cos(t): it oscillates with period 2pi. A cooling cup of coffee obeys Newton's law of cooling: the temperature difference from the room decays exponentially, with a half-life of about 10 minutes. A spring obeys m d^2x/dt^2 = -kx, whose solution is a sine wave with frequency sqrt(k/m).

Real applications: Physics is differential equations: motion, heat, waves, and fields are all described by them, and their solutions are the predictions of physics. Engineering designs with differential equations: the deflection of a beam, the vibration of a bridge, and the flow in a pipe are all solutions of differential equations. Biology models with differential equations: populations, epidemics, and the spread of a drug through the body are all differential equations. Finance prices with differential equations: the Black-Scholes equation is a partial differential equation, and its solution is the price of an option.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is a differential equation? An equation involving a function and its derivatives: it says how a quantity changes, and its solution is the quantity's history. Why are they so common? Because the laws of nature are laws of change: they say how a quantity changes, and a differential equation is the mathematical form of a law of change. What is the difference between ordinary and partial? Ordinary equations have one independent variable, usually time; partial equations have several, like time and space, and they describe fields. Can every differential equation be solved? No: most cannot be solved exactly, and they are solved numerically, on a computer, with a known error.

First-Order Equations

ID: JAH-DIFQ-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-differential-equations#JAH-DIFQ-000002 k=73a02b25

Definition: A first-order differential equation involves only the first derivative: dy/dx = f(x, y). It says how y changes with x, and its solution is a function whose derivative matches. First-order equations are solved by separation of variables, by integrating factors, or by numerical methods. They model growth, decay, cooling, and mixing: the simplest laws of change. The general solution is a family of curves, one for each value of the constant, and the initial condition picks one.

History: First-order equations were the first differential equations: Newton and Leibniz solved them in the 1670s, and the method of separation of variables was known by the 18th century. Bernoulli solved the Bernoulli equation in 1695, and Euler systematised the methods in his Institutiones calculi integralis of 1768. In the 19th century, the existence and uniqueness theorem was proved by Cauchy and by Picard, and in the 20th, the numerical methods were developed. Today first-order equations are the first equations taught, and they model the simplest laws of change.

Key concepts: The key ideas are first-order equation, separation of variables, integrating factor, linear equation, exact equation. A first-order equation involves only the first derivative: dy/dx = f(x, y). It says how y changes with x. Separation of variables is the method of writing the equation as g(y) dy = h(x) dx, and integrating both sides. An integrating factor is a function that turns a linear equation into an exact derivative: it is e to the integral of P(x). A linear first-order equation is dy/dx + P(x) y = Q(x). It is solved by the integrating factor. An exact equation is one that is the derivative of a function: M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy = 0 with dM/dy = dN/dx. Third, if it is linear, use the integrating factor. Fourth, integrate, and apply the initial condition. Fifth, check the solution by differentiating it and substituting it back. Worked examples: The equation dy/dx = 2x has the solution y = x^2 + C: differentiating x^2 + C gives 2x. With y(0) = 1, C = 1, so y = x^2 + 1. The equation dy/dt = -0.1y, with y(0) = 100, has the solution y = 100 e^(-0.1t): after 10 seconds, y = 100 e^(-1) = 36.8. The equation dy/dx + 2y = 4 has the integrating factor e^(2x): multiplying gives d/dx (e^(2x) y) = 4 e^(2x), so e^(2x) y = 2 e^(2x) + C, and y = 2 + C e^(-2x). The equation dy/dx = y/x separates to dy/y = dx/x, giving ln|y| = ln|x| + C, so y = Cx.

Real applications: Biology models with first-order equations: the growth of a population, the decay of a drug, and the spread of an epidemic are first-order. Physics models with first-order equations: the cooling of a body, the charging of a capacitor, and the decay of a radioactive sample are first-order. Chemistry models with first-order equations: the rate of a reaction is a first-order equation, and the concentration is its solution. Engineering models with first-order equations: the temperature of a system, the voltage in a circuit, and the level in a tank are first-order.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is separation of variables? The method of writing the equation as g(y) dy = h(x) dx, and integrating both sides: it works when the variables can be separated. What is an integrating factor? A function that turns a linear equation into an exact derivative: for dy/dx + P(x) y = Q(x), it is e to the integral of P(x). Does every first-order equation have a solution? Yes, under mild conditions: the existence and uniqueness theorem says that a first-order equation with a given initial condition has a unique solution. What is the difference between linear and non-linear? A linear equation is linear in y and its derivative: it can be solved by the integrating factor. A non-linear equation usually cannot be solved exactly.

Separable Equations

ID: JAH-DIFQ-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-differential-equations#JAH-DIFQ-000003 k=13684f81

Definition: A separable equation is a first-order equation that can be written as g(y) dy = h(x) dx: the variables can be separated, one on each side. It is solved by integrating both sides, and the constant of integration is fixed by the initial condition. Separable equations are the simplest differential equations, and they model growth, decay, and the simplest laws of change.

History: Separable equations were the first differential equations solved: Leibniz solved them in the 1670s, and the method was known by the 18th century. Euler systematised the method in 1768, and it became the first method taught in every differential equations course. In the 20th century, the method was recognised as the special case of the exact equation, and it is now the first tool for solving a first-order equation. Today separable equations are the first equations taught, and they model the simplest laws.

Key concepts: The key ideas are separable equation, integration, constant of integration, general solution, particular solution. A separable equation can be written as g(y) dy = h(x) dx: the variables are separated, one on each side. Integration is the inverse of differentiation: the integral of a derivative is the original function, up to a constant. The constant of integration is the unknown constant in the solution: it is fixed by the initial condition. The general solution is the family of solutions, with the constant free: y = x^2 + C is the general solution of dy/dx = 2x. A particular solution is one member of the family, with the constant fixed: y = x^2 + 1 is the particular solution with y(0) = 1. Third, solve for y, if possible, and write the general solution. Fourth, apply the initial condition to find the constant. Fifth, check the solution by differentiating it and substituting it back. Sixth, verify the separation: does each side depend on only one variable?. Worked examples: The equation dy/dx = y separates to dy/y = dx, giving ln|y| = x + C, so y = A e^x, where A is a constant. With y(0) = 3, A = 3, so y = 3 e^x. The equation dy/dx = 2xy separates to dy/y = 2x dx, giving ln|y| = x^2 + C, so y = A e^(x^2). The equation dy/dx = 1/(1 + x^2) has the solution y = arctan(x) + C, because the derivative of arctan(x) is 1/(1 + x^2).

Physics is separable equations: the decay of a radioactive sample, dy/dt = -ky, is separable, and its solution is the exponential decay. Chemistry is separable equations: the rate of a first-order reaction is separable, and the concentration decays exponentially. Finance is separable equations: the growth of an investment at a fixed rate is separable, and its solution is the exponential.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is a separable equation? A first-order equation that can be written as g(y) dy = h(x) dx: the variables are separated, one on each side, and the equation is solved by integrating both sides. What if the variables cannot be separated? Then the equation is not separable, and another method is needed: the integrating factor for a linear equation, or a numerical method. Why is the constant important? Because the general solution is a family: the constant picks one member, and the initial condition fixes it. Can the solution always be written explicitly? No: sometimes the integral cannot be done in elementary terms, or y cannot be isolated. Then the solution is implicit, or numerical.

Linear First-Order Equations

ID: JAH-DIFQ-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-differential-equations#JAH-DIFQ-000004 k=65c671d0

Definition: A linear first-order equation is dy/dx + P(x) y = Q(x): it is linear in y and its derivative. It is solved by the integrating factor, mu(x) = e to the integral of P(x): multiplying by mu turns the left side into the derivative of mu y, and integrating gives the solution. Linear first-order equations model cooling, mixing, circuits, and the simplest systems with an input.

History: Linear first-order equations were solved by Leibniz in the 1690s, and the integrating factor was introduced by Euler in the 18th century. In the 19th century, the theory of linear equations was systematised, and in the 20th, the linear equation became the model of the simplest systems: the RC circuit, the cooling body, and the mixing tank are all linear first-order equations. Today linear first-order equations are the second equations taught, after the separable ones, and they are the model of the simplest systems with an input.

Key concepts: The key ideas are linear equation, integrating factor, standard form, homogeneous equation, particular solution. A linear first-order equation is dy/dx + P(x) y = Q(x): it is linear in y and its derivative. The integrating factor is mu(x) = e to the integral of P(x): multiplying by it turns the left side into the derivative of mu y. The standard form is dy/dx + P(x) y = Q(x): the coefficient of dy/dx is 1, and the equation is ready for the integrating factor. A homogeneous linear equation has Q(x) = 0: its solution is y = C e^(-integral P), the exponential decay or growth. A particular solution is one solution of the full equation: the general solution is the particular solution plus the homogeneous solution. Second, compute the integrating factor mu(x) = e to the integral of P(x). Third, multiply by mu, and recognise the left side as the derivative of mu y. Fourth, integrate both sides, and divide by mu. Fifth, apply the initial condition, and check the solution. Worked examples: The equation dy/dx + 2y = 4 has P(x) = 2, so the integrating factor is e^(2x): multiplying gives d/dx (e^(2x) y) = 4 e^(2x), so e^(2x) y = 2 e^(2x) + C, and y = 2 + C e^(-2x). With y(0) = 0, C = -2, so y = 2 - 2 e^(-2x): as x grows, y approaches 2. The equation dy/dx + y = x has the integrating factor e^x: the solution is y = x - 1 + C e^(-x). A tank with 100 litres of water and 10 kg of salt, with fresh water flowing in at 5 litres per minute and the mixture flowing out at the same rate, has the salt satisfying dy/dt = -0.05y, so y = 10 e^(-0.05t): after 20 minutes, y = 10 e^(-1) = 3.68 kg.

Real applications: Engineering is linear first-order equations: the RC circuit, the cooling body, and the mixing tank are all linear first-order equations. Physics is linear first-order equations: the charging of a capacitor and the cooling of a body are linear first-order equations. Chemistry is linear first-order equations: the mixing of a solution and the rate of a reaction are linear first-order equations. Biology is linear first-order equations: the concentration of a drug in the blood is a linear first-order equation.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is the integrating factor? The function mu(x) = e to the integral of P(x): multiplying by it turns the left side of the equation into the derivative of mu y. Why does the method work? Because the product rule gives d/dx (mu y) = mu dy/dx + mu' y, and mu' = P mu, so the left side becomes mu dy/dx + P mu y, which is mu times the equation. What is the homogeneous solution? The solution of the equation with Q(x) = 0: it is the exponential decay or growth, and the general solution is the particular solution plus the homogeneous solution. Can a linear equation be non-linear? No: by definition, a linear equation is linear in y and its derivative. If the equation has y^2, it is non-linear, and the integrating factor does not apply.

Exact Equations

ID: JAH-DIFQ-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-differential-equations#JAH-DIFQ-000005 k=a74cd741

Definition: An exact equation is a first-order equation written as M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy = 0, where the equation is the derivative of a function: it is exact when dM/dy = dN/dx, which says that M and N are the partial derivatives of the same function. The solution is that function, set equal to a constant. Exact equations are the generalisation of separable equations, and they are the equations that come from a potential.

History: Exact equations were studied by Clairaut in the 18th century, who gave the condition dM/dy = dN/dx, and by Euler, who systematised the method. In the 19th century, the exact equation became the standard form for first-order equations, and in the 20th, it was recognised as the equation of a conservative field: the function is the potential, and the equation says that the potential is constant. Today exact equations are taught as the third method for first-order equations, after separation and the integrating factor.

Key concepts: The key ideas are exact equation, potential function, exactness condition, integrating factor, conservative field. An exact equation is M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy = 0 with dM/dy = dN/dx: it is the derivative of a function, set equal to zero. The potential function is the function whose partial derivatives are M and N: the solution is the potential, set equal to a constant. The exactness condition is dM/dy = dN/dx: it says that M and N are the partial derivatives of the same function. An integrating factor can turn a non-exact equation into an exact one: it is a function that multiplies the equation to make it exact. A conservative field is one that comes from a potential: the work done is independent of the path, and the field is the gradient of the potential. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, write the equation as M(x, y) dx + N(x, y) dy = 0. Second, check the exactness condition: is dM/dy equal to dN/dx?. Third, if it is, find the potential: integrate M with respect to x, and N with respect to y. Fourth, write the solution as the potential equal to a constant. Fifth, if it is not exact, look for an integrating factor. Worked examples: The equation (2x + y) dx + (x + 2y) dy = 0 is exact: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1, so they are equal. The potential is x^2 + xy + y^2: its partial derivative with respect to x is 2x + y, and with respect to y is x + 2y. The solution is x^2 + xy + y^2 = C. The equation y dx + x dy = 0 is exact: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1, and the potential is xy: the solution is xy = C. The equation (x + y) dx + x dy = 0 is not exact: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1 — wait, it is exact too, because dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 1. A non-exact example is y dx + dy = 0: dM/dy = 1 and dN/dx = 0, so it is not exact.

Real applications: Physics is exact equations: the work done by a conservative force is independent of the path, and the force is the gradient of a potential. Thermodynamics is exact equations: the differentials of the state functions are exact, and the exactness condition is a Maxwell relation. Engineering is exact equations: the flow of a fluid is described by a potential, and the equation is exact. Mathematics is exact equations: the exact equation is the generalisation of the separable equation, and it is the first step toward the theory of differential forms.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is an exact equation? An equation M dx + N dy = 0 with dM/dy = dN/dx: it is the derivative of a function, and the solution is that function set equal to a constant. Why is the condition dM/dy = dN/dx? Because M and N are the partial derivatives of the same function, and the mixed partials are equal: d/dy (dF/dx) = d/dx (dF/dy). What if the equation is not exact? Then look for an integrating factor: a function that multiplies the equation to make it exact. For an equation in x only, or y only, the factor is easy to find. What is a potential function? The function whose partial derivatives are M and N: the solution is the potential, set equal to a constant, and the level curves are the solutions.
