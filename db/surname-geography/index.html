What a Surname Is Rank 1 · simple

A surname is a family name added to a personal name, used to distinguish people who share a forename. Surnames arose at different times in different places, from patronymics, occupations, places, and nicknames, and they became hereditary, passing from parent to child, at different dates by region. A surname is a linguistic and historical fact about naming, not a proof of descent: two people sharing a surname need not be related, and related people may carry different surnames. This pair treats surnames as geography and language evidence, mapped in aggregate, never as personal data about any individual.

Patronymic Surnames Rank 2 · simple

Patronymic surnames are formed from a father's name, with a suffix or prefix meaning son of or daughter of: English -son, Scandinavian -sen and -sson, Irish Mac and O, Welsh ap and ab, and many other forms worldwide. In patronymic systems that had not yet fixed, a child's surname changed each generation with the father's name, so a pedigree in such a system cannot be traced by surname alone. Fixed patronymics kept the father's name as a permanent family name, which is why the same suffix appears across whole regions. The suffix is the clue to the system, not to any particular family.

Occupational Surnames Rank 3 · simple

Occupational surnames come from trades and offices: Smith for a metalworker, Baker, Miller, Taylor, and their equivalents in every language, from Schmidt in German to Ferraro in Italian. An occupational surname records what an ancestor did, or was said to do, at the time surnames fixed, and it does not mean that later bearers practiced the trade. Occupational names are among the commonest surnames precisely because the trades were common, which makes them the weakest evidence for relationship: thousands of unrelated Smith families exist, and sharing the name proves nothing about kinship.

Place-Name Surnames Rank 4 · simple

Place-name surnames come from a village, town, estate, or landscape feature where a person lived or came from: names formed from a place plus a preposition, or from the place name alone. A place surname may record origin, residence, or landholding, and the same place name may generate surnames in several unrelated families. Place surnames are most useful as geography: their distribution clusters near the place that gave them, and a cluster far from that place suggests migration. The place itself, not any family story, is the evidence, and it is checked against maps and local histories.

Nickname and Descriptive Surnames Rank 5 · simple

Nickname and descriptive surnames come from appearance, character, or habit: names meaning tall, short, red-haired, strong, or cheerful, in the language of the time. These names were given by neighbors, not chosen, and they describe one person, whose descendants then kept the name after the description ceased to fit. A descriptive surname therefore tells a researcher about medieval naming practice and language, not about any living or historical person's appearance. Where a descriptive name looks unflattering, it is a reminder that surnames were practical labels before they were family badges.

Surname Spellings and Variants Rank 6 · simple

Surname spellings and variants multiply because spelling was not fixed when surnames formed, and clerks wrote names as they heard them. A single surname may appear in dozens of spellings across registers, censuses, and deeds, and the standard modern spelling is often a later regularization. Sound searching collects variants by sound, not by spelling: vowel changes, consonant doubling, dropped letters, and translated forms. A variant list is a research tool, and a match across variants is proven by age, place, and associates, never by spelling similarity alone.

Surname Distribution Maps Rank 7 · compounded

Surname distribution maps show where a surname's bearers lived in aggregate, usually from census or telephone-directory counts by district. A map that clusters a surname in one county suggests an origin there, while a scattered map suggests an occupational name or a long migration. Maps are aggregate evidence: they show counts per district, not individuals, and they must be read with their date, because a map from one census is a snapshot, not a permanent geography. A surname that is common nationally will map everywhere, and its map proves little; a rare surname's map proves much more.

Surnames and Language Change Rank 8 · compounded

Surnames and language change together: sounds shift, languages replace one another, and names are translated, adapted, or respelled at borders and in new countries. A Welsh name may be anglicized, a Gaelic name may lose its prefix, and an immigrant name may be shortened by a clerk or by the bearer. Language change explains why the same family appears under different forms in different records, and why a surname's modern form may hide its origin. The remedy is linguistic: trace the name through its sound changes and its language history, using surname dictionaries, not family lore.

Hereditary Surnames: When They Fixed Rank 9 · compounded

Hereditary surnames fixed at different dates: in England largely by the fourteenth century, in Scotland and Wales later and unevenly, in Scandinavia in the nineteenth century in some areas, and in other parts of the world later still or not at all. Before fixing, a surname was a byname that could change with each generation, which means a pedigree that assumes a fixed surname before its fixing date is anachronistic. The fixing date for a region is a research fact that must be learned before any surname-based search begins, and it is stated in surname dictionaries by region and by name type.

Surnames in Census Aggregates Rank 10 · compounded

Surnames in census aggregates are counts, not people: the number of bearers of a name in a district in a census year, used to map distribution and change. Aggregate counts show growth, decline, and movement of a name's bearers as a group, and they show which names were common and which were rare. They do not show who any bearer was, and this pair reports no individual census entries for surnames. A surname's aggregate rise in a town may reflect immigration, a falling count may reflect emigration or name change, and either reading needs a second aggregate, such as birthplace data, to test it.

One-Name Studies Rank 11 · compounded

One-name studies are research projects that collect every occurrence of a single surname, worldwide and across time, to reconstruct its origins, variants, and distribution. A one-name study is a collective method: it gathers parish entries, civil entries, census aggregates, wills, and deeds for one name, and it distinguishes unrelated families carrying the same name by place and by lineage. The study's value is its completeness for that name, and its limit is the same as any surname work: shared name alone never proves shared descent. One-name studies are registered and published as surname research, not as personal data.

Surname DNA Projects: Aggregate Only Rank 12 · complex

Surname DNA projects in aggregate only are studies that group test results by surname and report them as aggregate patterns, such as how many distinct lineages carry a name. This pair reports only that aggregate method and its limits, and it holds no individual test results, no individual genomes, and no match lists. The aggregate finding for many common surnames is multiple origins: the same name arose independently in several places, which confirms what linguistics already suggested. A surname DNA aggregate can support a lineage hypothesis, but it cannot create a pedigree, and it makes no medical claim of any kind.

Women's Surnames Over Time Rank 13 · complex

Women's surnames over time follow different rules by period and place: in many systems a woman kept her birth surname, in others she took her husband's, and in some she used both in different records. A woman may therefore appear under two or more surnames across her life, and a search under one surname only will miss her. Parish and civil records help because marriage entries usually give both names, and Scottish civil records give a mother's maiden name, which bridges the change. The rule for a region and period must be learned before searching, or half the population will be searched under the wrong name.

Immigrant Name Changes: Evidence and Myth Rank 14 · complex

Immigrant name changes are often mythologized: the story that officials changed names at arrival ports is largely a myth, because passenger lists were made at departure and officials copied them, while name changes usually happened later, by the bearer's choice, for work or belonging. Evidence for a real change is a pair of records: an arrival record under one form and a later census, naturalization, or deed under another, with age, origin, and associates agreeing. A change asserted without that pair is a story, and a change asserted for a whole family without individual evidence is a story about a family. This record separates the documented pattern from the port myth.

Surname Frequency Tables: Reading Them Rank 15 · complex

Surname frequency tables list names by count in a population, and they must be read with their source, date, and coverage. A table from one census year ranks names for that year only, and a table from a directory ranks householders, not people. Frequency is relative: a name ranked first nationally may be rare in a given county, and a rare name nationally may dominate a village. Tables are used to judge how strong surname evidence is: a match on a rare name in the right place is strong supporting evidence, while a match on a common name is weak, and both readings require the age, place, and associate checks that surname evidence always needs.
