One Page, One Memory Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: buildable-now One-memory page design is established practice in scrapbooking today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a digital scrapbook creator that builds each page around one memory, with nothing on the page that fights it. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

A scrapbook page that tries to hold five memories holds none of them well: the photos crowd each other, the captions compete, and the reader cannot tell which memory matters most. A page works only when one memory is biggest, one memory is first, and everything else serves it. A creator that asks for the one memory first, makes its photo the biggest thing on the page, and refuses to let anything else grow as large, turns a crowded page into a clear one: the reader sees the memory at a glance, reads its caption, and feels it, because the page has room to breathe. The discipline is simple, and it is the difference between a scrapbook and a pile of photos.

Worked example: a page about a first day at the beach carries one big photo of the first step into the water, with a caption that says the water was cold and the laugh was loud.

History

The one-memory page is as old as scrapbooks themselves, where a single ticket or photograph filled a page and the caption said the rest. The creator applies that old discipline, before anything is placed.

Proposed solution

Ask for the one memory before anything else; Make its photo the biggest thing on the page; Keep every other element smaller and in service to it; Write one caption that says what the photo cannot; Refuse to add a second memory of the same size.

Dependencies

A way to name the one memory; sizes that keep its photo largest; one short caption; a refusal for a second equal memory.

Feasibility

buildable-now — One-memory page design is established practice in scrapbooking today; only the design stands in the way.

Verification path

Show the page to five people and check that at least four can name the one memory; check that no other element on the page is as large as the memory's photo.

A field guide index to the twenty-three records in this database: a map of the whole set, ordered simple to compounded to complex, so a reader can find any record by number and by name. This is a design index for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

This database holds twenty-three full records about the digital scrapbook creator, ordered from simple to compounded to complex, and a reader arriving without a map cannot tell which record to read first, which records belong together, or where the harder designs begin. The field guide index solves that by listing every record by number and by name, in order, with a one-line account of what each record covers and which rung of the ladder it sits on. Without the index, the simple records and the complex records blur together, and a reader who needs the basics may open a complex design first, while a reader who wants the hardest designs may never find them. The index is the map that keeps the ladder readable, and it is the record that turns twenty-three separate designs into one set a reader can walk through from start to finish.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order; Give each record a one-line account of what it covers; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.

Dependencies

The twenty-three records themselves, listed in order; the ladder ranks simple, compounded, and complex; agreement that an index lists, and never replaces, the records it points to.

Feasibility

buildable-now — An index is a list with one-line accounts, written with tools that exist today; only care in listing stands in the way.
