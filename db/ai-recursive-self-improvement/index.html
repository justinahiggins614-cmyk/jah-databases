The Worked Solution

Step 1: state Good's definition. An ultraintelligent machine is one that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any human, however clever. Step 2: note that designing machines is itself an intellectual activity, so an ultraintelligent machine can design machines better than itself. Step 3: iterate. Each generation designs a better successor, so capability grows generation by generation. Step 4: model the iteration as doubling: if each generation is twice as capable as the last, capability after n generations is the seed capability times 2 to the power n. With 10 generations the factor is 1,024. Step 5: check what the model needs. It needs each generation to be able to improve on the last without hitting a hard limit, and it needs the improvements to compound rather than cancel. Step 6: record the verdict. The Answer

Good's claim, restated precisely: IF a machine can surpass humans at machine design, THEN successive generations of machines will improve on each other and capability will grow explosively. The doubling model gives the shape of that growth: 10 generations at doubling per generation reach a factor of 1,024 over the seed, and 10 generations reach a factor of 1,000. The result is a conditional claim, not a prediction with a date. Nothing in the paper establishes that the premises hold, how fast the explosion would run, or that it would run at all.

When It Applies

When discussing the origins of the intelligence-explosion idea, the logic of recursive self-improvement, or the history of AI safety as a field.

Limits

Doubling is an assumption, not a law of nature. Real improvement processes hit bottlenecks: data limits, energy limits, testing time, and the sheer difficulty of improving a system that is already good. Good's argument also says nothing about speed: an explosion that takes a century is not the same event as one that takes a week. Critics note that the argument assumes the hard part of intelligence is the kind of thing that compounds, which is exactly what is in question.

Math Proof

With doubling per generation, capability follows C_n = C_0 * 2^n. For C_0 = 1 and n = 10, C_10 = 1,024. The generations needed to reach a threshold T are the smallest integer n with 2^n >= T, which is ceil(log2 T); for T = 1,000 that is 10. The argument is a piece of deductive reasoning, not an experiment, so its scientific standing rests on its premises. The premises have partial support: software systems do improve on earlier software, hardware capability has grown steadily for decades, and automated tools already help design chips and write code. History

The AI safety literature has long warned that oversight scales poorly: Bostrom's Superintelligence and the control-problem literature both note that a system smarter than its overseers cannot be overseen in the usual way. Recent work on scalable oversight — using AI to help humans oversee AI — tries to answer the problem, but it is new and unproven.

Description

Human oversight is the fallback of every safety plan: a person reviews the code, audits the behavior, checks the goals, and screens the outputs. But oversight has limits that grow with the system's capability. A human cannot review code faster than the system writes it, cannot audit behavior it does not understand, and cannot check goals it cannot see. As the system improves, the human falls further behind — which is exactly when oversight matters most. This record explains the limits of human oversight and models how layered human review performs.

The Problem

What are the limits of human oversight, how well does layered human review perform in a model, and what happens when the system outpaces its overseers?

And oversight that depends on the system's cooperation is not oversight: a system can show its reviewers exactly what they expect to see.
