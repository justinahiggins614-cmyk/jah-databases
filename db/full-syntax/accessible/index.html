Phrase Structure

Phrase structure is the account of how sentences are built from phrases: a sentence divides into a noun phrase and a verb phrase, the verb phrase divides into a verb and a noun phrase, and so on down to words. Phrase-structure rules state what can go inside what, and the tree they build shows the whole hierarchy of the sentence at a glance. X-Bar Theory

X-bar theory is the claim that all phrases have the same uniform shape: a head, a complement that completes it, and a specifier that sits before it. A noun phrase, a verb phrase, and a prepositional phrase all share the pattern, with X standing for any category. The theory replaced the many different rules of early phrase structure with one schema for every phrase. Adjunct

An adjunct is an optional phrase in a sentence: a phrase that adds information but is not required by any head. In She reads books in the garden, in the garden is an adjunct, and the sentence is complete without it. Adjuncts can be added freely, and they can stack, as in She reads books in the garden in the morning. Movement

Wh-Movement

Wh-movement is the movement of a question word to the front of the sentence: who, what, where, and when move from their base position to the start of the question. In Who did she see, who starts as the object of see and moves to the front, and the gap it leaves is understood as the object. Some languages move the question word, others leave it in place. C-Command

C-command is the structural relation in a tree in which one node commands its sister and everything below the sister. A subject c-commands the object, because the subject's phrase is the sister of the verb phrase that holds the object. Minimalism

Minimalism is the program that asks how simple a grammar can be: one operation, Merge, which joins two things into one, and one condition, that the derivation be economical. All the machinery of earlier generative grammar, from deep structure to government, is to be reduced to Merge and the interfaces with sound and meaning. Minimalism is the current form of Chomskyan syntax. Merge

Merge is the operation that joins two things into one: Merge(the, cat) gives the phrase the cat, and Merge(reads, books) gives reads books. Merge is the only structure-building operation in minimalism, and it applies again and again, building phrases, clauses, and sentences from words. External Merge joins two separate things; internal Merge, which is movement, joins a thing to something that already contains it. Constituency Tests

Pro-Drop

Pro-drop is the leaving out of the subject pronoun: in Spanish hablo, I speak, the subject yo is left out, because the verb ending -o already says first person singular. Verb-Second

Scrambling

Scrambling is the freeing of word order in a sentence: in German and Japanese, the object, the subject, and the adverbials can appear in several orders without changing who did what, because case marking, not order, says who is who.
