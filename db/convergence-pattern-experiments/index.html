Ripple Tank Interference JAH-CPEX-000001

A ripple tank is a shallow tray of water with a light above and a screen below, in which a vibrating source sends circular waves across the surface. With two sources, the waves overlap, and where crest meets crest the water rises higher while crest meeting trough cancels to stillness. The resulting pattern of bright and dark bands on the screen makes interference visible to the eye, which is why ripple tanks have taught wave physics for generations.

The experiment's controls are simple and instructive: water depth must be even, the sources must vibrate together, and the light must be steady, or the pattern drifts and blurs. Changing the source spacing changes the fringe spacing in a predictable way, and measuring that change gives a hands-on check of the wave relationship between wavelength, spacing, and fringe separation.

The demo simulates two point sources on a small grid, adds their sine waves, and prints the resulting row of heights. It is a wave-addition simulation, not a photograph of a real tank.

Chladni Plate Patterns JAH-CPEX-000002

A Chladni plate is a metal plate, usually square or round, sprinkled with fine sand and driven at a resonant frequency by a bow or a speaker. At resonance the plate vibrates in a standing-wave pattern with still lines, called nodes, where the sand gathers, and moving regions where the sand is shaken away. Each frequency produces a different nodal pattern, so sweeping the frequency draws a gallery of the plate's modes.

The experiment teaches resonance directly: off resonance the sand jitters without pattern, and on resonance a crisp figure appears within seconds. Plate shape, thickness, and clamping decide which patterns appear, and the same physics governs drumheads, bridge decks, and loudspeaker cones, which is why the demonstration survives in physics teaching.

The demo computes a simple two-dimensional standing-wave pattern on a small grid and prints the rows where the amplitude is near zero, marking the nodal lines. It is a mode-shape illustration, not a measurement of a real plate.

Double-Slit Fringes JAH-CPEX-000003

The double-slit experiment sends light through two narrow slits and watches the screen beyond: instead of two bright lines, a pattern of many bright and dark fringes appears. The fringes come from interference, because light from the two slits travels different distances to each screen point, and where the paths differ by whole wavelengths the waves reinforce while half-wavelength differences cancel. The fringe spacing grows with wavelength and screen distance and shrinks with slit separation.

The experiment's fame comes from its single-particle version, in which particles sent one at a time still build the fringe pattern dot by dot, which no classical picture of little bullets predicts. That result is why the double slit remains the standard introduction to quantum behavior, even though the basic fringe arithmetic is classical wave physics.

The demo computes fringe intensity across a screen from the standard two-slit formula with stated wavelength, slit separation, and distance, printing the intensity row. It is a formula illustration with assumed values.

Laser Speckle Patterns JAH-CPEX-000004

Laser speckle is the grainy pattern that appears when coherent laser light scatters off a rough surface: each point on the surface sends back a wave with its own phase, and the waves interfere randomly on the screen, producing bright grains where they reinforce and dark grains where they cancel. The grain size depends on wavelength, illuminated area, and viewing distance, and the pattern is exquisitely sensitive to tiny movements of the surface.

That sensitivity is useful: speckle interferometry measures vibrations, strains, and surface motion far smaller than a wavelength by watching the pattern shift. It is also a nuisance in laser displays and imaging, where speckle degrades the picture, and engineers reduce it by averaging many independent patterns, for example by moving a diffuser. The same random interference is therefore both a measurement tool and a noise source.

The demo generates a small random-phase speckle field on a grid and prints it as characters by intensity. It is a random-interference illustration, not a photograph of laser light.

Moire Fringe Patterns JAH-CPEX-000005

Moire patterns appear when two regular patterns, such as two grids of lines, overlap with a slight difference in spacing or angle. The overlap creates large-scale light and dark bands that neither pattern has alone, and the bands move dramatically when either pattern shifts slightly, which makes moire a sensitive amplifier of small displacements. Printers, weavers, and engineers have used the effect both as an artifact to avoid and as a measurement trick.

The experiment is easy to reproduce: print two identical line grids on transparencies, overlay them, and rotate one slowly. The bands sweep across the overlap as the angle changes, and counting bands over a known rotation gives a displacement measurement. The same principle underlies moire strain gauges, which read deformation from fringe movement.

The demo overlays two line grids at a small angle difference on a character grid and prints the resulting moire bands. It is a pattern illustration, not a strain measurement.

Lissajous Figures JAH-CPEX-000006

A Lissajous figure is the path traced by a point moving with sinusoidal motion in two directions at once, and its shape depends on the frequency ratio and phase difference of the two motions. Equal frequencies give ellipses and lines; a two-to-one ratio gives figure-eights; more complex ratios give woven patterns. Oscilloscopes once drew these figures routinely when comparing two signals, and the pattern instantly showed whether the signals matched in frequency and phase.

The experiment's lesson is that a complicated-looking curve can come from two simple motions combined, which is a useful antidote to the assumption that complex output needs a complex cause. Changing one frequency slightly makes the figure drift and evolve, which shows phase as a living quantity rather than a static number.

The demo samples a two-to-one Lissajous curve and prints its points. It is a curve illustration, not an oscilloscope trace.

Galton Board Distribution JAH-CPEX-000007

A Galton board drops balls through rows of pegs, where each peg sends a ball left or right with roughly equal chance, and the balls collect in bins at the bottom. A single ball's path is unpredictable, but many balls form a bell-shaped pile, because reaching an extreme bin requires the same turn at nearly every peg while middle bins can be reached by many different turn sequences. The board makes the central limit idea visible: many small random choices sum to a predictable distribution.

The experiment's controls matter: pegs must be even, balls must be identical, and the board must be level, or the pile skews and the lesson is lost. Counting balls per bin and comparing with the binomial prediction turns a striking picture into a quantitative check, and the agreement improves as more balls are dropped, which is itself part of the lesson.

The demo simulates two thousand balls through ten peg rows with a fixed seed and prints the bin counts. It is a statistical simulation, not a physical board.

Random Walk Convergence JAH-CPEX-000008

A random walk takes steps of fixed size in random directions, and its endpoint after many steps is unpredictable for any single walk, yet the distribution of endpoints over many walks follows a spreading bell shape whose width grows with the square root of the step count. That square-root growth is the signature of diffusion, and it explains why ink spreads slowly, why pollen grains jitter, and why errors accumulate more slowly than a straight march would suggest.

The experiment is to run many walks, record endpoints, and watch the distribution widen predictably while the average stays near zero. Biasing the steps slightly, so right steps are a little more likely than left ones, moves the whole distribution sideways, which shows how a small systematic effect emerges from noise when enough walks are averaged.

The demo runs five hundred walks of one hundred steps each with a fixed seed and prints the mean and spread of endpoints. It is a statistical simulation, not a physical diffusion measurement.

Monte Carlo Pi Estimation JAH-CPEX-000009

Monte Carlo estimation finds a number by random sampling instead of by formula: to estimate pi, throw random darts at a square that contains a quarter circle, count the fraction that land inside the circle, and multiply by four. The fraction approaches pi over four as the dart count grows, because the fraction estimates the ratio of the two areas. The method is slow for high precision but remarkably general, which is why it survives wherever exact formulas are unavailable.

The experiment's lesson is error scaling: quadrupling the dart count only halves the error, because sampling error shrinks with the square root of the count. That scaling governs every Monte Carlo method, from particle physics to finance, and it is why practitioners care as much about variance reduction as about raw sample counts.

The demo throws ten thousand seeded darts and prints the pi estimate and its error. It is a sampling simulation with a fixed seed, not a measurement.

Faraday waves are standing-wave patterns that appear on a liquid surface vibrated vertically at the right frequency: the flat surface becomes unstable and breaks into a crisp geometric pattern of peaks and troughs, often squares, stripes, or hexagons depending on frequency and amplitude. The experiment, first described in the nineteenth century, needs a level tray, a steady vibration source, and a strobe or camera fast enough to freeze the pattern, because the naked eye averages the motion into a blur. Changing frequency steps the pattern through its gallery, and each pattern's symmetry is a fingerprint of the driving conditions.

Optical Caustic Patterns JAH-CPEX-000012

A caustic is the bright pattern formed when light reflects or refracts through a curved surface and concentrates along curves or points: the shimmering network at the bottom of a swimming pool, the bright cusp in a coffee cup, and the focused spot under a lens are all caustics. Caustics teach that brightness is not just about how much light there is but about where rays agree to meet. A small change in the water surface or the cup's shape moves the whole network, which is why caustics look alive even when the light is steady. Artists and opticians both study them, for beauty in one case and for stray-light control in the other.

Diffraction Grating Spectra JAH-CPEX-000013

A diffraction grating is a surface ruled with thousands of fine parallel lines that split light into its colors by interference: each wavelength reinforces at a different angle, so white light spreads into a spectrum and a single color appears as sharp lines at predictable angles. The grating equation ties angle to wavelength and line spacing, and measuring the angles identifies the light's composition, which is the basis of spectroscopy.

Grating quality decides the result: even line spacing gives sharp lines, while spacing errors blur them, and the number of illuminated lines decides how narrow the lines are. That is why spectrometers illuminate as much of the grating as possible and why grating spectra, unlike prism spectra, spread colors nearly evenly by wavelength.

Pendulum Synchronization JAH-CPEX-000014

Pendulum synchronization, first noticed with clocks hung on a common beam, is the tendency of coupled oscillators to fall into step: energy leaks through the shared support, and the oscillators gradually align their phases until they swing together or exactly opposite. The experiment generalizes far beyond clocks: fireflies flashing together, pacemaker cells beating together, and power generators staying in phase all share the same mathematics of weakly coupled oscillators pulling each other into step. The demonstration with metronomes on a rolling board is the standard classroom version, because the board supplies the coupling visibly.

Sandpile Avalanche Patterns JAH-CPEX-000016

A sandpile model drops grains onto a grid, and whenever a cell holds four or more grains it topples, sending one grain to each neighbor, which may topple in turn. The pile organizes itself to a critical state where avalanches of every size occur, from single topples to cascades spanning the pile, and the avalanche sizes follow a broad distribution rather than clustering around a typical size. The model is the classic illustration of self-organized criticality.

The experiment's lesson is that a system driven slowly and steadily can produce events on every scale without any external tuning: the critical state is an attractor, not a setting. The same mathematics is invoked, with appropriate caution, for earthquakes, forest fires, and market fluctuations, where small triggers sometimes cascade and sometimes do not.

The demo drops fifty grains with a fixed seed onto a small grid with toppling, printing the final grid and total topples. It is a cellular-automaton illustration, not a sand experiment.

import random
rng = random.Random(9)
size = 7
grid = [[0] * size for _ in range(size)]
topples = 0
def topple_all():
    global topples
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for y in range(size):
            for x in range(size):
                if grid[y][x] >= 4:
                    grid[y][x] -= 4
                    topples += 1
                    changed = True
                    for dx, dy in [(1, 0), (-1, 0), (0, 1), (0, -1)]:
                        nx, ny = x + dx, y + dy
                        if 0 <= nx < size and 0 <= ny < size:
                            grid[ny][nx] += 1
for _ in range(50):
    grid[rng.randrange(size)][rng.randrange(size)] += 1
    topple_all()
for row in grid:
    print(row)
print('total topples:', topples)
