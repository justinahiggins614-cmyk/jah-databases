What Botany Is

ID: JAH-BOTAS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a teaching ladder that opens each topic simply before building to the complex point, plus side-by-side comparisons of plant groups and a design reading of how plant form solves environmental problems.

Definition: Botany is the branch of biology that studies plants: their structure, growth, reproduction, classification, distribution, and relationships with other organisms and the environment. It covers everything from microscopic algae to giant trees, including how plants make food, how they respond to light and seasons, and how they have evolved over hundreds of millions of years. Botanists work in laboratories, greenhouses, forests, and deserts, and their findings support agriculture, forestry, medicine, and conservation.

History: Botany began as practical knowledge. Early farmers selected seeds and learned seasons, while healers in Egypt, China, and India recorded useful plants. The Greek scholar Theophrastus, writing around 300 BCE, is often called the father of botany for his systematic descriptions of hundreds of plants. The Roman writer Dioscorides compiled a herbal around 60 CE that remained influential for more than a thousand years. During the Renaissance, herbalists produced illustrated plant books, and the invention of printing spread them widely. In the 1700s Carl Linnaeus created the two-part naming system still used today, bringing order to a flood of specimens from global exploration. Charles Darwin's work in the 1800s explained how plant diversity arises through evolution by natural selection. The 1900s added genetics, cell biology, and ecology, and the 2000s brought whole-genome sequencing of plants such as thale cress and rice. Today botany combines fieldwork, molecular biology, and computer modelling to understand plants from genes to ecosystems. In the 1800s, collectors such as Joseph Banks and Alexander von Humboldt mapped vegetation across continents, linking plant distribution to climate and altitude. The 1900s saw the rise of plant ecology, which studies how plants form communities, and of plant biochemistry, which revealed the chemistry of photosynthesis in detail. The discovery of the structure of DNA in 1953 opened molecular botany, and by the 2000s researchers could read whole plant genomes. Herbaria and long-term records let scientists track how flowering times and species ranges have shifted as the climate has warmed, making botany central to understanding environmental change.

Key concepts: Plant diversity means the enormous variety of plant forms, from single-celled algae to towering conifers, all sharing a common ancestry. Structure and function link a plant's parts to what they do: roots absorb water, leaves capture light, stems support and transport. Photosynthesis is the process that powers nearly all life on Earth, turning sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into sugars and oxygen. Reproduction includes seeds, spores, and vegetative growth, each with trade-offs between speed and genetic mixing. Ecology studies how plants interact with soil, climate, pollinators, and herbivores. Taxonomy names and classifies plants so scientists worldwide can communicate precisely. Evolution explains plant history through descent with modification over deep time. Adaptation is a trait shaped by natural selection that helps a plant survive where it lives, such as thick waxy leaves in deserts or broad thin leaves in shady forests. A life cycle describes how a plant grows from seed or spore to maturity and reproduces, a pattern that differs greatly between mosses, ferns, and flowering plants. Plant communities are groups of species growing together, shaping soil, climate, and wildlife around them. Together these concepts let botanists compare plants across the world and predict how they will respond to change.

How it works: Botanists study plants at many scales. In the field they survey vegetation, collect specimens, and record where species grow and how they respond to weather and soil. In the laboratory they examine cells under microscopes, measure rates of photosynthesis and water movement, and extract DNA to compare genes between species. Experiments test causes: for example, growing the same species in different light levels reveals how much of its shape is inherited and how much is a response to the environment. Herbaria, collections of pressed dried plants, preserve specimens for centuries and let researchers track how flowering times and ranges have shifted. Modern botany also uses satellites to map forests, DNA barcoding to identify species from a fragment, and computer models to predict how vegetation will respond to a warming climate. Together these methods turn observations into testable explanations. Field botanists also use quadrats, small sample squares, to count plants systematically, and they press specimens for herbaria so that others can check their identifications. In greenhouses they control light, water, and temperature to test one variable at a time. Data from many studies are combined into models that predict, for example, how a forest will grow over decades or how a crop will yield under drought.

Worked examples: Wheat shows botany in daily life: a grass bred over ten thousand years from wild ancestors, its grains feeding billions. The oak tree demonstrates long life and structure, with deep roots, thick bark, and acorns that feed wildlife. Sphagnum moss holds water like a sponge and builds peat bogs that store carbon. Giant kelp, an alga, grows in underwater forests that shelter fish. The Venus flytrap, a carnivorous plant, snaps shut on insects to gain nutrients in poor soils. The bristlecone pine, one of the longest-lived organisms on Earth, survives for thousands of years in harsh mountain conditions, showing how slow growth can be a survival strategy. Bamboo, a giant grass, can grow nearly a metre in a day, while the tiny watermeal floats on still water and doubles its numbers in days. The saguaro cactus stores water in its pleated stem and can live for two centuries in the desert.

Real applications: Botany underpins food security: crop breeding, seed banks, and soil science all depend on understanding plant biology. Forestry uses botany to manage timber, restore forests, and store carbon. Conservation relies on botanists to find rare species, map habitats, and design reserves. Horticulture applies plant knowledge to gardens, parks, and city greening. Plant science also supports the search for new materials, such as fibres and biofuels, and for compounds used in industry. Botanical knowledge helps predict how crops and wild plants will respond to drought, heat, and new pests, guiding decisions that affect food supplies and ecosystems worldwide. Botany also supports the timber and paper industries, the development of new fibres and biofuels, and the design of urban green spaces that cool cities, absorb rain, and support wildlife. Botanical knowledge guides the restoration of damaged land and the choice of plants for green roofs and walls.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is botany the same as horticulture? No: botany is the science of plants, while horticulture is the applied art and practice of growing them, though the two overlap. Are fungi plants? No: fungi form their own kingdom; they do not photosynthesise and are more closely related to animals than to plants. Do all plants flower? No: mosses, ferns, and conifers reproduce without flowers, using spores or cones. Can plants feel pain? Plants sense damage and send chemical signals, but they have no nervous system or brain, so they do not experience pain as animals do. Why do leaves change colour in autumn? As daylight shortens, trees stop making green chlorophyll, revealing yellow and orange pigments, while some species make red pigments as a protective response. How many plant species are there? About 390,000 vascular species are known, with new ones described every year, mostly from tropical forests. Do plants have DNA? Yes, and plant genomes vary enormously in size, from small genomes in algae to giant ones in some lilies. What is the smallest plant? The watermeal, Wolffia, is a tiny floating plant less than a millimetre across.

Plant Anatomy

ID: JAH-BOTAS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds labelled cross-section diagrams described in words, a ladder from a single cell to a whole tree, and comparisons of the same organ across plant groups.

Definition: Plant anatomy is the study of the internal structure of plants: the tissues, cells, and organs that make up roots, stems, leaves, flowers, and fruits. It explains how a plant is built and how each part contributes to survival. A leaf, for example, is not a flat green sheet but a layered organ with a waterproof skin, light-capturing cells, air spaces, and veins that carry water and sugars. Plant anatomy connects what we see on the outside to the microscopic machinery inside, and it is essential for identifying species and understanding how plants work.

History: The study of plant anatomy began with the microscope. In 1665 Robert Hooke looked at cork and saw box-like compartments he called cells, the first description of the cell. The Italian Marcello Malpighi and the Englishman Nehemiah Grew published the first detailed plant anatomies in the 1670s and 1680s, describing vessels, fibres, and the layered structure of stems and leaves. In the 1800s the cell theory established that all living things are made of cells, and improved microscopes revealed chloroplasts and the details of cell walls. The German botanist Gottlieb Haberlandt classified plant tissues by function in the early 1900s, a system still taught today. Electron microscopy in the mid-1900s revealed the fine structure of chloroplasts and cell walls. Today, imaging and genetic tools let researchers watch tissues develop cell by cell from a seedling to a mature plant. In the 1900s, plant anatomists such as Katherine Esau wrote the standard textbooks that still guide the field, describing how tissues develop and how they are arranged in roots, stems, and leaves. The development of the electron microscope revealed the fine structure of cell walls and chloroplasts. In the late 1900s, developmental genetics showed how genes control the pattern of tissues, explaining why a root forms a central vascular cylinder while a stem forms a ring of bundles. Today, three-dimensional imaging lets researchers reconstruct whole organs cell by cell, and anatomy is used to identify timber, to trace the evolution of plant form, and to understand how plants cope with drought and flooding.

Key concepts: Meristems are regions of dividing cells at shoot and root tips and in the cambium, the growth zones that let plants keep growing throughout life. Dermal tissue, the epidermis, covers and protects the plant, often with a waxy cuticle that limits water loss. Ground tissue makes up most of a plant: parenchyma cells carry out photosynthesis and storage, collenchyma gives flexible support, and sclerenchyma gives rigid strength. Vascular tissue transports materials: xylem carries water and minerals upward, phloem carries sugars to where they are needed. Organs are built from tissues: roots anchor and absorb, stems support and conduct, leaves photosynthesise, and flowers and fruits handle reproduction. Cell walls of cellulose give plants their rigidity. A tissue is a group of similar cells working together, and an organ is a structure made of several tissues, such as a leaf or a root. Plasmodesmata are tiny channels through cell walls that let neighbouring cells share water, nutrients, and signals. The Casparian strip is a waterproof band in root cells that forces water and minerals to pass through living cells, giving the plant control over what enters. Secondary growth is the thickening of stems and roots by the cambium, which produces wood in trees. Aerenchyma is spongy tissue with air spaces that lets oxygen reach roots in waterlogged soil.

How it works: A plant grows by adding cells in meristems, then expanding and specialising them. At a shoot tip, new cells divide and then differentiate into epidermis, ground tissue, or vascular strands, laid out in a precise pattern controlled by genes and hormones. Water absorbed by root hairs travels through the cortex to the xylem, then up the stem in narrow vessels pulled by evaporation from leaves, a process called transpiration. Sugars made in leaves move through phloem to growing tips, roots, and storage organs. In woody plants, the vascular cambium adds a new ring of xylem each growing season, producing the annual rings used to date trees. Leaves regulate gas exchange through stomata, pores that open and close to balance carbon dioxide intake against water loss. New tissues form in an orderly sequence: cells divide in the meristem, then expand, then lay down thick walls or develop chloroplasts according to their fate. Hormones and gene activity control this pattern, so that xylem forms on the inside and phloem on the outside of a vascular bundle. When a plant is wounded, nearby cells divide to seal the damage with corky tissue. In leaves, the arrangement of palisade cells near the upper surface maximises light capture, while spongy cells below allow gases to diffuse to every cell.

Worked examples: A sunflower stem shows the pattern clearly: a ring of vascular bundles surrounds a soft pith, with tough fibres for support. A maize leaf has parallel veins, typical of grasses, while a maple leaf has a branching net of veins. The water lily has large air spaces in its stems and leaves, letting oxygen reach roots buried in mud. Cactus stems are thick and green, taking over photosynthesis because the leaves have become spines. The trunk of an oak shows bark outside, wood inside, and a thin cambium layer between them that adds one growth ring each year. Onion bulbs store food in fleshy leaves, an adaptation for surviving dry seasons. The stem of a sunflower seedling shows a ring of vascular bundles, while the root of the same plant shows a central star of xylem. A pine needle has a thick cuticle and sunken stomata, adaptations that reduce water loss in cold, windy places. The Venus flytrap leaf has trigger hairs that, when touched twice, close the trap in a fraction of a second.

Real applications: Plant anatomy supports agriculture: understanding root structure helps breed crops that find water in dry soils, and knowing how vascular tissue works helps explain why some diseases wilt plants. Timber identification relies on wood anatomy, distinguishing species by their vessel patterns. Forensic botany can link a suspect to a place through microscopic plant fragments. In medicine, anatomical knowledge guides the study of plant-derived compounds and the design of herbal products. Conservation uses anatomy to identify rare species from fragments. Horticulturists use knowledge of meristems and cambium to graft plants successfully, joining a fruiting shoot to a hardy rootstock. Wood anatomy helps archaeologists identify the timber in ancient buildings and ships, revealing trade routes and building practices. In crop science, root anatomy guides the breeding of varieties whose roots reach deep water. Anatomical study of fossil plants reveals how early land plants were built and how they coped with life out of water. In horticulture, knowing where the cambium lies is essential for successful grafting.

Common questions and misconceptions: Do plants have hearts or pumps? No: water rises through xylem by a combination of evaporation pulling from the leaves, adhesion to vessel walls, and root pressure. Is wood dead? Most wood is dead xylem cells whose walls remain as a strong skeleton, but the cambium and inner bark are alive. Why do leaves have veins? Veins are vascular bundles that deliver water and carry sugars away, and they also stiffen the leaf. What is bark? Bark is the protective outer tissue, including the corky layer and the phloem, that shields the stem from damage, drying, and pests. Can you tell a tree's age from its rings? Usually yes in temperate climates, where one ring forms each year, but tropical trees may lack clear rings. Do all plants have the same tissues? The basic tissue types are shared, but their arrangement differs: monocots scatter their vascular bundles through the stem, while dicots arrange them in a ring. What is a node? The point on a stem where a leaf attaches, and where buds form. Why do some leaves feel rough? Tiny hairs or silica deposits on the surface deter insects and reduce water loss. Can anatomy tell a plant's age? In temperate trees, annual rings give the age, while in herbs the anatomy shows the season of growth.

Plant Physiology

ID: JAH-BOTAS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a day-in-the-life timeline of a single plant, simple water and energy budgets, and a ladder from one leaf to a whole field.

Definition: Plant physiology is the study of how plants function: how they take in water and nutrients, make food, grow, respond to light and gravity, and survive stress. It treats the plant as a living system with processes that can be measured, such as the rate of photosynthesis, the speed of water transport, or the timing of flowering. Where anatomy describes structure, physiology explains the work that structure does. The field links chemistry, physics, and biology, and it explains why a plant in a dark cupboard grows pale and spindly while the same plant in sunlight grows green and sturdy.

History: Plant physiology grew out of simple experiments. In the 1600s Jan Baptist van Helmont grew a willow in a weighed pot for five years and found it gained far more weight than the soil lost, suggesting plants take most of their substance from water and air. In 1779 Jan Ingenhousz showed that green plants release oxygen in sunlight, and later work identified carbon dioxide as the gas they absorb. The 1800s brought the discovery of plant hormones: Charles Darwin and his son Francis studied how shoot tips bend toward light, work that led to the identification of auxin in the 1920s. The 1900s revealed the chemistry of photosynthesis, including the Calvin cycle of carbon fixation, described by Melvin Calvin in the 1950s. Modern physiology uses genetic mutants, fluorescent sensors, and computer models to track signals moving through a plant in real time. In the 1900s, plant physiology became quantitative: researchers measured gas exchange with instruments, traced carbon through photosynthesis with radioactive labels, and discovered the hormones that control growth. The identification of the photoreceptor phytochrome in the 1950s explained how plants sense day length and shade. In the 1960s and 1970s, the discovery of the hormone abscisic acid explained how plants close their stomata in drought. The 2000s brought molecular physiology, showing how genes switch on in response to light, cold, and attack, and how signals travel through a plant as waves of calcium and electricity. Today, sensors and imaging let physiologists watch a single leaf respond to a passing cloud in real time.

Key concepts: Water relations describe how water moves from soil into roots, up the stem, and out of leaves, driven mainly by evaporation. Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in sugars. Respiration releases that energy for growth and maintenance, running day and night. Nutrition covers the mineral elements plants need, such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, and the deficiency symptoms when they are missing. Plant hormones are chemical signals, including auxin, gibberellin, cytokinin, and abscisic acid, that coordinate growth and responses. Photoperiodism is the response to day length, controlling flowering and dormancy. Stress physiology explains how plants cope with drought, salt, heat, and cold. Osmosis is the movement of water across a membrane toward higher solute concentration, the force that fills plant cells and keeps them firm. Turgor pressure is the pressure of water inside a cell pushing against its wall, which keeps soft tissues upright. Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from leaves, which pulls water up from the roots. Source and sink describe where sugars are made, the source, and where they are used or stored, the sink, such as a growing fruit. A signalling pathway is the chain of molecules that carries a message from a sensor to a response, for example from a light receptor to a growth change.

How it works: A plant's daily work starts with water. Root hairs absorb water by osmosis, and the water is pulled upward through xylem as leaves lose water vapour through stomata, creating tension that draws the whole column up. In the leaf, light energy captured by chlorophyll drives the splitting of water and the fixation of carbon dioxide into sugars. The sugars travel through phloem to growing and storage tissues. Hormones coordinate the whole plant: auxin from the shoot tip suppresses side branches, gibberellin promotes stem elongation, and abscisic acid closes stomata during drought. Light sensors called phytochromes measure day length, telling the plant when to flower or shed leaves. When stressed, plants make protective chemicals, close stomata, and slow growth until conditions improve. Signals move through a plant in several ways: hormones travel in the vascular system, electrical signals pass along membranes, and calcium waves spread from cell to cell. When a caterpillar bites a leaf, the plant releases volatile chemicals that warn neighbours and attract the caterpillar's enemies. Roots sense gravity through starch grains that settle in special cells, telling the root to grow downward. In cold, plants accumulate sugars and protective proteins that act like antifreeze. These responses are coordinated so that the plant balances growth against defence, investing in one or the other as conditions demand.

Worked examples: A sunflower tracks the sun across the sky, a movement driven by uneven growth on the shaded side of the stem. A mimosa folds its leaves within seconds of being touched, using rapid changes in water pressure inside cells. Wheat seedlings grow toward light, bending because auxin accumulates on the shaded side and speeds growth there. Desert cacti open their stomata at night, when it is cool, and store carbon dioxide for daytime photosynthesis, saving water. Rice plants in flooded fields grow air channels in their stems so oxygen can reach the roots. A maple tree in autumn withdraws nutrients from its leaves before dropping them, recycling resources for spring. The sensitive plant Mimosa pudica folds its leaflets when touched, a rapid movement driven by water leaving special motor cells. A Venus flytrap counts touches: two touches within seconds trigger the trap, while one touch does not. Poplar trees release chemicals when attacked by caterpillars, and neighbouring poplars respond by making defensive compounds. A seedling in a dark cupboard grows long and pale, stretching toward any light, a response called etiolation.

Real applications: Plant physiology guides farming: knowing how crops use water helps design irrigation that gives more yield per litre, and understanding photoperiod lets growers time flowering in greenhouses. It underlies fertiliser use, showing which nutrients limit growth and how much is enough. In forestry it explains how trees respond to drought and heat, informing which species to plant as climates change. Plant physiology also supports the development of crops that tolerate salt or flooding. In controlled-environment agriculture, growers tune light, temperature, and carbon dioxide using physiological principles to grow food year-round in cities and deserts. Greenhouse growers use physiological knowledge to control flowering with day length, producing flowers out of season. Seed companies test germination under controlled conditions to guarantee quality. In dry regions, understanding how plants close their stomata helps breeders select water-efficient crops. Plant physiologists also study how rising carbon dioxide affects growth, informing predictions of future food production. In space research, physiology guides the design of plant growth chambers for long missions.

Common questions and misconceptions: Do plants breathe? Plants exchange gases through stomata and other openings, taking in oxygen for respiration and releasing carbon dioxide, while photosynthesis does the reverse in light. Do plants sleep? Plants have daily rhythms, opening and closing leaves and stomata on a roughly 24-hour cycle even without light cues. Why do plants wilt? Wilting happens when water loss exceeds uptake, so cells lose pressure and the plant droops; watering restores pressure. Can plants communicate? Plants release chemical signals into the air and soil that neighbouring plants can respond to, but this is chemical signalling, not language. Do plants grow at night? Yes: much stem growth happens at night, when the plant uses sugars made during the day. Why do plants need light even when they are not photosynthesizing? Light is also a signal that controls growth, flowering, and leaf movement, sensed by photoreceptors. What happens to a plant in zero gravity? Roots and shoots lose their sense of up and down, and growth becomes disoriented, though plants can still grow in space with careful lighting. Do plants feel heat? They sense temperature through proteins that change shape, triggering heat-shock responses that protect cells. Why do some plants close at night? Nyctinasty, the nightly folding of leaves, may reduce heat loss or deter herbivores, though the full explanation is still debated.

Photosynthesis

ID: JAH-BOTAS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a step-by-step energy budget from sunlight to sugar, a comparison of C3, C4, and CAM strategies, and a ladder from one chloroplast to the global carbon cycle.

Definition: Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria capture sunlight and use its energy to build sugars from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen as a by-product. It is the foundation of nearly all life on Earth: plants feed themselves with it, and animals ultimately depend on the food and oxygen it produces. The process takes place mainly in leaves, inside cell structures called chloroplasts, where the green pigment chlorophyll absorbs light. Without photosynthesis there would be no crops, no forests, and no breathable air as we know it.

History: The story of photosynthesis unfolded over three centuries. In 1771 Joseph Priestley found that a plant could restore air in which a candle had burned out, and in 1779 Jan Ingenhousz showed that this only happened in sunlight and involved green parts. In the 1800s scientists identified carbon dioxide as the gas taken in and oxygen as the gas released, and Julius von Sachs showed that starch, a product of photosynthesis, forms in chloroplasts. In the 1930s Cornelis van Niel proposed that photosynthesis splits water to release oxygen, a claim confirmed by experiments using heavy oxygen in the 1940s. Melvin Calvin and colleagues mapped the carbon-fixation cycle in the 1950s, work that earned Calvin the Nobel Prize in 1961. Later research revealed two linked stages, the light reactions and the Calvin cycle, and in the 2000s scientists began designing artificial systems inspired by the natural process. In the 1800s, researchers showed that photosynthesis produces starch in chloroplasts and that its rate depends on light, temperature, and carbon dioxide. The English plant physiologist Frederick Blackman proposed the idea of limiting factors in 1905, explaining why raising one input does not always raise the rate. In the 1930s, Robert Hill showed that isolated chloroplasts could release oxygen in light, proving that water splitting happens in the light reactions. In the 1950s, Melvin Calvin mapped the carbon-fixation cycle using radioactive carbon, and in the 1960s, researchers discovered C4 photosynthesis in tropical grasses. The 1980s brought the first detailed structures of the photosynthetic machinery, and in 1988 Johann Deisenhofer, Robert Huber, and Hartmut Michel won the Nobel Prize for determining the structure of a photosynthetic reaction centre.

Key concepts: Chlorophyll is the pigment that absorbs blue and red light and reflects green, which is why plants look green. Chloroplasts are the organelles where photosynthesis happens, containing stacks of membranes called thylakoids. The light reactions use sunlight to split water, releasing oxygen and producing energy carriers. The Calvin cycle uses those carriers to fix carbon dioxide into sugars. Stomata are pores that let carbon dioxide in and oxygen and water vapour out. Limiting factors are the scarcest inputs: light, carbon dioxide, water, or temperature, which set the rate of the whole process. Some plants use variations called C4 and CAM photosynthesis to save water in hot, dry climates. A photon is a packet of light energy, absorbed by pigment molecules in the leaf. An electron transport chain is a series of proteins that pass electrons along, releasing energy used to make ATP. ATP and NADPH are energy carriers that power the building of sugars. Rubisco is the enzyme that captures carbon dioxide, and it is the most abundant protein on Earth, though it is slow and sometimes captures oxygen by mistake. Photorespiration is the wasteful process that results when Rubisco captures oxygen, and C4 plants have evolved a way to reduce it. A limiting factor is the scarcest input, which sets the rate of the whole process.

How it works: Photosynthesis runs in two linked stages. In the light reactions, chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs photons, and the energy drives electrons through a chain of proteins, pumping protons and producing ATP and NADPH, the cell's energy carriers; water is split in this stage, releasing oxygen. In the Calvin cycle, an enzyme called Rubisco captures carbon dioxide and attaches it to a five-carbon molecule, and a series of steps powered by ATP and NADPH builds three-carbon sugars, which the plant converts into starch, cellulose, and other compounds. The overall equation is six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six of water, powered by light, giving one glucose molecule and six oxygen molecules. The rate rises with light and carbon dioxide up to a point, then levels off when another factor limits it. Light energy is captured by antenna pigments and funnelled to a reaction centre, where it drives an electron to a higher energy state. The electron travels through a chain of proteins, and the energy released pumps protons across a membrane, building a gradient that drives the production of ATP. Meanwhile, water is split to replace the electrons, releasing oxygen and protons. In the stroma, the Calvin cycle uses ATP and NADPH to attach carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar, then rearranges the products into three-carbon sugars, some of which leave the cycle to become glucose and starch, while the rest regenerate the starting molecule so the cycle can continue.

Worked examples: A field of wheat on a sunny day fixes tonnes of carbon dioxide per hectare over a season, turning it into grain, straw, and roots. Maize uses C4 photosynthesis, concentrating carbon dioxide before fixation, which makes it efficient in hot climates. Pineapple and cactus use CAM photosynthesis, opening stomata at night to collect carbon dioxide and fixing it during the day, saving water in dry conditions. Algae in the ocean carry out about half of all photosynthesis on Earth, supporting marine food webs. A single large oak can produce enough oxygen in a year for several people, while its leaves also shade and cool the ground below. In a greenhouse, growers raise carbon dioxide levels to boost photosynthesis and increase yields. A single square metre of wheat canopy can fix several grams of carbon per day in bright sunlight. The water plant Elodea releases visible bubbles of oxygen in sunlight, a classic school demonstration of photosynthesis. Coral reefs depend on symbiotic algae that photosynthesise inside coral tissue, feeding their hosts. The giant kelp forests of California fix carbon at rates rivalling tropical rainforests. In winter, evergreen conifers photosynthesise slowly on mild days, while deciduous trees wait for spring leaf-out.

Real applications: Photosynthesis research drives crop improvement: breeders select varieties with more efficient photosynthesis to raise yields without more land or fertiliser. Understanding its limits helps farmers manage light, water, and carbon dioxide in greenhouses. The process also inspires artificial photosynthesis, where researchers design devices that use sunlight to split water and make fuels, a possible clean-energy technology. Measuring photosynthesis from satellites lets scientists track global carbon uptake and monitor forest health. In space exploration, algae-based life-support systems are studied for producing oxygen and food on long missions. Every climate model depends on accurate estimates of how much carbon plants absorb. Photosynthesis measurements help farmers decide when to irrigate and fertilise, since stressed plants photosynthesise less. Satellite measurements of photosynthesis track drought and crop health across whole regions. In biotechnology, researchers are engineering crops with more efficient photosynthesis, including faster recovery from shade, to raise yields. Artificial photosynthesis devices aim to use sunlight to split water and make hydrogen fuel, copying the natural process. Understanding ocean photosynthesis is essential for modelling how much carbon the sea absorbs.

Common questions and misconceptions: Do plants photosynthesise at night? No: the light reactions need light, though the Calvin cycle can briefly continue using stored energy carriers. Why are plants green? Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light most strongly and reflects green, so green light is the colour plants use least. Is photosynthesis perfectly efficient? No: most plants convert only one to two percent of incoming sunlight into stored chemical energy, and much light is reflected, transmitted, or lost as heat. Do all plants photosynthesise the same way? No: C3, C4, and CAM plants differ in how they capture and fix carbon, trading efficiency against water use. Does photosynthesis remove all our carbon dioxide? No: plants absorb a large share of emissions, but not all, and much carbon returns to the air when plants decay or burn. Why do some leaves turn red in sunlight? Red pigments called anthocyanins can act as sunscreen, protecting the leaf from excess light. Does photosynthesis happen in stems? Yes, green stems photosynthesise, and in cacti the stem does most of the work. What is the most efficient photosynthetic plant? Sugarcane and maize, using C4 photosynthesis, are among the most efficient, converting a higher share of sunlight into biomass than most plants. Can photosynthesis run in artificial light? Yes, and growers use LED lights tuned to the red and blue wavelengths that chlorophyll absorbs best.

Definition: Plant taxonomy is the science of naming, describing, and classifying plants into an organised system. It groups plants by shared features and evolutionary relationships, from broad divisions down to individual species. Every known plant has a two-part scientific name, such as Quercus robur for the English oak, that is recognised worldwide regardless of language. Taxonomy gives botanists, farmers, doctors, and conservationists a shared language, so that a plant discussed in one country is the same plant studied in another. It is the filing system of the plant kingdom.

History: People have grouped plants for thousands of years, first by use: food, medicine, timber, poison. Theophrastus classified plants by growth form, as trees, shrubs, or herbs, around 300 BCE. In the 1700s Carl Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature, giving each species a genus and species name, and a ranked hierarchy of kingdom, class, order, genus, and species. His system spread rapidly because it was simple and consistent. The 1800s brought natural systems based on many shared traits rather than a few, and Charles Darwin's theory of evolution gave classification a new purpose: reflecting real ancestry. In the 1900s and 2000s, DNA sequencing transformed taxonomy, revealing surprising relationships, such as the close kinship of water lilies to early flowering plants, and leading to revised family groupings published by the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group from 1998 onward. Before Linnaeus, plant names were long Latin descriptions that varied from author to author, making communication difficult. Linnaeus's Species Plantarum of 1753 fixed the binomial system and is still the starting point for plant names. In the 1800s, naturalists such as Augustin de Candolle and George Bentham built classification on many characters at once, producing systems that grouped plants by overall similarity. The 1900s added cytology, the study of chromosomes, and chemistry, which revealed that related plants often share the same compounds. The DNA revolution of the 1990s and 2000s, led by the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group, reorganised families: the old figwort family was split, the lilies were rearranged, and many familiar plants changed family. Today, online databases such as Plants of the World Online track hundreds of thousands of names and their status.

Key concepts: A species is a group of organisms that share distinctive features and can generally interbreed; it is the basic unit of classification. A genus groups closely related species, and a family groups related genera. Binomial nomenclature gives each species a Latin-style two-word name, with the genus capitalised and the species in lower case. A type specimen is the preserved plant that anchors a name, stored in a herbarium for reference. Ranks above family include order, class, and division, building a nested hierarchy. Phylogenetics uses DNA and shared derived traits to reconstruct evolutionary trees, showing which groups share a recent common ancestor. Synonyms arise when the same plant is described twice under different names, and taxonomists resolve them. A taxon is any named group, such as a species, genus, or family. A monophyletic group includes an ancestor and all its descendants, and modern taxonomy aims to name only such groups. A key is a tool for identification, offering a series of choices that lead to a species name. Nomenclature is the set of rules for naming, governed by the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants. A basionym is the original name under which a species was described, retained in the author citation when the name changes. DNA barcoding uses a short standard DNA sequence to identify a species, useful when flowers or fruits are absent.

How it works: Describing a new species starts with careful observation: the botanist records the plant's form, flowers, fruits, and habitat, compares it with known species, and checks type specimens in herbaria. If it is genuinely new, the botanist publishes a formal description with a chosen name, following international rules, and deposits a type specimen. Classification today combines morphology with DNA: researchers sequence several genes, compare them across species, and build evolutionary trees that show relationships. Plants that share a recent ancestor are grouped together, even if they look different, while look-alikes with separate ancestry are split apart. Names are updated when evidence demands it, and databases track the changes so that old names can still be found. The result is a living system that improves as evidence grows. Taxonomists gather evidence from many sources: the shape of flowers and fruits, the anatomy of leaves and wood, the number and shape of chromosomes, the chemistry of secondary compounds, and DNA sequences. They look for shared derived characters, traits inherited from a common ancestor, which indicate relationship. Computer programs analyse the data and build trees that best explain the pattern of shared traits. When a tree shows that a genus is split across two branches, taxonomists revise the classification, moving species or renaming groups. The revised names are published with descriptions and type specimens, and databases record the change so that old literature remains findable.

Worked examples: The rose family, Rosaceae, shows how taxonomy groups the familiar with the surprising: roses, apples, strawberries, and blackberries all belong to it, sharing flower structure despite very different fruits. Wheat, Triticum aestivum, sits in the grass family Poaceae alongside rice, maize, and bamboo. The tomato was once placed in its own genus, Lycopersicon, but DNA evidence moved it into Solanum with potatoes and aubergines. The water lily family Nymphaeaceae is one of the oldest flowering-plant lineages. The daisy family Asteraceae is the largest plant family, with more than 30,000 species, including sunflowers, lettuces, and dandelions. Mosses were once grouped with other simple plants, but are now recognised as their own ancient lineage. The genus Eucalyptus, with more than 700 species, dominates Australian forests.

Real applications: Taxonomy is essential for agriculture: knowing a crop's relatives helps breeders find wild genes for disease resistance. In conservation, accurate naming determines which species are rare and legally protected. Medicine and industry depend on correct identification, since a useful compound may come from one species and a poison from a near-identical relative. Biosecurity officers use taxonomy to identify invasive plants and quarantine pests. Horticulture relies on names to sell and grow the right plants. In ecology, surveys depend on accurate names to measure diversity and change. Customs officers use taxonomic knowledge to detect illegal trade in orchids, cacti, and timber. Gardeners rely on names to choose plants that suit their soil and climate. Digital herbaria now let anyone compare a specimen with millions of records online.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why do plant names keep changing? Names change when new evidence, especially DNA, shows that a plant belongs in a different group; the science improves, so the names follow. Are common names enough? Common names vary by region and language, and one name can cover several species, so science uses Latin-style names for precision. How many plant species are there? About 390,000 vascular plant species are known, and new ones are described every year, while many remain undiscovered, especially in tropical forests. Who decides a plant's name? The botanist who first validly publishes a description chooses the name, under rules set by the International Code of Nomenclature. Can one plant have two names? Yes, as synonyms, but only one name is accepted as correct at a time. What is a subspecies? A distinct population within a species, differing in some traits and usually in geography, but still able to interbreed. Why are some names in italics? Scientific names are traditionally italicised, with the genus capitalised and the species in lower case. How are new species discovered? Mostly by botanists exploring poorly known regions, by re-examining herbarium specimens, and by DNA studies that reveal hidden diversity. Who names a new species? The botanist who describes it, following the rules, often honouring a person or place. Can a plant be renamed? Yes, when new evidence shows it belongs elsewhere, but the rules aim for stability and the old name is kept as a synonym.

Plant Ecology

Definition: Plant ecology is the study of how plants interact with each other, with animals, and with their physical environment. It asks why a species grows here and not there, how plant communities change over time, and how vegetation shapes the climate, soil, and water of a place. Plant ecology works at scales from a single seedling competing for light to whole biomes such as tropical rainforests, grasslands, and tundra. It explains the patterns of green that cover the Earth and predicts how those patterns will shift as climates and land use change.

History: Plant ecology began with naturalists mapping where plants grow. In the 1800s Alexander von Humboldt described vegetation zones on mountains and across continents, linking plant distribution to climate. The Danish botanist Eugen Warming published the first ecology textbook in 1895, and the American Frederic Clements argued that plant communities develop through predictable stages toward a climax, while Henry Gleason countered that species act individually. The 1900s brought experiments: transplanting plants between habitats to test what limits them, and long-term plots that tracked change over decades. The discovery of mycorrhizal fungi showed that plants are linked underground in networks. Since the 1980s, satellite remote sensing has let ecologists watch vegetation change across whole continents, and climate models now use plant ecology to predict how ecosystems will respond to warming. In the 1900s, plant ecology became experimental: researchers removed species, added nutrients, or excluded herbivores to test what controls a community. The Rothamsted experiments in England, begun in the 1840s, showed how fertiliser changes grassland over decades, and the long-running Park Grass experiment is still running today. In the 1960s, Robert MacArthur and Edward Wilson proposed the theory of island biogeography, explaining why larger and nearer islands hold more species. The 1970s brought the study of plant-animal interactions, such as seed dispersal and pollination, as ecological processes. Since the 1990s, global experiments have tested how grasslands respond to extra carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and warming, and satellite data have revealed greening and browning trends across the planet.

Key concepts: A habitat is the place where a plant lives, defined by climate, soil, and water. A community is all the plant species growing together in one place, and an ecosystem adds animals, fungi, and the physical environment. Competition is the struggle for light, water, and nutrients; facilitation is when one plant helps another, for example by shading seedlings. Succession is the sequence of change as bare ground is colonised, first by pioneers, then shrubs, then trees. A niche is the set of conditions and resources a species needs. Biodiversity measures the variety of species and their abundance. Disturbance, such as fire, flood, or grazing, resets communities and can maintain diversity by preventing one species from dominating. A functional group is a set of species that play a similar role, such as nitrogen-fixing legumes or deep-rooted trees. A food web is the network of feeding relationships linking plants, herbivores, and predators. Primary productivity is the rate at which plants fix carbon, the base of all food webs. An ecotone is the transition zone between two communities, often rich in species. A keystone species is one whose impact is far larger than its abundance, such as a fig tree that feeds many animals. Resilience is an ecosystem's ability to recover after disturbance, while resistance is its ability to withstand it.

How it works: Plant ecology works through interactions. A seed germinates only where temperature and moisture suit it; the seedling then competes with neighbours for light, growing taller or spreading wider. Roots compete below ground for water and minerals, and some plants release chemicals that suppress rivals. Animals shape plant communities too: herbivores eat seedlings, pollinators move pollen, and seed dispersers carry fruits to new sites. Over time, communities change: fast-growing pioneers colonise bare soil, improve it, and are replaced by slower, shade-tolerant species. Climate sets the broad pattern, so deserts hold drought-adapted shrubs while rainforests hold tall evergreen trees. Human activity now overlays all of this, through farming, logging, and the introduction of species to new continents. Ecologists test their ideas with experiments and long-term observation. They may fence out deer to see how a forest regenerates, add nitrogen to a meadow to see which species benefit, or burn a plot to study recovery. They measure light, soil nutrients, and water, and they count species in quadrats over years. Models combine these measurements to predict how a community will change if the climate warms or a species is lost. Remote sensing adds the continental scale: satellites measure leaf area and productivity across whole biomes, revealing how vegetation responds to drought and to the seasons.

Worked examples: In a temperate forest, spring wildflowers bloom before the canopy closes, catching light in a narrow window. On a sand dune, marram grass traps sand and stabilises the dune, letting other plants establish behind it. In the African savanna, grasses and acacia trees coexist because fires and grazing keep trees from taking over. Mangrove forests grow where land meets sea, their roots trapping sediment and sheltering young fish. After a volcanic eruption on Krakatau, plants recolonised the bare ash over decades, a classic study of succession. In the tundra, low cushion plants hug the ground to escape wind and cold, flowering in a short summer. In the Serengeti, wildebeest grazing and fire keep the grassland open, preventing trees from taking over. In the Florida Everglades, sawgrass marshes depend on slow-moving water, and changes in water flow transform the vegetation. In the Alps, cushion plants create sheltered microclimates that let other species establish at high altitude. In Australian eucalypt forest, fire triggers seed release and germination, and the forest regenerates after burning. In a chalk grassland in England, dozens of wildflower species coexist in a single square metre.

Real applications: Plant ecology guides habitat restoration: ecologists choose species in the right order to rebuild a woodland or wetland. It informs conservation, showing which habitats hold the most species and which are most at risk. In farming, ecological knowledge supports intercropping, cover crops, and the control of weeds without heavy chemical use. Forestry uses succession theory to plan harvesting and replanting. Climate policy relies on plant ecology to estimate how much carbon forests and grasslands store. Urban planners use it to design green spaces that cool cities, absorb rain, and support wildlife. Ecological knowledge guides the design of nature reserves, showing how large and how connected they must be. It informs the control of invasive plants, identifying which species are likely to spread and how to stop them. In agriculture, intercropping and agroforestry use ecological principles to raise yields and protect soil. In cities, ecologists design green corridors that let wildlife move between parks. Restoration ecology rebuilds wetlands, heaths, and forests, and monitors whether the target species return. Climate models use plant ecology to predict carbon storage and feedbacks.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why do the same plants grow together in different places? Because they share similar needs for climate, soil, and water, so similar environments select similar communities. Do plants compete or cooperate? Both: they compete for resources, but they also share fungi networks, shelter each other, and improve soil for neighbours. What is succession? The gradual replacement of species in a place over time, from pioneers on bare ground to a stable community. Why are rainforests so diverse? Warmth, rainfall, and long stable history allow many species to specialise in narrow niches. Can damaged ecosystems recover? Often yes, given time and seed sources, but some losses, such as ancient peat bogs or old-growth forest, take centuries to replace. What is a biome? A large-scale vegetation type, such as tropical rainforest or tundra, defined by climate and dominant plant forms. Why are some places more diverse than others? Warmth, rainfall, long stable history, and varied habitats all favour diversity, while isolation and harshness reduce it. Do invasive plants always harm ecosystems? Many have little effect, but some transform habitats, outcompeting natives and changing fire or water regimes. How fast can vegetation change? After disturbance, change can be rapid, but the return of old-growth forest takes centuries. Can ecology predict the future? Models give scenarios, not certainties, and they improve as data and understanding grow.

Plant Genetics

Definition: Plant genetics is the study of heredity in plants: how traits pass from parents to offspring, how genes are organised in plant genomes, and how genetic variation arises and is used. It explains why a seed from a red flower may produce a pink one, why some wheat resists disease, and how new crop varieties are bred. Plant genetics combines the classic experiments of Gregor Mendel with modern DNA sequencing, and it underpins crop breeding, conservation, and our understanding of plant evolution. Every plant trait, from flowering time to drought tolerance, has a genetic basis.

History: Plant genetics began with Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk who crossed pea plants in the 1860s and discovered that traits are inherited as discrete units, now called genes. His work was ignored until 1900, when three botanists independently rediscovered it. In the early 1900s, researchers showed that genes sit on chromosomes, and Barbara McClintock discovered mobile genetic elements in maize in the 1940s and 1950s. The 1970s brought the first genetically modified plants, and in 2000 the thale cress Arabidopsis thaliana became the first plant with a fully sequenced genome. Rice followed in 2002, and since then hundreds of plant genomes have been sequenced, including wheat, with its huge genome completed in 2018. Today, gene editing tools let researchers change single letters of plant DNA to test what a gene does. After 1900, plant genetics grew rapidly: researchers showed that Mendel's laws apply to plants and animals alike, and they mapped genes on chromosomes in maize and other crops. In the 1940s, Barbara McClintock discovered transposons, jumping genes, in maize, work recognised with a Nobel Prize in 1983. The 1950s brought the discovery of DNA's structure, and the 1970s brought recombinant DNA and the first genetically modified plants. The sequencing of the thale cress genome in 2000 was a landmark, and the rice genome followed in 2002. Since then, the genomes of wheat, maize, barley, and hundreds of other plants have been sequenced, and gene editing has let researchers change single genes to test their function. Today, plant genetics combines genomics, field trials, and data science.

Key concepts: A gene is a DNA segment that influences a trait, and an allele is one version of a gene, such as the allele for red or white flowers. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones in the first generation of a cross. Genotype is the genetic makeup; phenotype is the observable trait, shaped by genes and environment. A genome is the complete set of DNA in an organism; plant genomes vary enormously, from about 60 million letters in some algae to more than 150 billion in a few lilies. Polyploidy, having extra chromosome sets, is common in plants and a major route to new species. Mutation creates new alleles, and recombination shuffles them. Quantitative traits, such as yield, are controlled by many genes at once. A chromosome is a long DNA molecule carrying many genes, visible under the microscope during cell division. An allele is one version of a gene, and a locus is its position on a chromosome. Linkage is the tendency of genes close together on a chromosome to be inherited together. A quantitative trait locus is a region of DNA associated with a trait controlled by many genes, such as yield. A mutant is a plant carrying a changed gene, used to discover what the gene does. Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population, the raw material for breeding and adaptation. Epigenetics covers changes in gene activity that do not alter the DNA sequence, such as chemical marks that switch genes on or off.

How it works: Inheritance in plants follows Mendel's rules with plant-specific twists. When a plant reproduces sexually, each parent contributes one copy of each gene through pollen and ovule, and the offspring's combination determines its traits. A cross between pure-breeding red and white flowered plants gives all pink or all red offspring in the first generation, depending on dominance, and a predictable ratio in the second, classically three to one for a single dominant trait. Breeders exploit this by crossing plants with desirable traits and selecting the best offspring over generations. Modern genetics adds DNA markers: researchers link a marker to a trait, then select seedlings carrying the marker without waiting for the plant to mature. Gene editing can now change a single gene precisely, for example to make a crop resistant to a disease. Breeders and geneticists work in a cycle: they cross plants, grow the offspring, measure traits, and select the best. DNA markers speed the process by revealing which seedlings carry a desired gene without waiting for maturity. Genome-wide association studies scan thousands of plants, linking DNA differences to trait differences. Gene editing with CRISPR uses a guide RNA to direct a cutting enzyme to a chosen sequence, where the cell's repair creates a precise change. The edited plant is then tested for the trait and for unintended effects. In conservation, genetic analysis reveals how much diversity a population holds and whether isolated populations are inbreeding.

Worked examples: Mendel's peas showed a three-to-one ratio of round to wrinkled seeds in the second generation, the classic evidence for dominant and recessive alleles. Maize shows the power of hybrid vigour: crossing two inbred lines produces offspring that grow taller and yield more than either parent. Wheat is a hexaploid, carrying three complete genomes from three wild ancestors, which gives breeders a rich store of variation. The colour of hydrangea flowers changes with soil acidity, showing how environment modifies a genetic trait. Strawberries are octoploid, with eight chromosome sets. In rice, a single gene called Sub1A lets some varieties survive two weeks underwater, a trait bred into flood-prone varieties. The semi-dwarf wheat varieties of the Green Revolution carry genes that shorten the stem, so the plant puts more energy into grain and does not fall over. The Sub1A gene in rice lets plants survive flooding by slowing growth until the water recedes. In tomatoes, the uniform ripening gene was bred out to improve flavour, a trade-off between appearance and taste. Wild emmer wheat, an ancestor of modern wheat, carries genes for disease resistance that breeders still use.

Real applications: Plant genetics drives crop breeding: it has produced high-yielding wheat, rice, and maize, disease-resistant potatoes, and drought-tolerant beans. Marker-assisted selection speeds breeding by identifying promising seedlings from DNA. Conservation genetics measures diversity in wild populations, guiding seed collection and reintroduction. Forensic botany uses DNA to identify plant material. In research, the small genome of thale cress makes it a model for discovering how genes control growth, flowering, and stress responses. Plant genetics also supports the study of evolution, tracing how crops were domesticated from wild ancestors. Genetics underpins the breeding of crops for yield, flavour, nutrition, and resistance to pests, diseases, and climate stress. It guides the conservation of crop wild relatives in seed banks. It supports forensic work, identifying timber and plant material by DNA. In research, model plants reveal how genes control flowering, root growth, and responses to stress. Plant genetics also helps restore endangered species, by choosing founders with enough diversity to build a healthy population. In industry, genetic knowledge guides the production of plant-based oils, fibres, and medicines.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are all plant traits inherited? No: traits come from genes plus environment, and the balance differs; height in a plant may be mostly genetic, while leaf size varies strongly with light and water. Do plants have more genes than humans? Not necessarily: thale cress has about 27,000 genes, similar to humans, but its genome is much smaller. What is a genetically modified plant? A plant whose DNA has been changed in the laboratory, for example by adding a gene for pest resistance; this is distinct from conventional crossing. Can genes move between plant species? Yes, through hybridisation and, rarely, through natural gene transfer, and breeders exploit hybridisation. Why do some plants have huge genomes? Mostly from repeated DNA and extra chromosome sets, not from having more genes. What is hybrid vigour? The increased growth and yield seen when two inbred lines are crossed, widely used in maize breeding. Do plants inherit traits from both parents? Usually yes, though chloroplasts and mitochondria are mostly inherited from the mother in most flowering plants. How many genes does a plant have? Thale cress has about 27,000, rice about 40,000, and wheat, with its three genomes, has more than 100,000. Can a gene be patented? Plant Diseases

Definition: Plant diseases are disorders caused by pathogens, such as fungi, bacteria, viruses, and nematodes, or by unfavourable conditions such as drought, nutrient deficiency, or pollution. They show as spots, wilts, rots, galls, or stunted growth, and they reduce yields, spoil food, and can kill trees and crops. Plant pathology, the study of plant disease, protects food supplies and forests by identifying causes, understanding how diseases spread, and finding ways to prevent and control them. Plant diseases have shaped history, from the potato blight famine of the 1840s to modern threats to wheat and bananas.

History: Plant diseases have been recorded since ancient times: the Bible and Roman writers describe blights and rusts on grain. The Irish potato famine of 1845 to 1852, caused by the water mould Phytophthora infestans, killed about a million people and forced mass emigration, and it spurred the science of plant pathology. In the 1860s, Anton de Bary proved that fungi cause plant disease, overturning the idea that fungi merely appear on sick plants. In the 1900s, researchers identified plant viruses, including tobacco mosaic virus, and developed resistant varieties. The 1970s southern corn leaf blight in the United States showed how uniform crops can be devastated. In the 2000s, new threats emerged: wheat stem rust strain Ug99, banana Panama disease Tropical Race 4, and the bacterium Xylella fastidiosa in European olive trees, all tracked by international surveillance. In the 1900s, plant pathology became a science of control: resistant varieties were bred, fungicides were developed, and quarantine systems were built. The Bordeaux mixture, a copper spray developed in France in the 1880s to protect grapes, was one of the first fungicides. The discovery of plant viruses in the 1930s, and the identification of tobacco mosaic virus, opened virology. In the 1940s, the gene-for-gene hypothesis explained how plant resistance genes match pathogen virulence genes. The 2000s brought DNA diagnostics that identify pathogens in hours, and the sequencing of pathogen genomes, revealing how they attack plants. Today, plant pathology uses remote sensing to detect disease from the air and models to forecast outbreaks.

Key concepts: A pathogen is an organism that causes disease; the main plant pathogens are fungi, oomycetes, bacteria, viruses, and nematodes. The disease triangle states that disease needs three things together: a susceptible host, a virulent pathogen, and a favourable environment. Infection is the process by which a pathogen enters and colonises a plant, often through wounds or natural openings. Symptoms are the visible effects, such as spots or wilts, while signs are the pathogen itself, such as fungal spores. Resistance is a plant's ability to limit infection, often through genes that recognise a pathogen. An epidemic is a disease outbreak spreading through a population. Quarantine is the restriction of plant movement to prevent a pathogen from reaching new areas. An inoculum is the pathogen material that starts an infection, such as spores on a leaf. A vector is an organism that carries a pathogen, often an insect that transmits a virus. A latent infection is one that is present without symptoms, becoming visible later. Systemic means spreading through the whole plant, often through the vascular system. A fungicide is a chemical that kills or inhibits fungi, while a bactericide targets bacteria. Biological control uses one organism to control another, such as a fungus that attacks a plant pathogen. Integrated pest management combines several methods to control disease with minimal chemical use.

How it works: A plant disease begins when a pathogen reaches a susceptible plant under the right conditions. Fungal spores land on a leaf, germinate in moisture, and penetrate through stomata or wounds, then grow through the tissue, stealing nutrients and releasing toxins. Bacteria enter through wounds or openings and multiply in the spaces between cells, blocking water flow. Viruses move from cell to cell and through the phloem, hijacking the plant's machinery to copy themselves. Nematodes, tiny worms, feed on roots and transmit viruses. The plant responds with defences: thickened cell walls, antimicrobial chemicals, and a hypersensitive response that kills infected cells to trap the pathogen. Disease spreads by wind, water, insects, tools, and traded plants and seeds, and it thrives when a single susceptible variety is planted over a large area. Disease control works at several points in the disease cycle. Resistant varieties block infection at the start, through genes that recognise the pathogen. Cultural methods reduce the inoculum: rotating crops, removing infected debris, and spacing plants for airflow. Chemical control protects healthy tissue or cures early infections, applied when weather forecasts predict disease. Biological control adds organisms that compete with or parasitise the pathogen. Quarantine prevents the pathogen from arriving at all. Together, these methods aim to keep disease below the level where it causes economic loss, rather than to eliminate it entirely, which is usually impossible.

Worked examples: Potato late blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, still threatens potatoes worldwide and is controlled by resistant varieties and fungicides. Definition: Plant biotechnology is the use of laboratory methods to study and improve plants, including tissue culture, genetic modification, and gene editing. It lets scientists grow whole plants from a few cells, move genes between species, or change single letters of a plant's DNA to test what a gene does. The field has produced crops that resist pests and diseases, tolerate herbicides, or contain extra vitamins, and it supports research into how plants grow and respond to stress. Plant biotechnology is regulated differently in different countries, and its use is a matter of public debate, which this record describes without taking a side.

History: Plant biotechnology began with tissue culture: in the 1950s, researchers showed that a single plant cell could regenerate a whole plant, a property called totipotency. In 1973, the first recombinant DNA experiments were reported, and in 1983 the first transgenic plant, a tobacco plant carrying a foreign gene, was produced. The first commercial genetically modified crop, the Flavr Savr tomato, was approved in the United States in 1994. Golden Rice, engineered to produce vitamin A precursor, was developed in 2000 and approved in the Philippines in 2021. Today, plant biotechnology combines genomics, editing, and data science, and it is debated in parliaments and in public, with different countries taking different paths.

Key concepts: Tissue culture is growing plant cells or tissues in a sterile nutrient medium, used to propagate plants and to regenerate them after genetic change. Transgenic means carrying a gene moved from another species; cisgenic means carrying a gene from the same or a closely related species. Gene editing, such as CRISPR, changes a plant's own DNA at a chosen site, often without adding foreign DNA. A trait is a characteristic, such as pest resistance or drought tolerance, that breeders aim to improve. Regulation is the system of laws and approvals that governs how modified plants are tested and sold, and it varies widely by country. A transgene is a gene moved from one species to another by genetic engineering. A knockout is a plant in which a gene has been disabled, used to discover what the gene does. A selectable marker is a gene added alongside a transgene to identify which cells received it, often conferring antibiotic resistance in the laboratory. A field trial is a test of a modified plant grown outdoors under controlled conditions, required by regulators in many countries. A risk assessment is the evaluation of potential harm from a modified plant, covering gene flow, effects on wildlife, and food safety. A regulatory framework is the set of laws governing the testing and sale of modified plants.

How it works: Plant biotechnology starts in the laboratory. In tissue culture, a piece of leaf or stem is placed on a nutrient gel with hormones, and it forms a mass of cells that can be coaxed into shoots and roots, producing many identical plants. To make a transgenic plant, scientists insert a gene into plant cells, often using the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens as a delivery vehicle, then regenerate whole plants from the changed cells and test which ones carry the gene. Gene editing uses a molecular tool, such as CRISPR-Cas9, guided to a precise DNA sequence, where it cuts the DNA; the cell's repair then changes the sequence, for example disabling a gene that makes a plant susceptible to disease. The edited plants are grown, tested in greenhouses, and, where the law requires, in field trials before any approval. Gene editing with CRISPR works like molecular scissors: a guide RNA leads the Cas9 enzyme to a chosen DNA sequence, where it cuts both strands. The cell repairs the cut, and in doing so often introduces a small change, such as deleting a few letters, which can disable a gene. To make a transgenic plant, scientists use Agrobacterium, a bacterium that naturally transfers DNA into plant cells, or a gene gun that shoots DNA-coated particles into cells. The changed cells are grown on selective medium, regenerated into whole plants, and tested. Each generation is checked for the change, for stable inheritance, and for the desired trait, a process that takes years before a variety can be considered for approval.

Definition: Algae are a diverse group of mainly aquatic organisms that photosynthesise but are not true plants: they lack roots, stems, leaves, and the complex tissues of land plants. Definition: Fungi are a kingdom of organisms that includes mushrooms, moulds, yeasts, and mildews. They are not plants: they do not photosynthesise, and they absorb nutrients from their surroundings instead. Fungi range from single-celled yeasts to the largest organisms on Earth, underground networks of mycelium spanning hectares. They decompose dead matter, form partnerships with plant roots, cause diseases, and provide food and medicines. Though this database is about plants, fungi belong in botany's story because they shape plant life profoundly, and because they were long classified with plants. The study of fungi is called mycology.

History: Fungi have existed for more than a billion years, and early fungi helped plants colonise land by forming partnerships with their roots. People have eaten mushrooms and used yeasts for bread and beer for thousands of years. In the 1700s, Linnaeus placed fungi with plants, a classification that lasted for centuries. In the 1800s, Anton de Bary showed that fungi cause plant diseases, founding plant pathology, and Louis Pasteur showed that yeasts ferment sugar into alcohol. No: it is a tool within breeding, used alongside crossing and selection, and its adoption depends on regulation and public acceptance. Can vertical farms feed the world? They suit leafy greens and herbs in cities, but staple grains and root crops are still grown in fields; vertical farming complements rather than replaces field agriculture. Can plants grow on Mars? Experiments suggest it may be possible with sheltered habitats, treated soil, and water, but no plant has yet grown on Mars, and many problems remain unsolved. Will photosynthesis be improved? Research has raised efficiency in experimental plants, but turning that into widely grown high-yielding crops is still in progress. Is the future of plant science only about crops?
