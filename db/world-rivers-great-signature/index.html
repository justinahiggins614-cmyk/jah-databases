Nile

ID: JAH-RIVRS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a river ladder: the Nile traced from Lake Victoria to the Mediterranean in four stages — lake, swamp, cataract, delta — with the river's character at each stage, so the whole journey can be followed in one table.

Definition: The Nile is the great river of northeastern Africa, about 6,650 kilometres long, traditionally considered the longest river in the world, though the Amazon may be longer by some measurements, and the two rivers' lengths are still debated. It flows north through eleven countries, from its sources in the highlands of East Africa to a wide delta on the Mediterranean Sea in Egypt. Its waters made Egyptian civilisation possible: the river's annual flood deposited rich silt on the desert, and for thousands of years Egypt was, in the words of the Greek historian Herodotus, "the gift of the Nile". The river's average discharge is about 2,830 cubic metres per second, and its basin covers about 3.4 million square kilometres, roughly a tenth of Africa.

History: The Nile's history is the history of Egypt. Farming began along its banks around 5000 BCE, and by 3100 BCE the kingdoms of Upper and Lower Egypt were united along the river. The annual flood, caused by summer rains in the Ethiopian highlands, arrived each year in Egypt, and the Egyptians built a calendar, a religion and an empire around it: they measured the flood with nilometers, stone gauges on the riverbank, and a good flood meant a good harvest. The river was the highway of the ancient world: stone for the pyramids was floated down it, and temples and cities, from Luxor to Aswan, were built on its banks. In the nineteenth century, European explorers raced to find the river's source: John Hanning Speke identified Lake Victoria as the source of the White Nile in 1858, and the source of the Blue Nile, Lake Tana in Ethiopia, had been known for centuries. The Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, ended the annual flood and gave Egypt electricity and year-round irrigation, but it also stopped the silt that had fed the delta's fields. In the twenty-first century, the Nile is shared by eleven countries, and the building of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam on the Blue Nile has made the river a matter of negotiation between Ethiopia, Sudan and Egypt.

Key concepts: A tributary is a smaller river that flows into a larger one; the Nile's two great tributaries are the White Nile and the Blue Nile, which meet at Khartoum in Sudan. The White Nile is the longer branch, flowing from Lake Victoria through Uganda and South Sudan. The Blue Nile is the shorter branch, flowing from Lake Tana in Ethiopia, and it carries most of the river's water and silt during the flood season. A delta is the fan of land built up where a river meets the sea, depositing the sediment it carries; the Nile Delta is one of the largest in the world. Discharge is the volume of water passing a point each second, measured in cubic metres per second. A basin is the whole area of land drained by a river and its tributaries. A nilometer is an ancient gauge used to measure the height of the Nile flood. A cataract is a stretch of rapids, and the Nile has six, between Aswan and Khartoum, which limited navigation in ancient times.

How it works: The Nile works as two rivers in one. The White Nile flows steadily all year, fed by the great lakes of East Africa, which store water and release it slowly. The Blue Nile is seasonal: summer rains in the Ethiopian highlands swell it from June to September, and it supplies about 80 percent of the Nile's water and nearly all of its silt during the flood. The two meet at Khartoum, and from there the Nile flows north through the Sahara, losing water to evaporation and seepage, and receiving almost no tributaries for the last 2,700 kilometres. The river's water is used by dams: the Aswan High Dam in Egypt stores the flood in Lake Nasser, one of the largest artificial lakes in the world, and releases it through the year for irrigation and electricity. The river's flow is also shaped by the seasons: the flood that once arrived in Egypt each summer now arrives as a managed release from the dam, and the delta, no longer fed by fresh silt, is slowly shrinking as the sea rises and the land sinks.

Worked examples: The White Nile begins at Lake Victoria, the largest lake in Africa, and flows through Uganda, where it passes the Murchison Falls, and through South Sudan, where it spreads into the Sudd, a vast swamp that loses half the river's water to evaporation. The Blue Nile begins at Lake Tana in Ethiopia and plunges over the Tis Issat Falls, known as the smoking water. At Khartoum the two branches meet, and the united Nile flows past the pyramids of Meroe, the ancient capital of the Kingdom of Kush, and past the temples of Abu Simbel, which were moved stone by stone in the 1960s to save them from Lake Nasser. Cairo, the largest city in Africa, stands on the river's banks near the head of the delta, and Alexandria, founded by Alexander the Great in 331 BCE, stands on the delta's edge. The Nile Delta is home to more than 40 million people, and its fields grow rice, cotton and wheat.

Real applications: The Nile's water is the lifeblood of Egypt and Sudan: more than 95 percent of Egypt's people live within a few kilometres of the river, and its water irrigates the fields that feed them. The Aswan High Dam generates electricity for Egypt and stores water for dry years, and the river's water is used for drinking, industry and fishing. The Nile is a transport route: boats carry goods and tourists along its length, and Nile cruises are a major part of Egypt's tourism. The river's history is a resource for archaeology: the temples and tombs along its banks are among the world's great heritage sites. For the countries upstream, the river is a source of hydroelectric power: Ethiopia's Renaissance Dam is designed to generate electricity for a country where most people have no power. For scientists, the Nile is a case study in how a river shapes a civilisation, and how a dam can transform a river's ecology, its delta and its politics.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the Nile the longest river in the world? Traditionally yes, at about 6,650 kilometres, but the Amazon's length is disputed: some measurements, using a different source, give the Amazon about 6,992 kilometres, which would make it longer. The two rivers' lengths depend on where the source is placed, and the question has no single agreed answer. Does the Nile flood every year? It did for thousands of years, but the Aswan High Dam, completed in 1970, ended the natural flood, and the river's flow is now controlled. A common misconception is that the Nile flows south; it flows north, from the highlands of East Africa to the Mediterranean, one of the few great rivers to do so. Another is that the Nile is a single river from its source; it is two rivers, the White Nile and the Blue Nile, which meet at Khartoum, and their waters are so different that the two can be told apart by colour. It is also wrong to think the Nile belongs to Egypt alone: eleven countries share its basin, and the river's future depends on agreements between them.

Amazon

ID: JAH-RIVRS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a tributary ladder: the Amazon's ten largest tributaries ranked by discharge, with the colour of each river's water, so the river system can be read as a family tree.

Definition: The Amazon is the greatest river in the world by the amount of water it carries, discharging about 209,000 cubic metres per second into the Atlantic Ocean, more than the next seven largest rivers combined, and about a fifth of all the river water that reaches the world's oceans. It is about 6,400 kilometres long, though some measurements give it about 6,992 kilometres, which would make it the longest river in the world, ahead of the Nile, and the question is still debated. It flows east across South America, from the Andes mountains of Peru to the Atlantic coast of Brazil, and its basin, about 7 million square kilometres, is the largest river basin on earth, covering about 40 percent of South America. The river and its rainforest are home to about a tenth of the world's known species.

History: The Amazon's history begins with its geology. The river is young: about ten million years ago, the rise of the Andes blocked the rivers that flowed west to the Pacific, and the water turned east, carving a path to the Atlantic and creating the basin. Humans have lived along the river for at least 11,000 years, and recent archaeology has shown that the rainforest was not a wilderness but a managed landscape, with towns, roads and farms, and a population that may have numbered in the millions before European contact. The Spanish explorer Francisco de Orellana was the first European to travel the river's full length, in 1541 to 1542, and he named it after the Amazons of Greek legend, after a battle with Indigenous women warriors. In the nineteenth century, the rubber boom brought wealth and suffering to the Amazon, and the city of Manaus grew rich on rubber, building an opera house in the jungle. In the twentieth century, roads, dams and cattle ranching began to clear the forest, and the river's future became a global concern: the Amazon rainforest stores carbon and makes rain, and its destruction affects the climate of the whole planet. Today the river is home to about 30 million people, including hundreds of Indigenous peoples, and its protection is one of the great environmental questions of our time.

Key concepts: Discharge is the volume of water passing a point each second, and the Amazon's discharge of about 209,000 cubic metres per second is the largest of any river. A basin is the whole area drained by a river and its tributaries, and the Amazon's basin covers about 7 million square kilometres. A tributary is a river that flows into a larger one; the Amazon has more than 1,000 tributaries, including the Negro, the Madeira and the Tapajós. A rainforest is a forest with high rainfall, and the Amazon rainforest is the largest in the world. A floodplain is the flat land beside a river that floods when the river rises; the Amazon's floodplain, the várzea, is flooded for months each year. The pororoca is a tidal bore, a wave that travels up the river from the Atlantic at the spring tide.

How it works: The Amazon works as a giant water machine. Rain falls on the Andes and on the forest itself, and the forest returns much of that water to the air through transpiration, the release of water vapour from leaves, so the forest makes its own rain: about half the rain that falls on the Amazon is recycled by the forest. The river rises and falls with the seasons: in the wet season, from December to May, the river can rise ten metres or more, flooding the forest for kilometres on either side, and fish swim among the trees, eating fruit and spreading seeds. The river's water is brown with sediment from the Andes in some tributaries, and black with organic matter in others, such as the Rio Negro, whose dark water is stained by leaves. The river carries about a billion tonnes of sediment to the Atlantic each year, building the delta and feeding the ocean. Dams on the tributaries, such as the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu, generate electricity but change the river's flow and block fish migration, and the river's future depends on how much of the forest is kept standing.

Worked examples: The river begins in the Andes, where the Marañón and the Ucayali join in Peru to form the Amazon proper. Manaus, the capital of the Brazilian state of Amazonas, stands at the Meeting of the Waters, where the dark Rio Negro and the muddy Solimões flow side by side for kilometres without mixing, a sight that draws visitors from around the world. Belém, at the river's mouth, is a port city where the river meets the Atlantic, and its market sells fish, fruit and forest products. The Madeira, the Amazon's largest tributary, carries more water than many of the world's great rivers on its own. The river is home to the pink river dolphin, the largest river dolphin in the world, and to the pirarucu, one of the largest freshwater fish, which can grow to three metres. The forest along the river holds about 400 billion trees, and its canopy is home to more species of birds than the whole of Europe.

Real applications: The Amazon's water and forest are resources for the whole planet: the river supplies drinking water and fish for millions of people, and its forest stores about 150 billion tonnes of carbon, more than a decade of human emissions. The river is a transport route: boats are the main way to travel in the Amazon, carrying people and goods between towns that have no roads, and the river's ports are the economic centres of the region. The forest is a source of medicines, foods and materials, and its plants have given the world rubber, cacao, quinine and many other products. For scientists, the Amazon is a living laboratory for studying biodiversity, climate and the water cycle, and its protection is a global priority: the forest's destruction would release vast amounts of carbon and change rainfall across South America. For the Indigenous peoples of the Amazon, the river and the forest are home, and their knowledge of its plants and animals is a resource for medicine and conservation.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the Amazon the longest river in the world? It depends on the measurement: the commonly cited length is about 6,400 kilometres, shorter than the Nile's 6,650, but some measurements, using a more distant source, give the Amazon about 6,992 kilometres, which would make it longer. The question is still debated, and the answer depends on where the source is placed. Is the Amazon rainforest the lungs of the world? It is often called that, but the phrase is misleading: the forest produces oxygen, but it also consumes nearly as much through decay, so its main role in the climate is storing carbon and making rain, not supplying oxygen. A common misconception is that the rainforest is untouched wilderness; it is not. Indigenous peoples have managed it for thousands of years, planting trees, building towns and shaping the forest. Another is that the river is safe to drink from anywhere; its water carries sediment and disease, and people along the river treat it before drinking. It is also wrong to think the Amazon is one river: it is a system of more than a thousand tributaries, and its basin is larger than the whole of India.

Yangtze

ID: JAH-RIVRS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a gorge ladder: the Yangtze's journey in four stages — plateau stream, mountain gorge, plain meander, delta — with the river's width and speed at each stage, so the whole descent can be followed.

Definition: The Yangtze is the longest river in Asia and the third-longest river in the world, about 6,300 kilometres long, flowing east across China from the Tibetan Plateau to the East China Sea at Shanghai. Its Chinese name is Chang Jiang, meaning Long River. It drains a basin of about 1.8 million square kilometres, about a fifth of China, and its average discharge is about 31,900 cubic metres per second. The river is the lifeblood of central China: about 400 million people live in its basin, and its valley grows rice, carries cargo, and generates electricity, including from the Three Gorges Dam, the largest hydroelectric dam in the world.

History: The Yangtze has been the centre of Chinese civilisation for thousands of years. Rice farming began in its valley more than 8,000 years ago, and the river's floodplain became the granary of China. The ancient state of Chu flourished along its middle reaches, and the river was the boundary between north and south China for centuries: in the third century CE, the Battle of Red Cliffs, fought on the river, decided the fate of the Three Kingdoms, and the river's gorges were a barrier that protected the south. In the nineteenth century, the river was opened to foreign trade after the Opium Wars, and steamships carried goods upriver to Chongqing. In the twentieth century, the river was the scene of war and flood: the 1931 flood killed hundreds of thousands, and the 1954 flood killed about 30,000, and the river's floods were among the deadliest disasters in history. The Three Gorges Dam, completed in 2012, was built to control floods and generate power, but it flooded towns and farmland and displaced more than a million people. Today the river is a highway: cargo ships carry goods from Shanghai to Chongqing, and the river's cities, from Wuhan to Nanjing, are among the largest in China.

Key concepts: A gorge is a narrow valley with steep sides, and the Three Gorges, Qutang, Wu and Xiling, are the most famous on the Yangtze, where the river cuts through mountains. A dam is a barrier built across a river to store water and generate electricity; the Three Gorges Dam is the largest in the world by power capacity. Discharge is the volume of water passing a point each second. A basin is the whole area drained by a river and its tributaries. A floodplain is the flat land beside a river that floods when the river rises. A tributary is a river that flows into a larger one; the Yangtze's largest tributaries include the Han and the Gan. The river's source is the high plateau of Tibet, where it begins as a small stream at an altitude of more than 5,000 metres.

How it works: The Yangtze works as a staircase of water. It begins on the Tibetan Plateau, drops rapidly through gorges in its upper reaches, then slows and widens across the plains of central China, where it meanders through lakes and floodplains, and finally flows through a delta at Shanghai into the sea. The river's flow is seasonal: summer monsoon rains swell it, and the Three Gorges Dam stores the flood water in a reservoir more than 600 kilometres long, releasing it through the dry season for irrigation and power. The dam's turbines generate about 100 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity a year, more than any other dam. The river also carries sediment: it once carried about 500 million tonnes a year to the sea, building the delta, but the dam now traps much of the sediment, and the delta is eroding. The river is navigable for ocean-going ships as far as Wuhan, about 1,000 kilometres inland, and for smaller ships as far as Chongqing, and its shipping carries more cargo than any other river in the world.

Worked examples: The river begins on the Tibetan Plateau as the Tuotuo, a small stream, and becomes the Jinsha, the River of Golden Sand, as it flows through the mountains of Yunnan. The Three Gorges, where the river cuts through the mountains, were famous for their beauty and for the danger of their rapids, and they are now flooded by the dam's reservoir. Chongqing, a city of more than 30 million in its municipality, stands at the upper end of the reservoir, and Wuhan, a city of 11 million, stands where the Han River joins the Yangtze. Nanjing, the former capital of China, stands on the river's lower reaches, and Shanghai, the largest city in China, stands at its mouth, where the river meets the sea. The river's delta is one of the most densely populated and economically important regions in the world, home to more than 100 million people. The river is home to the Yangtze finless porpoise, one of the few freshwater porpoises in the world, and the baiji, or Chinese river dolphin, which was declared extinct in 2006, the first dolphin driven to extinction by human activity.

Real applications: The Yangtze's water irrigates the rice fields that feed hundreds of millions of people, and its basin produces about half of China's grain. The river is the busiest inland waterway in the world: cargo ships carry coal, steel, grain and containers along its length, and its ports are among the largest in China. The Three Gorges Dam generates electricity for central China and controls floods that once killed hundreds of thousands. The river's cities are centres of industry, education and culture, and the river is a source of drinking water for tens of millions. For scientists, the Yangtze is a case study in how a great river can be managed, and in the costs of that management: the dam's benefits in power and flood control are balanced against the loss of farmland, the displacement of people, and the decline of the river's fish and dolphins. For historians, the river is the spine of Chinese civilisation, and its valley holds some of the oldest farming sites in the world.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the Yangtze the longest river in Asia? Yes, at about 6,300 kilometres, it is the longest in Asia and the third-longest in the world, after the Nile and the Amazon. Did the Three Gorges Dam stop the Yangtze's floods? It has reduced them, but it has not ended them: the dam can store flood water, but very large floods can still overwhelm it, and the river's lower reaches remain at risk. A common misconception is that the Yangtze is a single river from Tibet to the sea; it is a system of tributaries, and its name changes along its course: it is the Tuotuo at its source, the Jinsha in Yunnan, and the Chang Jiang in its lower reaches. Another is that the dam's reservoir is a lake; it is a flooded river valley, more than 600 kilometres long, and its water level rises and falls with the seasons. It is also wrong to think the river is clean: the Yangtze carries industrial and agricultural pollution, and its water quality is a major concern for the cities that depend on it.

Mississippi

ID: JAH-RIVRS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a confluence ladder: the Mississippi at four meetings — with the Missouri, the Ohio, the Arkansas and the sea — with the water each tributary brings, so the river's growth can be followed downstream.

Definition: The Mississippi is the great river of North America, about 3,766 kilometres long from its source at Lake Itasca in Minnesota to its delta in the Gulf of Mexico, and about 6,275 kilometres long when measured with its longest tributary, the Missouri, which makes the Mississippi-Missouri system the fourth-longest river system in the world. Its basin covers about 2.98 million square kilometres, about 40 percent of the United States, and its average discharge is about 16,800 cubic metres per second. The river is the spine of the American interior: it drains 31 states, carries grain and coal on barges, and its floodplain is some of the richest farmland in the world. Its name comes from the Ojibwe words "misi-ziibi", meaning great river.

History: The Mississippi has been a highway for thousands of years. Native American peoples built towns and mounds along its banks, including Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, which was the largest city in North America around 1200 CE, with perhaps 20,000 people. The French explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, travelled the river to its mouth in 1682 and claimed the whole basin for France, naming it Louisiana. In 1803, the United States bought the Louisiana Territory from France, and the river became the western boundary of the young nation. Steamboats transformed the river in the nineteenth century: Mark Twain, who worked as a river pilot, wrote about it in "Life on the Mississippi" and "Adventures of Huckleberry Finn", and the river became the setting for American literature. The river also carried the history of slavery: enslaved people were transported downriver, and the river's ports, New Orleans and Memphis, were centres of the slave trade. The great flood of 1927, one of the worst natural disasters in American history, inundated 27,000 square miles and displaced hundreds of thousands of people, and it led to the building of levees and floodways that still shape the river today. The flood of 1993 was the most expensive in American history, causing about 15 billion dollars in damage.

Key concepts: A tributary is a river that flows into a larger one; the Mississippi's great tributaries are the Missouri, the Ohio and the Arkansas. The Missouri is the longest tributary, and the Ohio carries more water than the Mississippi itself at their meeting point. A levee is a raised bank built to keep a river from flooding, and the Mississippi's levees are among the largest engineering works in the world. A floodplain is the flat land beside a river that floods when the river rises. A delta is the land built up where a river meets the sea; the Mississippi Delta is sinking, losing land to the Gulf of Mexico. Discharge is the volume of water passing a point each second. A basin is the whole area drained by a river and its tributaries.

How it works: The Mississippi works as a managed river. Its upper reaches are controlled by a series of 29 locks and dams, built to keep the river deep enough for barges, and its lower reaches are confined by levees that keep the river in its channel. The river's flow is seasonal: spring snowmelt and rain swell it, and the Army Corps of Engineers opens floodways, such as the Bonnet Carré Spillway near New Orleans, to divert water and protect the city. The river carries about 200 million tonnes of sediment a year, but the levees prevent it from spreading across the floodplain, so the delta, which was built by that sediment, is now sinking and shrinking: Louisiana loses about a football field of land every 100 minutes. The river's water is used for drinking, industry and irrigation, and its barges carry about 60 percent of America's grain exports. The river's ecology has been changed by the dams and levees: the floodplain forests are smaller, and the Gulf of Mexico at the river's mouth has a dead zone, an area of low oxygen caused by fertiliser washed down the river.

Worked examples: The river begins at Lake Itasca in Minnesota, where a small stream flows out of the lake, and it is possible to walk across the river at its source. St. Louis stands where the Missouri joins the Mississippi, and the Gateway Arch marks the city as the gateway to the West. Memphis, Tennessee, stands on a bluff above the river, and its music, the blues, grew up along the river's banks. Vicksburg, Mississippi, was the site of a decisive battle in the Civil War, fought for control of the river. New Orleans, near the river's mouth, is a city built below the river's level, protected by levees, and its French Quarter stands on the river's natural levee, the highest ground in the city. The Ohio River, which joins the Mississippi at Cairo, Illinois, carries more water than the Mississippi itself at that point, and the meeting of the two rivers is one of the great sights of American geography.

Real applications: The Mississippi's water irrigates the farms of the Midwest, which grow corn, soybeans and wheat for the world, and its barges carry grain, coal, steel and chemicals to the Gulf of Mexico for export. The river is a source of drinking water for millions of people, and its power plants and industries use its water for cooling. The river's floodplain is a major flyway for migratory birds, and its wetlands are home to fish, alligators and waterfowl. The river is a cultural resource: Mark Twain's books, the blues of Memphis, and the jazz of New Orleans all grew up along its banks, and the river is a symbol of America in literature and song. For engineers, the Mississippi is a case study in river management: the levees and floodways protect cities, but they also starve the delta of sediment, and the challenge now is to restore the delta while keeping the cities safe. For scientists, the river is a measure of the health of the American interior: its water quality reflects the farming, industry and cities of 31 states.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the Mississippi the longest river in the United States? The river's flow is managed by dams: the Guri Dam, on the Caroní tributary, is one of the largest hydroelectric dams in the world, and it generates most of Venezuela's electricity. The river's water is used for the oil industry of the Orinoco Belt, and its delta is a maze of channels where the Warao live.

The river's delta is home to the Warao people, who live in houses on stilts and travel by canoe, and to the scarlet ibis, a bird that nests in the mangroves. The river's basin is home to Angel Falls, on the Churún tributary, which drops 979 metres from a tepui, a flat-topped mountain, and is the tallest waterfall in the world. The river is home to the Orinoco crocodile, one of the largest in the world, and to the river dolphin, and to the piranha.

Real applications: The Orinoco's water generates electricity for Venezuela: the Guri Dam, on the Caroní, is one of the largest hydroelectric dams in the world, and it supplies most of the country's power. The river is a transport route: boats carry goods and people along it, and its ports are the economic centres of the region. The river is a source of fish for the peoples along its banks, and its llanos are a source of beef, and its delta is a source of oil: the Orinoco Belt holds some of the largest oil reserves in the world. The river is a cultural resource: its story is told in the songs of the llaneros, the cowboys of the plains, and in the novels of Rómulo Gallegos, who set "Doña Bárbara" on its banks. For scientists, the Orinoco is a case study in river bifurcation: the Casiquiare is one of the few rivers in the world that links two great river basins, and its study has shaped the understanding of how rivers work. For the peoples of Venezuela, the river is home, and its water is life.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is the Orinoco the longest river in Venezuela? Yes, at about 2,140 kilometres, it is the longest in Venezuela and one of the longest in South America. Does the Orinoco really connect to the Amazon? Yes, through the Casiquiare, a natural river that links the Orinoco to the Rio Negro, a tributary of the Amazon, so that water flows from one basin to the other, a phenomenon that was doubted by geographers until it was confirmed by Alexander von Humboldt in 1800. A common misconception is that the Orinoco is a tributary of the Amazon; it is not. It is a separate river, with its own basin and its own delta, though the Casiquiare links the two. Another is that the river is only a Venezuelan river; its basin touches Colombia and Brazil as well, and its tributaries bring water from the Guiana highlands and the llanos. It is also wrong to think the river is untouched: its dams, its oil and its fishing have changed it, and its delta is under pressure from development.
