SCIENCE PROOF
An inline duct fan moves air by volume, and its sizing rule is the volume rule: measure the space in cubic feet and choose a fan that can exchange that volume about once per minute, then add a margin for real-world losses. A carbon filter on the intake typically cuts effective airflow by roughly 20 to 25 percent, so the sources add a 25 percent buffer to the bare volume figure; a 4 by 4 by 8 foot tent is 128 cubic feet and needs a fan rated for at least about 160 CFM once the filter is counted. The fan in this record is a 6-inch unit rated at 402 CFM, which exchanges a 104 cubic foot tent nearly four times per minute at full speed and can therefore run well below full speed for quieter operation. Air velocity follows from the duct area: 402 cubic feet per minute through a 6-inch duct is about 10 metres per second, fast enough to carry humid air out but slow enough to keep duct noise down. EC motors matter because they hold their speed under load and draw less power than older AC motors at part speed; the 6-inch unit here draws about 40 watts at full speed, under one kilowatt-hour per day. HISTORY
The inline duct fan is a cylindrical fan that sits inside a run of ducting and pulls air through an enclosed path, and it became the standard ventilation tool for grow tents as indoor gardening grew. Earlier ventilation used axial fans that blew air around an open room; the inline fan instead mounts in the duct itself, so it can exhaust stale air from a tent through a carbon filter and out of the room. The common sizes are 4, 6, 8, and 10 inches of duct diameter, with the 4-inch fan moving about 190 to 210 CFM for a 2 by 2 or 2 by 4 tent and the 6-inch fan moving about 390 to 440 CFM for a 4 by 4 tent. Modern fans use EC motors with speed controllers and temperature and humidity sensors, and the best-known lines, such as the AC Infinity CLOUDLINE series, publish 205 CFM at 28 dBA for the 4-inch model and 402 CFM at 32 dBA for the 6-inch model.
