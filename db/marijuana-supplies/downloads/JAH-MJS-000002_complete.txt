SCIENCE PROOF
An electric grinder converts stored chemical energy into rotational kinetic energy. The lithium-ion cell holds charge as ions shuttle between electrodes; on discharge it delivers about 3.7 volts, and the motor turns that electrical power into torque on the blade shaft. Power equals voltage times current, so a 5-watt motor at 3.7 volts draws about 1.35 amps while running. The blades cut by the same shear action as a manual grinder, but the motor supplies many more cutting passes per second, which is why a load is ground in seconds rather than a dozen hand turns. Short bursts matter: running the motor too long adds friction heat and can turn herb toward powder. The battery capacity in milliamp-hours converts to watt-hours by multiplying volts by amp-hours, and dividing that stored energy by the energy of one grind gives the number of grinds per charge. USB-C charging simply refills the same cell through a standard 5-volt supply. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

MATH PROOF
The demo starts from the battery: 3.7 volts times 2000.0 milliamp-hours divided by 1000 gives the stored energy in watt-hours. One grind costs motor power times time: 5.0 watts times 6.0 seconds is 30.0 joules, which divided by 3600 gives watt-hours per grind. Dividing the stored watt-hours by the watt-hours per grind gives the number of grinds per charge, and dividing motor power by voltage gives the motor current in amps.
