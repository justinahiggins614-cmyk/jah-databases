The Humidity Control Pack

SCIENCE PROOF
A humidity control pack is applied physical chemistry: a saturated salt solution has a fixed equilibrium relative humidity. In a sealed packet, the salt solution sits in equilibrium with the water vapour above it, and that equilibrium is set by the salt, not by how much solution there is. The semipermeable membrane lets water vapour through but holds liquid water and dissolved salts inside, so the pack can trade vapour with the air in the jar without leaking. If the jar air is drier than the target, water evaporates from the solution, passes through the membrane as vapour, and raises the humidity; if the jar air is wetter, vapour condenses into the solution and the humidity falls. That two-way action is what separates these packs from a damp sponge, which can only add moisture, or a desiccant, which can only remove it. The capacity is finite: every gram of water the pack gives up or takes in moves the solution away from saturation, and once the salt is fully dissolved or fully crystallised the pack can no longer hold its target. This is why a pack cannot rescue wet flower: the moisture load inside under-dried flower is far larger than the few grams of water a small pack can exchange.

MATH PROOF
The reference pack targets 62 percent relative humidity with an accuracy of about plus or minus 2 percent, so in a truly airtight jar the humidity should sit between 62 - 2 = 60 percent and 62 + 2 = 64 percent. At 20 degrees Celsius, saturated air holds about 17.3 grams of water vapour per cubic metre, so air at 62 percent holds 17.3 x 0.62 = 10.73 grams per cubic metre. A quart jar has a headspace of about 236.5 cubic centimetres, which is 0.0002365 cubic metres, so the vapour in that headspace weighs 0.0002365 x 10.73 = 0.00254 grams. If the jar air starts at 40 percent relative humidity, it holds 17.3 x 0.40 = 6.92 grams per cubic metre, so the pack must add (10.73 - 6.92) x 0.0002365 = 0.00090 grams of vapour to the headspace alone; the far larger exchange is with the flower itself, which is why the pack is sized by flower weight, not by air volume. The size 67 pack covers 450 grams of flower, so the ratio is 450 / 67 = 6.7 grams of flower per gram of pack; the size 8 pack covers one ounce, 28.35 grams, giving 28.35 / 8 = 3.5 grams of flower per gram of pack, a more generous ratio for small jars.

HISTORY
Two-way humidity control was first developed for cigars, where a dry cigar burns hot and harsh and a damp cigar will not draw, and the same problem appears in any stored plant material. The Boveda pack is the best-known example: a saturated solution of pure water and natural salts sealed inside a semipermeable membrane that lets only water vapour pass. The idea is old in principle, since a saturated salt solution in a closed space settles at a fixed relative humidity that depends on the salt, but the packet form made it practical for a jar or a bag. Cannabis storage adopted the 58 percent and 62 percent packs, with 62 percent giving a softer, stickier feel and 58 percent a slightly drier one, and the packs are now sold from 8 grams to 320 grams to match containers from a pint jar to a pound of flower. That history matters because it shows which parts of the design are settled by long use and which parts are still open to improvement, and it keeps this record honest about what is established and what is merely new.
