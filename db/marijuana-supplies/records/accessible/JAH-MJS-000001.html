1. Description and history

The manual herb grinder is one of the basic supplies of the marijuana supplies archive: a manual herb grinder that works by two interlocking halves with opposing diamond-shaped teeth; twisting the halves in opposite directions shears dried herb caught between the teeth. It is made from an anodised aluminium (6061) body, stainless-steel teeth, a stainless-steel mesh screen, and a neodymium magnet lid closure, and a common example measures 63 mm diameter, 48 mm tall, in four pieces, with a grinding chamber 28 mm deep.

The herb grinder as a two-half cylindrical shredder is documented in the early 1900s, with William Wingfield and John Balding of Boort, Victoria, Australia patenting a novel grinding device in 1905, and the form was popularised in the 1960s as cannabis use spread. Early grinders were simple wood or metal cylinders; the modern four-piece aluminium grinder with a kief screen is a later refinement of that same two-half idea. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape.

In everyday use it is a simple, mechanical object with no electronics in the manual form and no hidden parts: what you see is what does the work, and its behaviour can be checked by measuring its parts and doing the arithmetic in this record.

The problem it solves: dried herb needs to be prepared evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good grinder has to hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Standard math — grinding-chamber volume (cylinder):

V = π · (d / 2)² · h

With d = 63.0 mm, h = 28.0 mm:

  • r = d / 2 = 31.5 mm
  • V = π · 31.5² · 28.0 = 87,282.87 mm³ = 87.28 cm³

Screen area (circle): A_screen = π · (d_screen / 2)² = π · 28.0² = 2,463.01 mm² (screen diameter 56.0 mm).

Tooth density: 49 teeth ÷ (π · 31.5² / 100) cm² = 1.57 teeth per cm² — a check that the teeth are spread evenly rather than crowded.

Cutting passes: 49 teeth × 12 turns = 588 passes in the worked example.

Every step can be repeated with a pocket calculator: each input is named, each unit is stated, and no hidden constant is used anywhere. The demo program prints these same values on two runs with matching SHA-256 digests (see Section 6).

4. Science proof

Shear, not crushing, is the working principle. Opposing diamond teeth pass each other with a small clearance, and the edges cut dried flower along its natural fracture planes. Aluminium 6061 is chosen because it is light, machines cleanly, and its anodised surface resists corrosion from plant resins. The teeth stay sharp because the load is spread across 49 teeth and each tooth meets only a small piece of herb at a time. Ground particles fall through sized holes in the grinding plate by gravity once they are smaller than the hole diameter, which is why a dozen steady turns produces a fairly uniform particle size instead of powder. The 100-micron mesh in a four-piece grinder is a sieve: particles finer than 100 microns, including the small resin glands called trichomes, pass through, while larger ground herb stays above. Friction is low because the herb is dry and brittle, and the magnet lid keeps the halves aligned so the teeth never clash metal on metal.

The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

Honest limit (from the record's skeptical analysis): this record does not prove that every example of this supply matches the dimensions given here, because makers vary sizes, materials, and tolerances, and the numbers describe a common example drawn from the cited sources. It does not prove that the supply performs the same way in every hand, because packing, temperature, and technique change the result. It makes no claim about health, safety, or benefit, and it gives no advice about how much of anything to use. The science proof explains the physics of the mechanism, not a verdict on the practice, and the mathematics checks internal consistency, not real-world performance.

Build it yourself (pilot extension, honestly labeled): a metal four-piece grinder cannot be made at home to the record's specification — machining anodised aluminium teeth and a 100-micron stainless mesh is a machine-shop job, and no home method produces it safely. The closest genuine maker activity is (a) assembling a bought kit — buy the four pieces in matching diameters, check the fit dry before any load goes in, confirm the teeth clear each other and the magnet faces meet flush — and (b) making a wooden stand and a cleaning brush: cut a circular recess the diameter of the grinder foot into a hardwood block, sand it smooth, line it with cork so the aluminium does not rest on bare wood. Check the fit dry, work slowly, and keep the finished piece clean and dry in storage.

3D-print directions (pilot extension — actual directions a printer can use): the metal grinder itself is not home-3D-printable — no home filament prints anodised aluminium or a stainless mesh, and printed plastic teeth wear flat quickly and cannot hold the 100-micron sieve tolerance. The printable items are a protective stand/cradle and a replacement lid grip for the grinder. Print them in PETG (tolerates warm water for cleaning and does not crack as easily as PLA), with a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer height, 4 perimeters, and 25 percent gyroid infill. Print the cradle flat on its bottom face with no supports, so the circular recess prints cleanly; print the lid grip on its side with supports under the overhang. Print time (ESTIMATE): 2 hours 40 minutes for the cradle and 35 minutes for the lid grip, at the settings given. Unmodelled factors that change real print time and strength are wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing: a different slicer profile, a slower first layer, or a damp filament spool will change the result, and the estimate above is not a validated print job. A printed grinder body is a display model only, not a working grinder: printed teeth will not shear dried herb the way steel teeth do, and the printed part must not be presented as equivalent to the metal supply. Check the finished accessory against the grinder it serves before use, and reprint it if the fit is loose.
