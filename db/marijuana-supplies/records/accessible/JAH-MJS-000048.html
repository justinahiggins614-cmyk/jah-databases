The drying rack is a hanging tower of mesh tiers that holds plant material in the air so it can dry evenly, and it descends from the herb-drying frames and net bags that gardeners have used for centuries. The modern form is a collapsible cylinder of polyester mesh over a flexible steel wireframe, with zippered chambers stacked one above another and a hook at the top so the whole tower hangs from a bar. The standard example in this record is the six-tier rack, 600 millimetres across and 1,194 millimetres long when hanging, with each chamber 171 millimetres high; a four-tier version, 457 millimetres across, fits a 2 by 2 tent, and eight-tier versions reach 1,700 millimetres. The rack collapses flat into a carry bag when the season is over, and it springs open from the bag in seconds. The record here describes the rack as a product — its tiers, its mesh, its frame, its hook — and it does not tell anyone how to dry any particular plant or how long any drying or curing should take. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage of that change.

Materials and design

A drying rack is a tower of mesh and wire, and its design is a balance between holding a load and folding flat. The mesh is non-toxic polyester, chosen because it is breathable, washable, and strong enough to hold material without stretching, and because it lets air pass through the floor of each tier as well as around the sides. The frame is flexible steel wire, chosen because it holds each disc open under load and still folds flat into a carry bag when the season is over; the sources describe the wire as rust-proof and high-tensile. Each tier is a disc 600 millimetres across in the standard example, giving about 0.28 square metres of area, and the chambers are 171 millimetres high, which is generous enough to lay material in a single layer without pressing it against the tier above. The zipper on each chamber is the detail that makes the rack work: it keeps the material contained and undisturbed while the mesh still breathes, and it lets one tier be opened without disturbing the others. The hanging point is a single steel hook with a Velcro loop, so the whole tower hangs from one bar and air can move around it on all sides. Tolerances are sewing tolerances: the mesh is cut to the wireframe within a few millimetres, the zippers are sewn straight so they do not jam, and the tiers are spaced evenly so the tower hangs straight. Design varies by size: 2, 4, 6, and 8 tiers, diameters from 457 to 900 millimetres, and some versions have removable tiers that unclip into sections.

4. Science proof

A drying rack works by giving air access to material from every direction at once. Each tier is a flat disc of open mesh, so air passes through the floor of the chamber as well as around its sides, and the material is spread in a single layer instead of piled in a heap where the middle stays damp. The mesh is non-toxic polyester, chosen because it is breathable, washable, and strong enough to hold a load without stretching, and the frame is flexible steel wire, chosen because it holds the disc open under load and still folds flat for storage. The numbers show the scale: one tier of the six-tier rack is 600 millimetres across, giving about 0.28 square metres of drying area per tier and about 1.70 square metres across all six, with each chamber about 171 millimetres high and about 48 litres of space. The zipper on each chamber matters because it keeps the material contained and undisturbed while the mesh still breathes, and the single hanging point matters because the whole tower hangs clear of the floor and air can move around it on all sides. Build it yourself

A drying rack is a genuine woodworking and sewing build, and it can be made from dowels, mesh, and cord. Cut six discs of polyester mesh 600 millimetres across, and cut six wooden rings from 12 millimetre dowel bent into circles 600 millimetres across, or use ready-made embroidery hoops of the same size as the rings. Sew each mesh disc to its ring with a whip stitch around the full circle, pulling the mesh taut so it does not sag in the middle. Cut three lengths of cord 1,400 millimetres long, and tie them to the rings at three points spaced evenly around the circle, spacing the rings 171 millimetres apart along the cords so the chambers match the standard rack. Tie the three cords together at the top into a single hanging loop, and hang the tower from a hook or a bar, checking that it hangs straight and that each tier stays level. Test the finished rack by placing a light load on each tier and checking that the mesh does not sag onto the tier below. A second genuine maker task is a flat version: a wooden frame with mesh stretched across it, laid on a table, which gives the same single-layer drying surface without the hanging tower. This build makes the rack only; it says nothing about what goes on it or how long anything should stay there.

3D-print version

A drying rack itself is not 3D-printable: its tiers are polyester mesh and its frame is steel wire, and no home printer prints mesh or wire, so a printed rack would be a solid plastic disc with no airflow and would not dry anything. This record says so plainly and gives directions for printable accessories instead: tier clips and a hook adapter. Print the tier clips in PETG, which is tough and survives being handled with wet hands: use a 0.4 millimetre nozzle, 0.2 millimetre layer height, 3 walls, and 30 percent gyroid infill, printed flat on the bed with no supports. The clips are C-shaped, 50 millimetres long, and snap over the wireframe ring to hold a label tag or to join two tiers together. Print the hook adapter in PETG at the same settings, a ring that fits over a tent roof bar and holds the rack hook so it cannot slide along the bar. Print time (estimate): about 55 minutes for a set of six tier clips and 30 minutes for the hook adapter on a standard desktop printer. After printing, test the clips on the real wireframe ring, sand the edges lightly, and wash both parts in warm soapy water. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness changes the clip grip, nozzle settings change stringing in the C-shape, printer motion affects the roundness of the adapter ring, and PETG shrinks slightly as it cools, so print one clip first and test the fit before printing the set. The clips never carry the full weight of the rack.

Print times are estimates, not validated print jobs. Unmodelled real-world factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, material-specific slicing.

"""Demo: drying rack — The Drying Rack."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    tiers = 6; d_mm = 600.0; chamber_h = 171.0; length_mm = 1194.0; capacity_kg = 3.0
    tier_area = math.pi * (d_mm / 2.0) ** 2 / 1000000.0
    total_area = tier_area * tiers
    tier_vol = tier_area * (chamber_h / 1000.0) * 1000.0
    total_vol = tier_vol * tiers
    per_tier_kg = capacity_kg / tiers
    return {"tiers": tiers, "diameter_mm": d_mm, "chamber_height_mm": chamber_h, "hanging_length_mm": length_mm, "tier_area_m2": round(tier_area, 4), "total_area_m2": round(total_area, 3), "tier_volume_L": round(tier_vol, 1), "total_volume_L": round(total_vol, 1), "capacity_kg": capacity_kg, "per_tier_kg": round(per_tier_kg, 2)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    print(f"tiers: {a['tiers']}")
    print(f"diameter_mm: {a['diameter_mm']}")
    print(f"chamber_height_mm: {a['chamber_height_mm']}")
    print(f"hanging_length_mm: {a['hanging_length_mm']}")
    print(f"tier_area_m2: {a['tier_area_m2']}")
    print(f"total_area_m2: {a['total_area_m2']}")
    print(f"tier_volume_L: {a['tier_volume_L']}")
    print(f"total_volume_L: {a['total_volume_L']}")
    print(f"capacity_kg: {a['capacity_kg']}")
    print(f"per_tier_kg: {a['per_tier_kg']}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
