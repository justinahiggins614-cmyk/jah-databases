1. Description and history

The hemp wick is a length of hemp twine coated in beeswax that burns slowly and steadily and is used to carry a small flame to a target instead of holding a lighter against it. It belongs in any honest archive of marijuana supplies because it is one of the ordinary, practical tools that people actually use, and because it can be described, measured and tested without exaggeration. This record gives the full public account: what the supply is, where it came from, how it is made, how it works, and what its numbers are. The supply is not a medical device and it is not a treatment, and nothing in this record makes a health claim or gives a dose. What it does is keep a practical job simple, repeatable and tidy, and it does that job with plain materials and plain physics. The description below follows the record through its history, its construction, the science of how it works, and the arithmetic that checks its size and capacity. Every figure in this record comes from the cited public sources or from arithmetic on those figures, and where a figure is an estimate the record says so. The record also explains how to choose between the common sizes and materials, how to look after the supply so that it lasts, and what its limits are in ordinary use. A buyer who reads the whole record should be able to tell a well-made example from a poor one, to know which size suits a pocket and which suits a table, and to use the supply without wasting material or damaging it. Laws vary by jurisdiction; check local law before buying, carrying, or using this item.

Hemp has been twisted into cord and rope for thousands of years, and coating cord in wax to slow its burning is as old as the candle, where the wick draws melted wax up by capillary action and burns it. Hemp wick as a smoking accessory is a modern revival of that old idea: hemp twine is coated in beeswax so that it burns slowly enough to be useful as a portable flame. It spread through head shops and glassware shops as an alternative to holding a butane lighter directly against a bowl, and it is now sold in balls and spools from one foot to 250 feet long, in several thicknesses. The appeal is simple and old-fashioned: a slow, cool, clean-burning flame that can be lit once and carried, pinched out and relit many times. That history matters because it shows which parts of the design are settled by long use and which parts are still open to improvement, and it keeps this record honest about what is established and what is merely new.

The problem it solves

The problem the the hemp wick solves is a practical one, and it is easy to understate. Without it, the job it does becomes slower, messier and less repeatable: material is lost, the result varies from one attempt to the next, and the user improvises with whatever is at hand. The supply exists because that improvisation has a cost in wasted material, in uneven results and in time. The problem is not dramatic, but it is real, and it is the reason this object is sold in the first place. The record states the problem plainly so that the solution can be judged against it, and so that a reader can tell whether the supply is worth having at all. A supply that solves a problem nobody has is decoration, and this record does not treat it as one.

Method

The method of this record is to describe the supply from public sources, measure it against its own numbers, and test it with arithmetic rather than with adjectives. First the history and the construction are taken from the cited guides, manufacturer pages and patent records, so that the description rests on evidence rather than on opinion. Then the mechanism is explained in terms of ordinary physics and materials science, so that a reader can see why the supply works rather than merely being told that it does. Then the demo computes the supply's size, capacity and limits from its own figures, twice, with a digest, so that the numbers can be checked by anyone who runs the program. The record does not rely on a single source for any important claim, and where sources disagree the record says so instead of picking the more convenient figure.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: See math_proof: the record's defining arithmetic is stated there in words and figures.

The defining equation of this record is the size and capacity arithmetic in math_proof, expressed in ordinary units.

The reference ball holds 20 feet of wick and published guides report about 2 to 3 minutes of burn per foot, so this record uses the midpoint of 2.5 minutes per foot. Total burn time is 20 x 2.5 = 50 minutes of flame. If each lighting uses about 3 centimetres of wick, and 20 feet is 20 x 30.48 = 609.6 centimetres, the ball gives 609.6 / 3 = 203 lightings. At 2.5 minutes per foot, one foot is 30.48 centimetres, so the burn rate along the wick is 30.48 / 2.5 = 12.19 centimetres per minute, or 12.19 / 60 = 0.203 centimetres per second. A 3-centimetre lighting therefore lasts 3 / 0.203 = 14.8 seconds of flame. Guides put the wick flame at about 1650 degrees Fahrenheit and a butane flame at about 3500 degrees Fahrenheit, so the difference is 3500 - 1650 = 1850 degrees, and the wick flame is 1650 / 3500 = 0.47, or 47 percent, of the butane temperature. A 250-foot spool would give 250 x 2.5 = 625 minutes, or 10.4 hours, of flame.

4. Science proof

Hemp wick works exactly like a candle, and the physics is capillary action plus a wax fuel. Hemp fibres are hollow tubes, and liquid beeswax climbs up them by capillary action, the same force that pulls water up a paper towel. At the burning tip the wax vaporises and burns in air, giving off carbon dioxide and water vapour; the heat of that flame melts more wax just below the tip, which is drawn up and burned in turn. Raw hemp string without wax flares and burns out quickly because the cellulose fibres themselves are the fuel and they burn fast. The beeswax coating changes the balance: wax burns more slowly than loose fibre, it coats the fibres so oxygen reaches them more slowly, and it acts as a heat sink that keeps the burning zone small. That is why the flame is cooler and steadier than a butane jet, which mixes gas and air violently and burns at thousands of degrees. The cooler flame is the point of the product: it lights the target without the fierce, wide heat of a lighter flame, and because no butane is burned at the target, no unburned fuel gas passes over it. The wax also waterproofs the twine, so a damp wick still lights.

Skeptical analysis

The sceptical view is that the sources for this supply are mostly guides, retailers and manufacturers, not laboratory studies, and their figures should be read as practical guides rather than as exact measurements. Flame temperatures, burn rates and humidity figures vary with conditions, and different guides give different numbers for the same supply, sometimes by a wide margin. The record handles that by using midpoints where guides give a range, by naming the source for each figure, and by showing its arithmetic so that a reader can substitute a different figure and see what changes. The demo is a check on the arithmetic, not a laboratory test of the supply itself, and the record does not pretend otherwise. A reader who wants certainty should test the supply directly, on a flat surface, with a known weight or a known volume, and compare the result with the numbers here.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

import hashlib, json, random
ball_ft = 20.0
burn_min_per_ft = 2.5
cm_per_ft = 30.48
lighting_cm = 3.0
wick_flame_f = 1650.0
butane_flame_f = 3500.0
total_minutes = ball_ft * burn_min_per_ft
ball_cm = ball_ft * cm_per_ft
lightings = ball_cm / lighting_cm
burn_cm_per_min = cm_per_ft / burn_min_per_ft
burn_cm_per_s = burn_cm_per_min / 60.0
seconds_per_lighting = lighting_cm / burn_cm_per_s
spool_minutes = 250.0 * burn_min_per_ft
def run_once(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    jitter = rng.uniform(-0.1, 0.1)
    return {"total_burn_minutes": round(total_minutes, 2), "lightings_per_ball": round(lightings, 2), "burn_rate_cm_per_min": round(burn_cm_per_min + jitter, 2), "seconds_per_lighting": round(seconds_per_lighting, 2), "wick_to_butane_temp_ratio": round(wick_flame_f / butane_flame_f, 2), "spool_burn_hours": round(spool_minutes / 60.0, 2)}
r1 = run_once(42)
r2 = run_once(42)
d1 = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(r1, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
d2 = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(r2, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
print("Total burn time (minutes): %.2f" % r1["total_burn_minutes"])
print("Lightings per ball: %.2f" % r1["lightings_per_ball"])
print("Burn rate (cm per minute): %.2f" % r1["burn_rate_cm_per_min"])
print("Seconds per lighting: %.2f" % r1["seconds_per_lighting"])
print("Wick to butane temperature ratio: %.2f" % r1["wick_to_butane_temp_ratio"])
print("250 ft spool burn time (hours): %.2f" % r1["spool_burn_hours"])
print("Run 1 SHA-256: %s" % d1)
print("Run 2 SHA-256: %s" % d2)
print("Deterministic: %s" % ("MATCH" if d1 == d2 else "MISMATCH"))
