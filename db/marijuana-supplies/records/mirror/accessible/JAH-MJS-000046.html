The inline duct fan is a cylindrical fan that sits inside a run of ducting and pulls air through an enclosed path, and it became the standard ventilation tool for grow tents as indoor gardening grew. Earlier ventilation used axial fans that blew air around an open room; the inline fan instead mounts in the duct itself, so it can exhaust stale air from a tent through a carbon filter and out of the room. The common sizes are 4, 6, 8, and 10 inches of duct diameter, with the 4-inch fan moving about 190 to 210 CFM for a 2 by 2 or 2 by 4 tent and the 6-inch fan moving about 390 to 440 CFM for a 4 by 4 tent. Modern fans use EC motors with speed controllers and temperature and humidity sensors, and the best-known lines, such as the AC Infinity CLOUDLINE series, publish 205 CFM at 28 dBA for the 4-inch model and 402 CFM at 32 dBA for the 6-inch model. The record here describes the fan as a product — its size, its airflow, its power, its noise — and not how to grow anything in the space it ventilates. Materials and design

An inline duct fan is a small machine built around one job: moving a known volume of air through a duct at a known noise level. The housing is ABS plastic in the standard example and steel in the premium version: ABS is light, cheap, and quiet, while steel is heavier and survives knocks in a workshop. Inside, an EC motor spins an aluminium impeller; EC motors are chosen because they hold their speed under load, run quietly at part speed, and draw less power than older AC motors when turned down. The impeller blades are curved to push air along the duct rather than churn it, and the housing is a smooth cylinder so the air path has no sharp edges to whistle. The speed controller is a separate box on the standard version and a smart controller with temperature and humidity sensors on the premium version; the controller sets the motor speed and therefore the airflow. Noise is a design number, not an afterthought: the 6-inch unit is rated at 32 dBA at full speed, and running it below full speed drops the noise further, which is why the fan is sized above the minimum so it rarely runs flat out. Tolerances are machining tolerances: the impeller must spin true inside the housing with a gap of a millimetre or two, or it will rub and buzz, and the duct flanges must match the duct diameter exactly so the clamps seal without leaking air. The finish is plain moulded plastic or powder-coated steel, with no coating inside the air path.

4. Science proof

An inline duct fan moves air by volume, and its sizing rule is the volume rule: measure the space in cubic feet and choose a fan that can exchange that volume about once per minute, then add a margin for real-world losses. A carbon filter on the intake typically cuts effective airflow by roughly 20 to 25 percent, so the sources add a 25 percent buffer to the bare volume figure; a 4 by 4 by 8 foot tent is 128 cubic feet and needs a fan rated for at least about 160 CFM once the filter is counted. The fan in this record is a 6-inch unit rated at 402 CFM, which exchanges a 104 cubic foot tent nearly four times per minute at full speed and can therefore run well below full speed for quieter operation. Air velocity follows from the duct area: 402 cubic feet per minute through a 6-inch duct is about 10 metres per second, fast enough to carry humid air out but slow enough to keep duct noise down. EC motors matter because they hold their speed under load and draw less power than older AC motors at part speed; the 6-inch unit here draws about 40 watts at full speed, under one kilowatt-hour per day. The demo computes the duct area, the air velocity, the exchanges per minute for the tent, and the daily energy, so the reader can check the sizing arithmetic directly. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the fan, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

There is no genuine do-it-yourself build for an inline duct fan: winding an EC motor, balancing an impeller, and rating a fan at 402 CFM needs a factory and test equipment, so a home-made fan would be a rough imitation that moves far less air than its label claims. This record says so plainly and gives the closest genuine maker activity instead: assembling a duct run and mounting a bought fan in it. Measure the run from the tent port to the outside, cut flexible ducting to length with a little slack, and fit a hose clamp over each end. Mount the fan high in the tent, because warm humid air rises, using the steel mounting brackets and screws, and check that the airflow arrow on the housing points out of the tent. Push the ducting onto the fan flanges and tighten the clamps until the ducting cannot be pulled off by hand, then fit the carbon filter on the intake side of the fan with its own clamp. Connect the speed controller to the fan and to mains power, switch it on at a low speed, and check that air flows out of the tent by holding a strip of paper near the exhaust: it should be pulled toward the duct. Any mains wiring beyond a plug-in controller should be done by a qualified electrician, and the record says so plainly. Never run the fan with the intake blocked, because the motor will overheat.

3D-print version

The inline duct fan itself is not 3D-printable as a working fan: its EC motor and balanced impeller cannot be printed, and a printed impeller would spin out of balance and fail. This record says so plainly and gives directions for printable accessories instead: a duct adapter and a mounting bracket. Print the duct adapter in PETG, which is tough and holds its shape in warm air: use a 0.4 millimetre nozzle, 0.2 millimetre layer height, 4 walls, and 40 percent gyroid infill, printed upright with the bore vertical so no supports are needed inside the bore. The adapter steps a 6-inch duct down to a 4-inch duct, with a 20 millimetre straight section at each end for the hose clamps. Print the mounting bracket in PETG at the same settings, a flat plate with two bolt holes and a curved cradle that matches the fan housing. Print time (estimate): about 2 hours 10 minutes for the adapter and 50 minutes for the bracket on a standard desktop printer. After printing, test the adapter on real ducting, ream the bore lightly if it is tight, and check that the bracket holds the fan without flexing. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness changes the bore diameter, supports inside a bore would ruin the surface, nozzle settings change stringing in the bore, printer motion affects the roundness of the adapter, and PETG shrinks slightly as it cools, so measure the printed bore with calipers before fitting it. The printed parts are accessories, not the fan.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: inline duct fan — Signature Inline Duct Fan."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    d_in = 8.0; cfm = 807.0; power_w = 60.0; tent_ft3 = 162.5
    d_m = d_in * 0.0254
    duct_area = math.pi * (d_m / 2.0) ** 2
    flow_m3s = cfm * 0.000471947
    velocity = flow_m3s / duct_area
    exchanges = cfm / tent_ft3
    energy_day = power_w * 24.0 / 1000.0
    return {"duct_diameter_in": d_in, "duct_area_m2": round(duct_area, 5), "airflow_cfm": cfm, "velocity_ms": round(velocity, 2), "exchanges_per_min": round(exchanges, 2), "power_w": power_w, "energy_kwh_per_day": round(energy_day, 2)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    print(f"duct_diameter_in: {a['duct_diameter_in']}")
    print(f"duct_area_m2: {a['duct_area_m2']}")
    print(f"airflow_cfm: {a['airflow_cfm']}")
    print(f"velocity_ms: {a['velocity_ms']}")
    print(f"exchanges_per_min: {a['exchanges_per_min']}")
    print(f"power_w: {a['power_w']}")
    print(f"energy_kwh_per_day: {a['energy_kwh_per_day']}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
