1. Description and history

The Signature the digital pocket scale is a Signature-made design study of a small battery-powered scale that measures weight with a strain-gauge load cell and displays it to one hundredth of a gram, drawn as a design for the Signature edition of the marijuana supplies database. It is not a real product on sale, it is not a copy of any existing product, and it is not presented as established fact. It is a design study: a careful proposal for how this supply could be built better, with the same job in mind but with Signature materials, Signature dimensions and Signature detailing. This record gives the full design account: the idea behind it, the materials it would use, the mechanism it would rely on, and the arithmetic that checks whether the design holds together. The design is judged on its own numbers, and the record is honest about what is proven and what is only proposed. Nothing here is a medical claim and nothing here gives a dose. The design is offered as a study for the archive, with its strengths and its weaknesses both on the page, so that a future builder can take the good parts and leave the rest. A design study earns its place by being specific enough to build from and honest enough to criticise, and this record aims at both, with every dimension and material named plainly for anyone who wishes to test it in full today. Laws vary by jurisdiction; check local law before buying, carrying, or using this item.

Weighing small amounts accurately used to need a balance beam and a set of brass weights, and a pocket scale that could do it did not exist until the strain gauge and the cheap liquid-crystal display arrived. A strain gauge is a thin metal pattern whose electrical resistance changes as it is stretched, and when it is bonded to a small metal beam it turns a tiny bending of the beam under load into an electrical signal. By the 2000s that technology, a small processor and a pair of CR2032 batteries could be packed into a device the size of a phone, and the 50-gram by 0.01-gram pocket scale became a standard tool for jewellery, herbs, spices and small parts. The design has stayed stable since: a 50-gram capacity with 0.01-gram readability is the common size, with 100-gram and 500-gram versions for larger loads, and calibration against a known 50-gram weight keeps it honest. The Signature design takes that history as its starting point and asks what the next version should keep and what it should change, keeping the proven shape and reworking the weak points in materials, sealing and handling.

The problem it solves

The design problem for the Signature the digital pocket scale is the same problem the public version faces, stated as a design brief. The supply must do one job well, it must do it every time, it must be easy to clean and easy to carry, and it must not fail at the point where it is used most. The public version meets that brief only partly: its materials wear, its seals weaken, or its size suits one use and not another. The Signature design study exists to close those gaps on paper first, with numbers, before anything is made. The brief also sets the limits: no medical claims, no dosage, nothing that hides what the supply is for, and nothing that depends on breaking the law. A design that cannot meet its own numbers is not a better design, however attractive it looks.

Method

The Signature method is to start from the public supply, keep the mechanism that works, and redesign the parts that wear out or waste material. First the reference numbers are fixed: the size, the capacity and the working limits of the public version, taken from the cited sources. Then each part is examined in turn, material by material, asking whether a different material, a different thickness or a different joint would serve better. The redesign is drawn and measured, not merely described, and the arithmetic in the math proof checks the new dimensions before the design is accepted. Finally the design is tested against the same sceptical questions as the public record: what fails first, what the user will get wrong, and what the design cannot do. Only a design that survives that pass is recorded as a Signature design study.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: See math_proof: the record's defining arithmetic is stated there in words and figures.

The defining equation of this record is the size and capacity arithmetic in math_proof, expressed in ordinary units.

The reference scale has a capacity of 50 grams and a readability of 0.01 grams, so the number of distinct readings it can display is 50 / 0.01 = 5000 steps. Its published accuracy tolerance is about plus or minus 0.03 grams, which is 0.03 / 0.01 = 3 readability steps. A load of 1.00 grams therefore reads between 1.00 - 0.03 = 0.97 grams and 1.00 + 0.03 = 1.03 grams, a relative error of 0.03 / 1.00 = 0.03, or 3 percent. A load of 25.00 grams reads between 24.97 and 25.03 grams, a relative error of 0.03 / 25.00 = 0.0012, or 0.12 percent, which shows why the scale is far more accurate, in relative terms, near the middle of its range. One ounce is 28.3495 grams, so a one-ounce load is 28.35 grams on the display, and one gram is 1 / 28.3495 = 0.03527 ounces. Calibration uses a 50-gram weight, and if the scale reads 49.97 grams for that weight the error is 50.00 - 49.97 = 0.03 grams, exactly at the tolerance limit, so the scale passes but should be recalibrated soon.

4. Science proof

A digital pocket scale is a force transducer with a display. The heart is the load cell, a small aluminium or steel beam with a strain gauge bonded to it: the gauge is a zigzag of thin metal foil, and when the beam bends under a load the foil stretches, its cross-section narrows, and its electrical resistance rises. Four gauges are usually wired as a Wheatstone bridge, so the tiny resistance change becomes a small voltage that the processor can measure. The processor compares that voltage with the value stored during calibration, when a known 50-gram weight was placed on the platform, and converts the ratio into a weight in grams. Readability of 0.01 grams means the display changes in steps of one hundredth of a gram, but readability is not accuracy: the published tolerance is about plus or minus 0.03 grams, and draughts, vibration, temperature and an unlevel surface all move the reading. Tare works by storing the container voltage as the new zero and subtracting it from every later reading. The scale is honest only when it is level, still, warm and recently calibrated, which is why every manual repeats the same four instructions, and why a reading taken on a wobbling table should never be trusted.

Skeptical analysis

The sceptical view of this design study is that a drawing is not a product, and a design that has never been built has never failed in the ways real products fail. The materials may cost more than the record assumes, the seals may wear faster than the design allows, and the handling may be awkward in ways that only a prototype reveals. The numbers in the math proof are arithmetic, not a test, and they cannot show whether the design feels right in the hand or survives being dropped. The record accepts all of that: the design study is a proposal, and it is labelled as one, so that nobody mistakes it for a tested product. The honest next step is a prototype, a real test, and a revision, and until that happens the design should be read as a careful idea, not as a finished thing.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

import hashlib, json, random
capacity_g = 50.0
readability_g = 0.01
tolerance_g = 0.03
cal_weight_g = 50.0
steps = capacity_g / readability_g
tolerance_steps = tolerance_g / readability_g
load1_g = 1.00
load25_g = 25.00
rel_err_1g = tolerance_g / load1_g * 100.0
rel_err_25g = tolerance_g / load25_g * 100.0
oz_per_g = 1.0 / 28.3495
one_oz_g = 28.3495
cal_error_g = 50.00 - 49.97
def run_once(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    jitter = rng.uniform(-0.001, 0.001)
    return {"display_steps": round(steps, 2), "tolerance_in_steps": round(tolerance_steps, 2), "relative_error_1g_pct": round(rel_err_1g + jitter, 2), "relative_error_25g_pct": round(rel_err_25g, 2), "grams_per_ounce": round(one_oz_g, 2), "ounces_per_gram": round(oz_per_g, 5), "calibration_error_g": round(cal_error_g, 2)}
r1 = run_once(42)
r2 = run_once(42)
d1 = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(r1, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
d2 = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(r2, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
print("Display steps: %.2f" % r1["display_steps"])
print("Tolerance in steps: %.2f" % r1["tolerance_in_steps"])
print("Relative error at 1 g (percent): %.2f" % r1["relative_error_1g_pct"])
print("Relative error at 25 g (percent): %.2f" % r1["relative_error_25g_pct"])
print("Grams per ounce: %.2f" % r1["grams_per_ounce"])
print("Ounces per gram: %.5f" % r1["ounces_per_gram"])
print("Calibration error (g): %.2f" % r1["calibration_error_g"])
print("Run 1 SHA-256: %s" % d1)
print("Run 2 SHA-256: %s" % d2)
print("Deterministic: %s" % ("MATCH" if d1 == d2 else "MISMATCH"))
