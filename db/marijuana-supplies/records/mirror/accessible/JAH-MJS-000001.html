1. Description and history

The Signature Helix Grinder is a Signature-made design study for manual herb grinder, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — two interlocking halves with opposing diamond-shaped teeth; twisting the halves in opposite directions shears dried herb caught between the teeth — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature anodised aluminium body with a helical tooth ring; stainless-steel mesh; ceramic-coated grinding plate. The design is sized at 70 mm diameter x 52 mm tall; grinding chamber 30 mm deep; 4 pieces, which sets every number in this record, from the volumes and areas in the demo to the scale variants at the end. This record is a design study, not a real manufactured product, and nothing here claims the design has been built, tested, or sold. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, run the program, and see exactly where each number comes from. The Signature edition exists alongside the public edition so the archive holds both what exists in the world and what the Signature system designs next, clearly labelled, so the two are never confused. Like every Signature record, it is filed as a design study, with its own generator and its own file AI, and it marches with the rest of the database toward a million records.

The herb grinder as a two-half cylindrical shredder is documented in the early 1900s, with William Wingfield and John Balding of Boort, Victoria, Australia patenting a novel grinding device in 1905, and the form was popularised in the 1960s as cannabis use spread. Early grinders were simple wood or metal cylinders; the modern four-piece aluminium grinder with a kief screen is a later refinement of that same two-half idea. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. Museums, trade guides, and manufacturer manuals preserve the steps of that change, and the sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage. The history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: manual herb grinder needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Method

The method in this record is to describe the supply from its parts, its dimensions, and its mechanism, and then to check those claims with arithmetic and a runnable program. First the parts are named and their materials given, so the reader knows what is metal, what is glass, and what is paper. Then the dimensions are stated in millimetres, grams, watts, or litres, so every later number has a source. Then the mechanism is explained step by step, in the order the user actually follows it, so the reader can picture the motion or the flow. The science proof explains the physics behind that mechanism, and the math proof works the arithmetic from the stated dimensions. Finally the demo program computes the same quantities from the same numbers, prints them with units, and runs twice with SHA-256 digests, so the reader can repeat the whole check on their own machine without trusting this record on faith.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: V = pi * (d/2)^2 * h

V is the grinding-chamber volume in cubic millimetres; d is the chamber diameter in millimetres; h is the chamber depth in millimetres.

The demo computes the grinding chamber as a cylinder. With diameter 70.0 mm the radius is 35.0 mm, so the chamber volume is pi times radius squared times the chamber depth of 30.0 mm, which the program reports in cubic millimetres and cubic centimetres. The screen area uses the same circle formula with the screen diameter of 62.0 mm. Dividing the tooth count of 56 by the chamber floor area gives teeth per square centimetre, a check that the teeth are spread evenly rather than crowded. Each of the 8 turns in the worked example moves the teeth past each other once, so the total cutting passes are the tooth count times the turns, and the program prints the volume and area so the reader can repeat the arithmetic with a pocket calculator. The formulas are shown in full, the numbers come from the specification in this record, and the demo prints both runs with SHA-256 digests so the reader can confirm the computation is deterministic. The reader can check every step with a pocket calculator, because each input is named, each unit is stated, and no hidden constant is used anywhere in the calculation.

4. Science proof

Shear, not crushing, is the working principle. Opposing diamond teeth pass each other with a small clearance, and the edges cut dried flower along its natural fracture planes. Aluminium 6061 is chosen because it is light, machines cleanly, and its anodised surface resists corrosion from plant resins. The teeth stay sharp because the load is spread across 56 teeth and each tooth meets only a small piece of herb at a time. Ground particles fall through sized holes in the grinding plate by gravity once they are smaller than the hole diameter, which is why a dozen steady turns produces a fairly uniform particle size instead of powder. The 100-micron mesh in a four-piece grinder is a sieve: particles finer than 100 microns, including the small resin glands called trichomes, pass through, while larger ground herb stays above. Friction is low because the herb is dry and brittle, and the magnet lid keeps the halves aligned so the teeth never clash metal on metal. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: manual grinder chamber volume — The Manual Herb Grinder."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    d = 70.0; h = 30.0; teeth = 56; screen_d = 62.0; turns = 8
    r = d / 2.0
    chamber_mm3 = math.pi * r * r * h
    chamber_cm3 = chamber_mm3 / 1000.0
    screen_area_mm2 = math.pi * (screen_d / 2.0) ** 2
    teeth_per_cm2 = teeth / (math.pi * r * r / 100.0)
    return {"chamber_volume_mm3": round(chamber_mm3, 2), "chamber_volume_cm3": round(chamber_cm3, 2), "screen_area_mm2": round(screen_area_mm2, 2), "teeth": teeth, "turns": turns, "teeth_per_cm2": round(teeth_per_cm2, 2)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ["chamber_volume_mm3", "chamber_volume_cm3", "screen_area_mm2", "teeth", "turns", "teeth_per_cm2"]:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
