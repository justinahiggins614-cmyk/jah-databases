1. Description and history

The Signature Ceramic Ashtray is a Signature-made design study for ceramic ashtray, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — ash and spent material fall into the round Signature bowl; rim notches hold a cigarette or joint by gravity; the weight keeps the dish from tipping — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature glazed stoneware with a crackle glaze; fired to cone 5 or 6; food-safe glaze; unglazed base. The design is sized at dish 140 mm round and 37 mm tall; bowl 126 mm across and 30 mm deep; wall 7 mm thick; four rim notches; about 469 grams, which sets every number in this record, from the ceramic ashtray capacity and heat storage in the demo to the scale variants at the end. This record is a design study, not a real manufactured product, and nothing here claims the design has been built, tested, or sold. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, and run the program to check every number. The record also gives the materials and design, a build-it-yourself section, 3D-printing directions, and a use program, so the design can be judged as a whole, not only as a set of numbers. Like every Signature record, it sits beside its public twin in the same pair, so the reader can compare the public form with the Signature reworking side by side.

The ceramic ashtray is as old as the ashtray itself, because pottery was one of the first fire-retardant materials a household could make, and the earliest ashtrays were simple clay dishes. The word ashtray came into use around 1920, and by the start of the twentieth century ceramic ashtrays were already common, from plain stoneware dishes to porcelain trays with floral decoration. Ceramic won the table for the same reasons it won the kitchen: it tolerates heat, it takes glaze in any colour, and a glazed surface wipes clean. Over the twentieth century the form split into round, square, and slab shapes, into earthenware, stoneware, and porcelain bodies, and into hand-built and wheel-thrown making, while the job stayed the same. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage, and the history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: ceramic ashtray needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Materials and design

The ceramic ashtray is a clay-body design, and the choice is between earthenware, stoneware, and porcelain. Stoneware is chosen here because it fires to cone 5 or 6, making a dense, durable body that withstands high temperatures without cracking, while earthenware fires lower and stays more porous, and porcelain is finer and more delicate. The glaze is a fired glass skin, typically a few tenths of a millimetre thick, that seals the clay so resin and smell do not soak in; a clear glaze shows the clay colour, a coloured glaze sets the look, and a matte glaze gives grip. Wall thickness is the design compromise, set here at 7 mm: thinner walls are lighter and fire faster but chip at the rim, thicker walls survive drops better but make the dish heavy and slow to dry in the kiln. The rim notches are carved while the clay is leather-hard, using a dowel the width of a cigarette to press the exact U-shape, and their tolerance is the depth of the cut, deep enough to hold a cigarette without letting it roll. The finish is a fired glaze inside and out, with the base left unglazed so it does not stick to the kiln shelf, and the design varies by shape, by clay body, and by glaze, while the working principle stays the same.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: V = pi * r^2 * h

V is the dish volume in cubic millimetres; r is the dish radius in millimetres; h is the dish height or depth in millimetres.

The demo treats the round ashtray dish as a cylinder and the ceramic shell as the difference between two cylinders. With a dish diameter of 140.0 mm and a height of 37.0 mm, the outer volume is pi times 70 squared times 37, about 569,571 cubic millimetres. The inner bowl is 126.0 mm across and 30.0 mm deep, so its capacity is pi times 63 squared times 30, about 374,069 cubic millimetres, or 374.07 cubic centimetres. The shell is the outer cylinder minus the inner bowl, 195.50 cubic centimetres, and at a stoneware density of 2.4 grams per cubic centimetre the mass is 469.20 grams. Multiplying that mass by the specific heat of 0.85 and a 200 kelvin rise gives about 79,765 joules of stored heat. 4. Science proof

A ceramic ashtray works by heat resistance, mass, and a sealed surface. Fired stoneware has a density near 2.4 grams per cubic centimetre and a specific heat near 0.85 joules per gram per kelvin, so a dish of about 469 grams stores roughly 79,800 joules when heated by 200 kelvin, which is why the dish feels cool for a long time after an ember lands in it. Ceramic is a poor thermal conductor, so heat from an ember stays local instead of racing through the dish to the table, and the fired glaze seals the porous clay body so tar and smell sit on the surface instead of soaking in. The glaze is a thin glass skin, and because it is fired onto the clay it does not peel or fade with washing, which is why a glazed ashtray can be washed in a dishwasher. The bowl is a round cylinder, so its capacity is pi times radius squared times depth, and the rim notches are U-shaped cuts that hold a cigarette by gravity. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

A round Signature ceramic ashtray is a genuine studio build, and this record gives it in full, with one plain limit: it needs a kiln, so it is a pottery-studio build, not a home build. Start with a bag of cone 5 or cone 6 stoneware clay, the kind any pottery studio stocks, and wedge it until it is free of air pockets. On a wheel, centre about 600 grams of clay and pull a round dish 140 mm across and 37 mm tall, leaving a 7 mm wall and a 30 mm deep bowl, then shape the rim level with a wooden rib. When the clay is leather-hard, mark four rest points on the rim at equal spacing, and press a dowel the width of a cigarette into each mark to form the U-shaped notch, then round every edge with a damp sponge, because sharp ceramic edges are dangerous after firing. Let the piece dry slowly under plastic until it is bone dry, which takes two or three days, then bisque fire it in the studio kiln to cone 04. Glaze the inside and outside with a food-safe crackle glaze, leaving the base bare, and glaze fire it to cone 5 or 6, the maturity temperature of the clay. Let the kiln cool completely before opening it, then check the notches with a cigarette and the capacity against the demo numbers before using it.

3D-print version

The ceramic ashtray itself is not 3D-printable in ceramic on a home printer, and this record says so plainly: home printers extrude plastic filament, and no plastic survives contact with hot embers, so a printed ashtray would melt and give off fumes. What is printable is the accessory that goes with it: a PETG base ring and rest pad that fits the 140 mm round dish and keeps it from sliding or scratching the table. Print it in PETG, which tolerates warm water and cleaning better than PLA, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, a 0.2 mm layer height, 20 percent gyroid infill, and 3 perimeters, giving a wall about 1.2 mm thick. Print the ring flat on its base with no supports, at a nozzle temperature of 235 degrees C and a bed temperature of 80 degrees C, with the fan at 50 percent. Print time (estimate): about 1 hour 5 minutes for the ring at standard size. Post-processing is simple: remove any brim, check the 140 mm inner opening with calipers, and sand the rim lightly if it is sharp. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing all change the real print time and strength, so the estimate is not a validated print job; measure the printed ring before trusting it with a real ceramic dish.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: ceramic ashtray capacity and heat storage — Signature Ceramic Ashtray."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    d = 140.0; h = 37.0; depth = 30.0; wall = 7.0; rests = 4; rho = 2.4; cp = 0.85; dT = 200.0
    outer_mm3 = math.pi * (d / 2.0) ** 2 * h
    cap_mm3 = math.pi * ((d - 2.0 * wall) / 2.0) ** 2 * depth
    cap_cm3 = cap_mm3 / 1000.0
    shell_mm3 = outer_mm3 - cap_mm3
    shell_cm3 = shell_mm3 / 1000.0
    mass_g = shell_cm3 * rho
    heat_J = mass_g * cp * dT
    return {"outer_volume_mm3": round(outer_mm3, 2), "capacity_mm3": round(cap_mm3, 2), "capacity_cm3": round(cap_cm3, 2), "shell_volume_cm3": round(shell_cm3, 2), "ceramic_mass_g": round(mass_g, 2), "heat_to_200C_J": round(heat_J, 2), "rim_notches": rests}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ['outer_volume_mm3', 'capacity_mm3', 'capacity_cm3', 'shell_volume_cm3', 'ceramic_mass_g', 'heat_to_200C_J', 'rim_notches']:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Use, as a program

To use the ceramic ashtray, set it on a flat surface, rest the cigarette or joint in a rim notch between draws, and tap ash into the bowl rather than onto the table. Empty it when the ash reaches a third of the depth, while the embers are out, and wash it with soap and water or in a dishwasher, because the glazed surface releases tar easily. Do not quench a hot ashtray in cold water, and do not drop it on a hard floor, because ceramic chips and cracks even though it tolerates heat. The program in this record computes what the dish holds and how it handles heat: the bowl capacity from its length, width, and depth, the ceramic shell volume from the outer and inner boxes, the mass from the stoneware density, and the stored heat from the mass, the specific heat, and a 200 kelvin rise. Run the demo to see the numbers, then change one input at a time — a wider dish, a deeper bowl, a thicker wall — and watch the capacity and the mass move. The program is the use check: if the mass it reports is far below 470 grams for a dish this size, the wall is thinner than the record states, and the reader should re-measure the dish before trusting it.
