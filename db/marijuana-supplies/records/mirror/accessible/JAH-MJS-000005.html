1. Description and history

The Signature Comet Spoon Pipe is a Signature-made design study for glass spoon pipe, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — herb is packed in a bowl at one end, lit while the user draws through a stem to a mouthpiece, and a carb hole on the side is covered to fill the pipe with smoke and uncovered to clear it — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature borosilicate glass; double-walled stem; glass bowl. The design is sized at 110 mm long; bowl 22 mm across and 20 mm deep; double-walled stem 80 mm long, which sets every number in this record, from the volumes and areas in the demo to the scale variants at the end. This record is a design study, not a real manufactured product, and nothing here claims the design has been built, tested, or sold. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, run the program, and see exactly where each number comes from. The Signature edition exists alongside the public edition so the archive holds both what exists in the world and what the Signature system designs next, clearly labelled, so the two are never confused. Like every Signature record, it is filed as a design study, with its own generator and its own file AI, and it marches with the rest of the database toward a million records.

The glass spoon is a recent invention in pipe history: sources place its arrival in the mid-to-late 1970s, when glass workers in the United States combined elements of tobacco pipes and older cannabis pipes into a small hand pipe of borosilicate glass. It became the most widely used Western cannabis pipe because it was simple, pocket-sized, and ran cooler than a chillum while taking up far less space than a bong. Borosilicate glass was the material from the start, chosen for heat resistance and a clean flavour, and the form has since spawned sherlocks, hammers, and sidecars. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. Museums, trade guides, and manufacturer manuals preserve the steps of that change, and the sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage. The history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: glass spoon pipe needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Method

The method in this record is to describe the supply from its parts, its dimensions, and its mechanism, and then to check those claims with arithmetic and a runnable program. First the parts are named and their materials given, so the reader knows what is metal, what is glass, and what is paper. Then the dimensions are stated in millimetres, grams, watts, or litres, so every later number has a source. Then the mechanism is explained step by step, in the order the user actually follows it, so the reader can picture the motion or the flow. 3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: V = pi * r^2 * h

V is the volume of a cylindrical part in cubic millimetres; r is its radius in millimetres; h is its depth or length in millimetres.

The demo treats the bowl and the stem as cylinders. The bowl volume is pi times the bowl radius squared times the bowl depth: with a bowl diameter of 22.0 mm and a depth of 20.0 mm, the radius is 11.0 mm. The stem volume is pi times the stem radius squared times the stem length: a bore of 7.0 mm over 80.0 mm. Adding the two volumes gives the total smoke-path volume, reported in cubic millimetres and cubic centimetres. Every number comes from the specification in this record, the formulas are shown in full, and the program prints each volume so the reader can check the arithmetic. The computation runs twice with SHA-256 digests printed, so the reader can confirm it is deterministic. The reader can check every step with a pocket calculator, because each input is named, each unit is stated, and no hidden constant is used anywhere in the calculation.

4. Science proof

A spoon pipe is a direct-flow combustion pipe with a manual air valve, the carb. Lighting the herb starts combustion, and the draw creates a pressure drop along the stem: air at atmospheric pressure enters through the bowl, passes over the burning herb, and the hot gases travel down the stem to the mouth. Covering the carb forces all incoming air through the bowl, which keeps the herb burning and fills the stem and bowl chamber with smoke. Releasing the carb opens a low-resistance path for fresh air, which sweeps the stored smoke into the mouth in one clear. Borosilicate glass matters because it has a low coefficient of thermal expansion, so it tolerates the hot bowl end and the cool mouthpiece end without cracking, and because glass is chemically inert, it adds no flavour of its own. The bowl is a shallow cylinder, so its volume is pi times radius squared times depth, and the stem is a long narrow cylinder whose volume sets how much smoke the pipe holds between the bowl and the mouth. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: glass spoon pipe volumes — The Glass Spoon Pipe."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    length = 110.0; bowl_d = 22.0; bowl_h = 20.0; stem_d = 7.0; stem_l = 80.0
    bowl_mm3 = math.pi * (bowl_d / 2.0) ** 2 * bowl_h
    stem_mm3 = math.pi * (stem_d / 2.0) ** 2 * stem_l
    total_mm3 = bowl_mm3 + stem_mm3
    return {"bowl_volume_mm3": round(bowl_mm3, 2), "bowl_volume_cm3": round(bowl_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "stem_volume_mm3": round(stem_mm3, 2), "stem_volume_cm3": round(stem_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "total_volume_mm3": round(total_mm3, 2), "total_volume_cm3": round(total_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "pipe_length_mm": length}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ["bowl_volume_mm3", "bowl_volume_cm3", "stem_volume_mm3", "stem_volume_cm3", "total_volume_mm3", "total_volume_cm3", "pipe_length_mm"]:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
