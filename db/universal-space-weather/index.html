The Solar Wind JAH-FSW-000001 · simple

The solar wind is a stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun at 300-800 kilometres per second, carrying the Sun's magnetic field with it.

The solar wind is a stream of charged particles flowing outward from the Sun at 300-800 kilometres per second, carrying the Sun's magnetic field with it. It takes about two to four days to reach Earth, where it presses on the magnetosphere and feeds the aurora. The Parker Solar Probe, launched in 2018, flies through the solar wind near the Sun and measures it directly.

Coronal Mass Ejections JAH-FSW-000002 · intermediate

A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a billion-tonne cloud of plasma thrown off the Sun, carrying its own magnetic field.

A coronal mass ejection (CME) is a billion-tonne cloud of plasma thrown off the Sun, carrying its own magnetic field. If it hits Earth, it can trigger a geomagnetic storm: auroras, satellite drag, GPS errors and power-grid currents. Fast CMEs cross the Sun-Earth distance in under a day. CMEs are tracked by coronagraphs such as SOHO and STEREO, which watch the Sun's outer atmosphere.

Solar Flares JAH-FSW-000003 · intermediate

A solar flare is a sudden brightening of the Sun's surface caused by magnetic reconnection, releasing up to 10^25 joules in minutes.

A solar flare is a sudden brightening of the Sun's surface caused by magnetic reconnection, releasing up to 10^25 joules in minutes. Flares are classified by peak soft X-ray flux: A, B, C, M and X, each ten times the last. X-class flares can black out high-frequency radio for hours and, when accompanied by a CME, drive strong storms. The largest recorded flare was the X28 event of November 2003.

The Kp Index JAH-FSW-000005 · simple

The Kp index measures global geomagnetic activity on a scale of 0 to 9, derived from magnetometers around the world every three hours.

The Kp index measures global geomagnetic activity on a scale of 0 to 9, derived from magnetometers around the world every three hours. Kp 5 and above means a geomagnetic storm; Kp 9 is extreme. It is the headline number of space weather, used by power grid operators, satellite operators and aurora watchers alike. Kp is a measure of what is happening, not a forecast of what will.

The Dst Index JAH-FSW-000006 · advanced

The Dst index measures the strength of the ring current around Earth, in nanoteslas, from four low-latitude magnetometers.

The Dst index measures the strength of the ring current around Earth, in nanoteslas, from four low-latitude magnetometers. Negative Dst means a storm is under way: the March 1989 storm reached about -589 nT, and the Carrington Event of 1859 is estimated near -850 nT. Dst is the standard measure of storm intensity used in research, complementing the operational Kp index.

The Van Allen Belts JAH-FSW-000007 · intermediate

The Van Allen radiation belts are two doughnut-shaped regions of trapped charged particles held by Earth's magnetic field, discovered by Explorer 1 in 1958.

The Van Allen radiation belts are two doughnut-shaped regions of trapped charged particles held by Earth's magnetic field, discovered by Explorer 1 in 1958. The inner belt holds energetic protons; the outer belt holds electrons whose population swells during storms. Satellites crossing them need shielding, and astronauts pass through them quickly on the way to the Moon. Their dynamics are driven by the solar wind.

The ionosphere is the electrically charged upper atmosphere, from about 60 to 1,000 kilometres up, where solar radiation strips electrons from atoms. It reflects high-frequency radio, which is why shortwave broadcasts travel worldwide, and it delays GPS signals, which is why navigation must correct for it. The Solar Cycle JAH-FSW-000010 · intermediate

The solar cycle lasts about 11 years, from minimum — few sunspots, quiet Sun — to maximum, when sunspots, flares and CMEs are frequent.

The solar cycle lasts about 11 years, from minimum — few sunspots, quiet Sun — to maximum, when sunspots, flares and CMEs are frequent. Solar Cycle 25 began in December 2019 and peaked around 2024-2025, stronger than predicted. The cycle is driven by the Sun's magnetic dynamo, and its length and strength vary; the Maunder Minimum of 1645-1715 saw almost no sunspots for seventy years.

Satellite Drag JAH-FSW-000012 · intermediate

Satellite drag is the friction of the upper atmosphere on low-orbit satellites, and it rises sharply during geomagnetic storms because the atmosphere heats and expands.

Satellite drag is the friction of the upper atmosphere on low-orbit satellites, and it rises sharply during geomagnetic storms because the atmosphere heats and expands. In February 2022 a geomagnetic storm destroyed 38 of 49 newly launched Starlink satellites before they could raise their orbits. GPS Disruption JAH-FSW-000013 · intermediate

GPS signals pass through the ionosphere, which delays them by an amount that changes with solar activity; receivers correct for it using models and dual frequencies.

GPS signals pass through the ionosphere, which delays them by an amount that changes with solar activity; receivers correct for it using models and dual frequencies. During strong storms the delay can vary rapidly, degrading accuracy from metres to tens of metres, and scintillation — rapid flickering of the signal — can cause loss of lock entirely. Aviation, farming and surveying all depend on GPS accuracy, so storm warnings go directly to them.

Astronauts beyond Earth's magnetic shielding face galactic cosmic rays and solar particle events. Space weather forecasting predicts storms from solar observations: sunspot groups, flare history, CME speed and direction, and solar wind measurements from the L1 point, which give 30-60 minutes of warning. The Magnetosphere JAH-FSW-000022 · intermediate

The magnetosphere is the magnetic bubble that shields Earth from the solar wind, carved into a comet shape with a long tail stretching away from the Sun.

The magnetosphere is the magnetic bubble that shields Earth from the solar wind, carved into a comet shape with a long tail stretching away from the Sun. It deflects most charged particles, traps some in the radiation belts, and funnels others into the auroral zones. During storms it compresses and reconnects, releasing energy that powers auroras and drives currents. Without it, Earth would lose its atmosphere as Mars did.
