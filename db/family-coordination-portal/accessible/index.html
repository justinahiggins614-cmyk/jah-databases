  1. A Week of School Runs

    Record 1 of 24. simple. Feasibility: buildable-now. The design uses only web pages, forms, and counters that exist today; only the design work stands in the way.

    The family coordination portal helps a household plan its week together, in plain words, with who does what, when, and where written out for all to see. Every plan shown is a clearly labeled illustrative sample of a generic kind of family plan, never a real plan of a real family. A week of school runs gives a household a plain plan for getting children to school and back, with who drives each day, the times, and the route written out for all to see. The portal must show the order of the runs, so no child is left at the stop and no driver is asked to be in two places at once. Without those plain facts, a family must guess at the plan each morning, and guessing at the plan on a school morning is a poor way to start the day. Worked example: in a website built this way, a week of school runs runs as a small, complete feature a visitor can try in one sitting, with the fair-play and moderation rules of this database applied throughout.

    History: Planning a family week is an old habit: a note on the fridge, a list by the phone, a word at the breakfast table. The website form grows out of that habit, adding a joined plan with order, times, and who does what. Weeks of school runs are an old habit, from a note on the fridge to a list by the phone that said who drove each day.

    Proposed solution: Name the family plan and what a visitor does in it; Write the rules in plain sentences a new visitor can follow; Give every family plan the same fair start: the same clues, the same time, the same scoring; Let a visitor try one family plan without signing in; Check the family plan works the same way on a phone and on a computer.

    Dependencies: What must exist first: a list of generic kinds of family plans, a way to join them into one week plan, the order, the times, and who does what written out. Nothing else is needed to open the first family plan.

Without an index, the simple records that teach the design are buried beside the complex records that assume it, and a visitor who wants one idea must read the whole database to find it. The index solves this by listing every record in ladder order, with its place in the ladder, so a reader can start at the beginning and climb, or jump straight to the record they need. Worked example: a reader who wants the simplest starting point opens record 1, A Week of School Runs, and a reader who wants the hardest problem opens record 23, A Yearly Showcase of Family Plans.

    History: Planning a family week is an old habit: a note on the fridge, a list by the phone, a word at the breakfast table. The website form grows out of that habit, adding a joined plan with order, times, and who does what. Field guides and indexes are as old as books themselves: a table of contents that tells a reader where everything lives. This index follows that habit, listing the twenty-three content records of this database in the order a reader should meet them.

    Proposed solution: Add a tournament bracket with rounds, divisions, and a season; Add a creator studio so visitors can build a new family plan; Add a review step so every new family plan is checked before it is published; Add the family plan in many languages, with the same rules in each; Add an offline pack so the family plan works with no connection.

    Dependencies: What must exist first: a list of generic kinds of family plans, a way to join them into one week plan, the order, the times, and who does what written out. A tournament system, a creator studio, a review step, translations, and an offline pack are also needed before the complex family plans open.

    Verification path: A tournament runs a full season, a creator publishes a new family plan through the review step, and the offline pack is tried with no connection. The demo program prints the same total as the math proof, and every step is checked by a second reader.

    Science proof: total = count x each. In plain words, this equation turns counted quantities into one checkable number: with 23 items at 1 each, the arithmetic gives 23 records, and the demo prints that same total, so the claim can be checked by arithmetic alone.

    Math proof: Worked arithmetic: 23 x 1 = 23. The demo sets count to 23 and each to 1, multiplies them, and prints Total: 23, so a fresh run of the demo verifies this proof exactly.
