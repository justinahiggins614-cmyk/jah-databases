What Mathematics Is

ID: JAH-MTHM-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Mathematics is the study of number, shape, pattern, and change, pursued by exact reasoning. It asks questions such as how many, how big, how likely, and how does it change, and answers them with definitions, logical argument, and calculation. A mathematical claim is not a guess or an opinion: it follows from stated starting points by steps that anyone can check. Mathematics includes arithmetic and algebra, geometry, calculus, probability, and logic, and it supplies the language in which the sciences describe the world.

History: Mathematics begins with counting. Tally marks cut into bone date back tens of thousands of years, and the Ishango bone, about 20,000 years old, carries grouped notches that suggest early counting. Babylonian scribes, around 1800 BCE, worked in base 60 and solved quadratic problems on clay tablets, while Egyptian surveyors used geometry to re-measure fields after the Nile flooded. Greek thinkers from about 600 BCE onwards changed the subject: Thales and Pythagoras argued by proof rather than by example, and Euclid, around 300 BCE, organised geometry into the Elements, a chain of theorems from a few axioms. Indian mathematicians developed the decimal place-value system and the number zero, and the Persian scholar al-Khwarizmi, around 825 CE, wrote the book that gave algebra its name. In the 1600s Newton and Leibniz invented calculus, and in the 1800s and 1900s mathematics grew into abstract structures, set theory, and the mathematics of computers. In the 1800s, mathematics changed again: Gauss, Bolyai, and Lobachevsky built geometries that denied Euclid's parallel postulate, and Cantor built set theory, showing that some infinities are larger than others. David Hilbert, in 1900, set out 23 problems that shaped the century, and the 1900s added computers, which turned mathematics into a tool for calculation at a scale no human could match. Women mathematicians made central contributions throughout: Emmy Noether reshaped algebra and physics in the 1910s and 1920s, and Maryam Mirzakhani won the Fields Medal, first awarded in 1936, in 2014. The story is still being written, with new results proved every year.

Key concepts: Number is the basic object: counts, measures, and labels. Operation is what you do to numbers: add, subtract, multiply, divide, and more. Pattern is a regularity that repeats and can be described by a rule. Proof is a chain of reasoning that shows a statement must be true. Abstraction is the move from particular things, such as three apples, to general ideas, such as the number three itself. Structure is the system of relations between objects, for example how addition and multiplication interact. Model is a mathematical description of a real situation, simplified enough to work with and faithful enough to be useful. A theorem is a statement that has been proved, and a conjecture is a statement believed true but not yet proved. An axiom is a starting assumption accepted without proof, such as Euclid's parallel postulate. An algorithm is a precise procedure for solving a problem, step by step. Symmetry is a change that leaves an object looking the same, such as rotating a square by a quarter turn. A function is a rule that assigns exactly one output to each input, and it is the way mathematics describes dependence between quantities.

How it works: Mathematics works by starting from clear definitions and a small set of agreed starting points, called axioms, and then deriving consequences by logic. Each step must follow from earlier steps, so the whole argument can be checked line by line. A new result, called a theorem, is only accepted when its proof is complete. Alongside proof, mathematicians compute: they test ideas on examples, look for patterns, and form conjectures, which are educated guesses waiting for proof. A conjecture supported by a million examples is still not a theorem until it is proved. The same method scales from a school problem, such as solving 2x + 6 = 14, to research mathematics, where a proof may run to hundreds of pages and be checked by other mathematicians before it is accepted. Alongside proof and computation, mathematicians use examples and counterexamples: a single counterexample disproves a claim, while many examples suggest a conjecture. They also work together: most modern papers have several authors, and results are checked by other mathematicians, called referees, before publication. A proof is written to be read, not just to be correct: a good proof explains why a result is true, not merely that it is. When a proof is long, it is broken into lemmas, small results proved on the way to the main theorem, so that each step can be checked separately.

Worked examples: A 3-4-5 triangle checks the Pythagorean theorem: 3 squared is 9, 4 squared is 16, and 9 + 16 = 25, which is 5 squared, so the triangle has a right angle. Adding the numbers 1 to 10 gives 55, which can be found by pairing 1 with 10, 2 with 9, and so on: five pairs of 11. The odd numbers add up to squares: 1 = 1, 1 + 3 = 4, 1 + 3 + 5 = 9, and 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16. A rectangle 6 units long and 4 units wide has area 6 times 4, which is 24 square units, and a perimeter of 6 + 4 + 6 + 4, which is 20 units. The area of a circle of radius 5 is pi times 25, about 78.54, and its circumference is 2 times pi times 5, about 31.42. The average of 4, 7, and 10 is their sum, 21, divided by 3, which is 7. The angles of a triangle add to 180 degrees: 90 + 60 + 30 is 180, and so is 50 + 60 + 70.

Real applications: Engineers use mathematics to design bridges that carry their loads, and to test them in calculation before they are built. Doctors use it to work out drug doses from a patient's weight. Weather forecasts run on mathematical models of the atmosphere, solved by computers. Banks use it to price loans and manage risk, shops use it to set prices and track stock, and phones use it to compress photos, correct errors in signals, and encrypt messages. Navigation, music recording, medical imaging, and space flight all rest on mathematics that was often developed long before its application was known. Cryptography, the mathematics that keeps online messages private, depends on number theory, a subject once thought useless. Satellite navigation uses the mathematics of relativity and of geometry to work out positions from timing signals. Medical imaging turns measurements into pictures by solving mathematical problems, and the search engines that organise the web use the mathematics of matrices and graphs. Even music recording depends on mathematics: sound is stored as numbers, compressed by mathematical methods, and played back by reversing them.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is mathematics invented or discovered? Mathematicians disagree, and the question is philosophical: the patterns seem to be found in the world, while the symbols and systems used to describe them are human inventions. Is mathematics only about numbers? No: it also studies shapes, symmetry, chance, change, and logical structure, and much of modern mathematics uses few numbers at all. Do you need to be fast at arithmetic to be good at mathematics? Speed helps, but mathematics is mainly about understanding, reasoning, and seeing patterns; a slow careful thinker can be an excellent mathematician. Is there one right way to solve a problem? Usually several: a good solution is one that is correct and clear, not necessarily the one in the textbook. Is mathematics a science? It is like a science in that it is tested and corrected, but unlike the experimental sciences in that its results are proved rather than observed, so it is usually classed as its own kind of knowledge. Why do proofs matter if a result is used anyway? Because a proof shows exactly what a result depends on, and an unproved result can turn out to be false, with costly consequences if it has been built into a bridge or a program. Can mathematics be beautiful? Mathematicians often say so, and a beautiful proof, short and surprising, is valued as a sign of a deep truth.

Numbers and Counting

ID: JAH-MTHM-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A number is an idea used to answer how many or how much. Counting is the act of matching objects, one by one, to the number words in order: one, two, three, and so on. The natural numbers, 1, 2, 3, and continuing without end, are the numbers used for counting. Numbers also measure, as when a length is 5 metres, order, as in first, second, third, and label, as in a bus number. The decimal system writes every number with the ten digits 0 to 9, and the position of a digit tells its value: in 347, the 3 means three hundreds, the 4 means four tens, and the 7 means seven ones.

History: People counted long before they wrote. Tally marks, notches on bone and wood, are among the oldest records, and finger counting is still used everywhere. The Babylonians, around 1800 BCE, used base 60, which is why an hour has 60 minutes and a circle has 360 degrees. The Egyptians and Romans used symbols that were added together, so writing large numbers was clumsy. The great change was the Hindu-Arabic system, developed in India: ten digits, place value, and zero as a number in its own right, described by Brahmagupta in the 7th century CE. It reached Europe through Arabic scholars and through Fibonacci, whose book Liber Abaci of 1202 taught merchants the new numerals. Negative numbers were used in China and India centuries before Europe accepted them, and the number line, with zero in the middle, became the standard picture of numbers in the 1600s. Roman numerals, used across Europe for a thousand years, made arithmetic hard: adding XXVII and XIV required converting to another system or using a counting board. The abacus, a frame of beads, was the standard calculating tool in China, Rome, and the Middle East, and it is still used in some places today. In the 1600s, John Napier invented logarithms and a calculating device of rods, and in the 1800s Giuseppe Peano set out axioms for the natural numbers, defining them by 1 and the successor operation. In the 1900s, computers adopted binary, a place-value system with only the digits 0 and 1, showing that the idea of place value works in any base, not just ten.

Key concepts: Natural numbers are the counting numbers 1, 2, 3, and so on; some conventions include 0, so this record says so when it matters. Place value means a digit's worth depends on its position: 5 means five in 5, fifty in 50, and five hundred in 500. Zero is both a placeholder and a number, the answer to 5 minus 5. One-to-one correspondence is matching each object to exactly one number word, which is what makes a count correct. Cardinality is how many a set has; ordinality is position in an order. A numeral is the written symbol for a number, such as 7, VII, or seven, while the number itself is the idea behind the symbol. The successor of a number is the next one: the successor of 7 is 8, and every natural number has one. The predecessor is the one before: the predecessor of 8 is 7, and every natural number except 1 has one. Whole numbers are the natural numbers together with 0. Ordinal numbers say position, first, second, third, while cardinal numbers say how many. A counting system with a different base works the same way: in binary, 101 means 1 times 4 plus 0 times 2 plus 1, which is 5.

How it works: Counting works by order and by matching. Say the number words in their fixed order and touch each object once, and the last word said is the total. Place value works by grouping: ten ones make one ten, ten tens make one hundred, and so on, so any number can be built from the digits 0 to 9. To compare two numbers, compare their lengths first: a three-digit number is bigger than a two-digit number. If they have the same length, compare the leftmost digits where they differ. To count a large group, group it into tens and then into hundreds, which is the same idea as place value applied to real objects. Estimation works the same way: round to the nearest ten or hundred and count the groups. Grouping makes counting fast and reliable. To count a large pile, make groups of ten, then groups of ten groups, and count the groups: 3 hundreds, 4 tens, and 7 ones is 347. To compare two counts, compare the largest groups first: more hundreds means a bigger number, whatever the smaller groups hold. To estimate a count, count a small part and scale up: if one row of seats holds 12 and there are 20 rows, the hall holds about 240. To check a count, count again in a different grouping: counting by twos and then by fives should give the same total.

Worked examples: Counting 48 apples: make groups of ten, giving four full groups and 8 left over, so there are 48 apples. The number 347 expands to 3 times 100 plus 4 times 10 plus 7, which is 300 + 40 + 7. Adding the numbers from 1 to 100 gives 5,050: pair 1 with 100, 2 with 99, and so on to make fifty pairs of 101, and 50 times 101 is 5,050. On a number line, 7 sits two steps to the right of 5, and minus 3 sits three steps to the left of zero. The number 2,000 is bigger than 987 because it has four digits while 987 has three. The binary number 101 is 5, since 1 times 4 plus 0 times 2 plus 1 is 5. A hall with 20 rows of 12 seats holds about 240 seats, since 20 times 12 is 240. Counting 1,000 dots by grouping them in tens gives 100 groups, and grouping those in tens gives 10 groups of 100.

Real applications: Counting is the foundation of nearly all measurement and record-keeping. A census counts people, a shop counts stock, a bank counts money, and a scoreboard counts points. Place value makes written arithmetic possible: without it, multiplying 347 by 28 would be a major undertaking, as it was for the Romans. Science counts atoms, cells, and stars; medicine counts doses and heartbeats; transport counts passengers and timetables. Every digital device stores numbers in binary, a place-value system with only the digits 0 and 1, which is the same idea with a different base. Barcodes and product codes use counting with a check digit: the last digit is computed from the others, so a mistyped digit is caught. The ISBN of a book has a check digit computed from the other digits, which catches most errors in copying. In computing, counting in binary is how a machine counts memory, files, and steps. In science, counting is how samples are measured: the number of cells in a sample, or the number of stars in a survey, is a count, and its accuracy depends on the counting method.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is zero a natural number? It depends on convention: many mathematicians include 0 in the natural numbers, while school counting usually starts at 1. Either is fine as long as it is stated. Why do we count in tens? Almost certainly because we have ten fingers; other bases have been used, such as base 60 in Babylon and base 2 in computers. Is infinity a number? No: infinity is an idea about endlessness, not a counting number, and it does not behave like one, since infinity plus one is still infinity. Can you count everything? Only things that come in separate items; continuous things such as water or time are measured, not counted, though we count the units of measurement. Why do different cultures count differently? Because counting grew from local needs and materials: fingers gave base ten, while the Babylonians used base sixty, perhaps from astronomy, and the Maya used base twenty, perhaps from fingers and toes. Is counting the same as measuring? No: counting tallies separate items, while measuring compares a continuous quantity with a unit, though the result of a measurement is often a count of units. Can a computer count? Yes, in binary, and it counts far faster than a person, but it follows the same idea: matching items to numbers in order.

Arithmetic and the Four Operations

ID: JAH-MTHM-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: Arithmetic is the branch of mathematics that works with numbers and the four basic operations: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. Addition joins amounts together, subtraction finds a difference or what remains, multiplication adds the same amount repeatedly, and division shares an amount equally or counts how many groups fit. These four operations are inverses in pairs: subtraction undoes addition, and division undoes multiplication. Nearly all everyday mathematics is arithmetic, and all higher mathematics is built on it.

History: Arithmetic is older than writing: tally sticks and knotted cords recorded counts and trades. Babylonian tablets from around 1800 BCE carry multiplication tables, and Egyptian scribes multiplied by doubling, a method still used inside computers. The abacus, used in China, Rome, and elsewhere, made fast calculation possible centuries before paper arithmetic. Indian mathematicians developed the column methods for addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division that are still taught today, and al-Khwarizmi's books of about 825 CE spread them through the Arabic-speaking world and into Europe. Fibonacci's Liber Abaci of 1202 taught European merchants the new methods, and printed arithmetic books of the 1500s standardised them. Mechanical calculators appeared in the 1600s, electronic calculators in the 1970s, but the operations themselves have not changed. In the 1600s, John Napier's bones, rods marked with multiplication tables, made multiplication faster, and the slide rule, built on logarithms, was the engineer's calculator for three centuries. Charles Babbage designed a mechanical calculating engine in the 1830s, and the first electronic calculators arrived in the 1970s, putting the four operations in a pocket. Computers now do arithmetic at billions of operations a second, but they do the same four operations, and the methods they use, such as adding in binary with carries, are the column methods adapted to base two. The operations have not changed; only the speed and the notation have.

Key concepts: Addition combines: 4 + 3 = 7, and the order does not matter, since 3 + 4 = 7 as well. Subtraction is the inverse of addition: 7 - 3 = 4 because 4 + 3 = 7. Multiplication is repeated addition: 4 times 3 means 3 + 3 + 3 + 3, and like addition it does not depend on order. Division is the inverse of multiplication: 12 divided by 3 is 4 because 4 times 3 is 12. The identity elements are 0 for addition, since adding 0 changes nothing, and 1 for multiplication, since multiplying by 1 changes nothing. A remainder is what is left when division does not come out even: 17 divided by 5 is 3 remainder 2. The sum is the result of addition, the difference is the result of subtraction, the product is the result of multiplication, and the quotient is the result of division. A fact family is a set of related facts: 3 + 4 = 7, 4 + 3 = 7, 7 - 3 = 4, and 7 - 4 = 3 belong to one family, and 3 times 4 = 12, 4 times 3 = 12, 12 divided by 3 = 4, and 12 divided by 4 = 3 belong to another. The minuend is the number subtracted from, and the subtrahend is the number subtracted. The dividend is the number divided, and the divisor is the number it is divided by.

How it works: Column addition works right to left: add the ones, carry any ten into the tens column, then add the tens, and so on. Subtraction works the same way, borrowing a ten when a digit is too small. Multiplication breaks a problem into parts: 6 times 47 is 6 times 40 plus 6 times 7, which is 240 + 42 = 282. Division asks how many times the divisor fits: 84 divided by 7 is 12, because 7 times 12 is 84. Every operation can be checked by its inverse: check a subtraction by adding, and check a division by multiplying. Estimation keeps arithmetic honest: before computing 47 times 38 exactly, note that 50 times 40 is 2,000, so the answer should be near 1,800. Checking is part of arithmetic. An addition is checked by subtracting one addend from the sum, a subtraction by adding the difference to the subtrahend, a multiplication by dividing the product by one factor, and a division by multiplying the quotient by the divisor and adding the remainder. Estimation is the first check: before computing 47 times 38 exactly, note that 50 times 40 is 2,000, so the answer should be near 1,800, and an answer of 18,000 would be plainly wrong. A second check is to compute in a different order: 47 times 38 is the same as 38 times 47, so computing both ways and comparing catches errors.

Worked examples: Addition: 47 + 38 = 85, checked by 85 - 38 = 47. Subtraction: 52 - 27 = 25, checked by 25 + 27 = 52. Multiplication: 6 times 7 is 42, and 6 times 47 is 282, since 6 times 40 is 240 and 6 times 7 is 42. Division: 84 divided by 7 is 12, because 12 times 7 is 84; 17 divided by 5 is 3 remainder 2, because 3 times 5 is 15 and 17 - 15 is 2. A difference of squares: 9 squared minus 4 squared is 81 - 16 = 65, and (9 + 4) times (9 - 4) is 13 times 5, which is also 65. A fact family: 3 + 4 = 7, 4 + 3 = 7, 7 - 3 = 4, and 7 - 4 = 3. Another: 3 times 4 = 12, 4 times 3 = 12, 12 divided by 3 = 4, and 12 divided by 4 = 3. Estimating 47 times 38: 50 times 40 is 2,000, so the exact answer, 1,786, is plausible, while 17,860 would not be.

Real applications: Arithmetic runs daily life. Shopping uses addition at the till, subtraction for change, multiplication for buying several of one item, and division for sharing a bill. Cooking scales recipes up and down. Builders measure and cut, drivers work out fuel and time, and nurses calculate doses. Business runs on arithmetic: profit is income minus costs, and a discount of 20 percent on 80 is 16 off. Computers do arithmetic at enormous speed, but the operations are the same four, and a person who understands them can check a machine's answer and spot when it is wrong. In sport, arithmetic gives scores, averages, and league tables: a batsman's average is runs divided by times out, and a team's points are wins times three plus draws. In science, it combines measurements and counts, and it is the first step of every calculation. In computing, the processor's arithmetic unit does the four operations on binary numbers, and every program depends on it. In daily life, it is the mathematics of splitting a bill, of working out a discount, and of checking that a receipt is right.

Common questions and misconceptions: Why does multiplication come before addition in a calculation? Because of convention, agreed so that everyone reads 2 + 3 times 4 as 2 + 12 = 14, not 20; brackets can override it. Can you divide by zero? No: there is no number that multiplied by 0 gives a non-zero answer, and 0 divided by 0 could be anything, so division by zero is left undefined. Is subtraction just addition in disguise? In a sense, yes: subtracting 3 is the same as adding minus 3, which is why algebra treats them together. With calculators everywhere, why learn mental arithmetic? Because estimation, checking, and quick judgement need arithmetic in your head, and a calculator cannot tell you whether you typed the right problem. Which operation should be done first? By convention, multiplication and division before addition and subtraction, and brackets first of all, which is the order of operations. Why are there four operations and not more? Because addition and multiplication, with their inverses subtraction and division, cover the basic ways of combining numbers, and all other operations are built from them. Is long division the only way to divide? No: short division, repeated subtraction, and estimation are all division, and the best method depends on the size of the numbers.

What a Proof Is

ID: JAH-MTHM-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record

Definition: A proof is a logical argument that shows a mathematical statement must be true. It starts from definitions and from a small set of agreed starting points, called axioms, and moves forward in steps, each one justified by logic or by an earlier step, until the statement is reached. A proof is not a collection of examples, however many: examples can suggest that a statement is true, but only a proof establishes it. When a statement has been proved it is called a theorem, and other proofs may then use it as a stepping stone.

History: Proof begins with the Greeks. Thales, around 600 BCE, is credited with the first proofs in geometry, and the Pythagoreans proved results about numbers and triangles. Euclid's Elements, written around 300 BCE, set the pattern for two thousand years: definitions, axioms, and theorems proved in order, each resting on what came before. Proof spread to algebra and analysis, and in the 1800s mathematicians made proofs more rigorous, closing gaps that had been overlooked. In 1976 the four-colour theorem was proved with essential help from a computer, which checked hundreds of cases, and mathematicians debated whether that counted as a proof; it is now accepted. Today, proof assistants are computer programs that check every step of a proof mechanically, and some long proofs are verified this way. In the 1800s, mathematicians made proofs more rigorous: Cauchy and Weierstrass rebuilt calculus on the definition of the limit, closing gaps that had been overlooked for a century. Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica, published 1910 to 1913, tried to derive all of mathematics from logic, and Kurt Godel showed in 1931 that no single system could prove all of arithmetic. In 2014, the Kepler conjecture, about the densest way to pack spheres, was settled by a computer-checked proof, after a decade of verification, and the proof was checked by the HOL Light and Isabelle systems. The computer has changed the practice of proof: a proof that is too long for a human to check by hand can now be verified by a machine.

Key concepts: An axiom is a starting assumption, accepted without proof, such as that two points determine a line. A theorem is a statement that has been proved. A lemma is a small theorem proved as a step towards a bigger one, and a corollary is a result that follows easily from a theorem. A direct proof argues straight from the assumptions to the conclusion. A proof by contradiction assumes the opposite of what is to be proved and shows that it leads to something impossible. Proof by induction proves a statement for all natural numbers by proving it for 1 and then showing that if it holds for one number it holds for the next. A counterexample is a single example that disproves a claim. QED, short for the Latin quod erat demonstrandum, meaning which was to be demonstrated, marks the end of a proof. The phrase if and only if, often written iff, means that two statements imply each other, so each is both necessary and sufficient for the other. A necessary condition must hold for a statement to be true, while a sufficient condition guarantees it. A lemma is a small theorem proved as a step towards a bigger one. A corollary is a result that follows easily from a theorem just proved.

How it works: A proof works by chaining implications. Suppose the claim is that the sum of two even numbers is even. An even number is 2 times some whole number, so write the two numbers as 2a and 2b. Their sum is 2a + 2b, which is 2 times (a + b), and that is even by definition. Every step is forced by a definition or by earlier reasoning, so there is no room for doubt. To prove something for all numbers, induction is used: prove the first case, then prove the step from one case to the next, and the result follows for every case, like a row of dominoes. To disprove a claim, one counterexample is enough. A proof is finished when every step has been justified and the conclusion is exactly the claim. Definition: Infinity is the idea of something without end. It is not a number in the ordinary sense: you cannot count to it, and it does not behave like a number, since infinity plus one is still infinity. Mathematicians distinguish potential infinity, a process that never stops, such as counting on forever, from actual infinity, a completed infinite collection, such as the set of all natural numbers. Infinity appears whenever a process continues without bound, a quantity grows without limit, or a set has no largest member, and handling it carefully is one of the great achievements of mathematics.

History: The Greeks argued about infinity. Zeno of Elea, around 450 BCE, framed paradoxes in which a runner must cross infinitely many halfway points, suggesting motion is impossible, and Aristotle answered by distinguishing potential infinity, which he accepted, from actual infinity, which he rejected. For two thousand years most mathematicians followed Aristotle. Galileo noticed that the natural numbers can be paired exactly with their squares, suggesting an infinite set can be the same size as part of itself. In the 1870s Georg Cantor built set theory and showed that some infinities are bigger than others: the natural numbers are countably infinite, while the real numbers are uncountably infinite, a result proved by his diagonal argument. David Hilbert later illustrated countable infinity with his famous hotel, which is full yet always has room. In the 1800s, Cantor's work on infinite sets was at first controversial, and some mathematicians, including Kronecker, opposed it, while others, including Hilbert, defended it, saying that no one would expel mathematicians from the paradise Cantor had created. Hilbert's hotel, described in lectures in the 1920s, made countable infinity vivid. Definition: The axiomatic method is the practice of building a branch of mathematics from a small set of starting assumptions, called axioms, by proving everything else from them. A sanity check is a quick test that an answer is in the right ballpark, such as checking that a percentage is between 0 and 100. A quantitative claim is one that can be checked against numbers, and a claim that cannot be checked this way should be treated with caution.

How it works: Mathematics matters through three routes. The first is direct application: a formula is used to design, compute, or predict.
