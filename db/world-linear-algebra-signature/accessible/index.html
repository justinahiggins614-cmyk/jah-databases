What Linear Algebra Is

Linear algebra is the mathematics of vectors and matrices: lists of numbers, and tables of numbers, with rules for adding them, multiplying them, and solving the equations they describe. It is the mathematics of many variables at once: a system of linear equations, a network, a picture, or a dataset is a matrix, and the tools of linear algebra — elimination, determinants, eigenvalues — are how it is solved. Linear algebra is the most used mathematics in computing.

The system 2x + y = 5 and x - y = 1 has the solution x = 2, y = 1: adding the equations gives 3x = 6, so x = 2, and then y = 1. Check: 2 times 2 + 1 = 5, and 2 - 1 = 1. A vector in the plane, (3, 4), has length sqrt(3^2 + 4^2) = 5. A matrix with rows (1, 2) and (3, 4) times the vector (1, 1) gives (3, 7): the first row gives 1 times 1 + 2 times 1 = 3, and the second gives 3 times 1 + 4 times 1 = 7. A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels is a matrix with a million entries.

Vectors

A vector is a quantity with magnitude and direction: a force, a velocity, or a displacement. In coordinates, a vector is a list of numbers: (3, 4) is a vector in the plane, and (1, 2, 3) is a vector in space. Vectors can be added, by adding their components, and scaled, by multiplying each component by a number. The length of a vector is the square root of the sum of the squares of its components.

The vectors (3, 4) and (1, 2) add to (4, 6): 3 + 1 = 4, and 4 + 2 = 6. The vector (3, 4) has magnitude sqrt(9 + 16) = 5, and its unit vector is (3/5, 4/5) = (0.6, 0.8). Scaling (3, 4) by 2 gives (6, 8), with magnitude 10. The vector from the point (1, 2) to the point (4, 6) is (3, 4): subtract the start from the end. A force of 10 N at 30 degrees to the horizontal has components 10 cos 30 = 8.66 N horizontally and 10 sin 30 = 5 N vertically.

Vector Spaces

A vector space is a set of vectors, with addition and scaling, that obeys ten axioms: the sum of two vectors is a vector, scaling a vector gives a vector, there is a zero vector, and so on. The plane is a vector space; so is the set of all polynomials, the set of all functions, and the set of all matrices of a given size. A vector space is the abstract setting in which linear algebra lives.

The plane is a vector space: the sum of two vectors in the plane is in the plane, and a scaled vector is in the plane. The set of all polynomials of degree at most 2 is a vector space of dimension 3, with basis 1, x, x^2: every such polynomial is a + bx + cx^2. The vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) form a basis of the plane: every vector (a, b) is a(1, 0) + b(0, 1). The vectors (1, 2) and (2, 4) are dependent, because (2, 4) = 2(1, 2): they span only a line, not the plane. The set of all 2-by-2 matrices is a vector space of dimension 4.

Matrices

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, with rows and columns. Matrices can be added, by adding corresponding entries, and multiplied, by the row-by-column rule: the entry in row i and column j of the product is the dot product of row i of the first matrix and column j of the second. Matrix multiplication is the composition of linear transformations, and it is not commutative: AB is usually not BA.

The matrices [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and [[5, 6], [7, 8]] multiply to [[19, 22], [43, 50]]: the (1, 1) entry is 1 times 5 + 2 times 7 = 19, the (1, 2) is 1 times 6 + 2 times 8 = 22, the (2, 1) is 3 times 5 + 4 times 7 = 43, and the (2, 2) is 3 times 6 + 4 times 8 = 50. In the other order, the product is [[23, 34], [31, 46]], which is different: multiplication is not commutative. The identity matrix [[1, 0], [0, 1]] times any matrix leaves it unchanged. The transpose of [[1, 2, 3], [4, 5, 6]] is [[1, 4], [2, 5], [3, 6]].

Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication is the rule for combining matrices: the (i, j) entry of the product is the dot product of row i of the first matrix and column j of the second. It is defined when the number of columns of the first equals the number of rows of the second, and the product has the rows of the first and the columns of the second. Matrix multiplication is the composition of linear transformations: applying one transformation and then another is multiplying their matrices.

A 2-by-3 matrix times a 3-by-2 matrix gives a 2-by-2 matrix. The product of [[1, 2], [3, 4]] and [[0, 1], [1, 0]] is [[2, 1], [4, 3]]: the (1, 1) entry is 1 times 0 + 2 times 1 = 2, and the (2, 2) is 3 times 1 + 4 times 0 = 3. Multiplying a matrix by the identity leaves it unchanged. A rotation by 90 degrees is the matrix [[0, -1], [1, 0]]: applied to (1, 0), it gives (0, 1), which is a 90-degree rotation. Composing two rotations gives another rotation, and the product of their matrices is the matrix of the composed rotation.

Determinants

The determinant is a number assigned to a square matrix: it measures how the matrix scales volume, and whether it is invertible. A 2-by-2 matrix [[a, b], [c, d]] has determinant ad - bc. If the determinant is zero, the matrix is singular: it squashes space flat, and it has no inverse. If it is non-zero, the matrix is invertible. The determinant also appears in Cramer's rule, which solves a system by ratios of determinants.

The matrix [[1, 2], [3, 4]] has determinant 1 times 4 - 2 times 3 = -2, so it is invertible, and it scales areas by a factor of 2, flipping orientation. The matrix [[1, 2], [2, 4]] has determinant 1 times 4 - 2 times 2 = 0, so it is singular: its columns are dependent, and it squashes the plane onto a line. The identity matrix has determinant 1. A rotation matrix has determinant 1: it preserves area. Cramer's rule solves 2x + y = 5 and x - y = 1: the determinant of the coefficient matrix is -3, the determinant with the first column replaced by (5, 1) is -6, so x = (-6)/(-3) = 2, and similarly y = 1.

Systems of Linear Equations

A system of linear equations is a set of equations, each linear in the unknowns, to be solved together. The system can be written as a matrix equation Ax = b, where A is the coefficient matrix, x is the vector of unknowns, and b is the vector of constants. The solution is found by Gaussian elimination: the system is reduced to row-echelon form, and then solved by back-substitution. A system can have one solution, no solution, or infinitely many.

The system 2x + y = 5, x - y = 1 is solved by elimination: adding the equations gives 3x = 6, so x = 2, and then y = 1. The system x + y = 2, x + y = 3 is inconsistent: subtracting gives 0 = 1, so there is no solution. The system x + y = 2, 2x + 2y = 4 has infinitely many solutions: the second equation is twice the first, so y is free, and the solutions are (x, 2 - x) for any x. A system of 3 equations in 3 unknowns is solved the same way: elimination reduces it to a triangular system, and back-substitution gives the answer.

Gaussian Elimination

Gaussian elimination is the algorithm for solving a system of linear equations: the augmented matrix is reduced to row-echelon form by three row operations — swapping rows, scaling a row, and adding a multiple of one row to another — and then the system is solved by back-substitution. The operations do not change the solution set, and the triangular form is easy to solve. Gaussian elimination is the most used algorithm in numerical linear algebra.

The system 2x + y = 5, x - y = 1 is written as the augmented matrix [[2, 1 | 5], [1, -1 | 1]]. Subtract half the first row from the second: the second row becomes [0, -1.5 | -1.5], so -1.5y = -1.5, and y = 1. Back-substitute: 2x + 1 = 5, so x = 2. The LU factorisation of [[2, 1], [1, -1]] is L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]] and U = [[2, 1], [0, -1.5]]: multiplying L and U gives the original matrix. Partial pivoting swaps rows so that the largest entry is the pivot, which controls rounding errors.

Linear Independence

A set of vectors is linearly independent when no one of them is a linear combination of the others: each vector adds a new direction. A set is dependent when one vector can be written as a combination of the others: it adds nothing new. Independence is the test for a basis: a basis is an independent set that spans the space. The number of independent vectors in a set is its rank.

The vectors (1, 0) and (0, 1) are independent: neither is a multiple of the other, and they span the plane. The vectors (1, 2) and (2, 4) are dependent: (2, 4) = 2(1, 2), so the second adds nothing. The vectors (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), and (0, 0, 1) are independent, and they form the standard basis of three-dimensional space. The vectors (1, 1), (1, -1), and (2, 0) are dependent, because (2, 0) = (1, 1) + (1, -1): the third is the sum of the first two. The matrix [[1, 2], [2, 4]] has rank 1, because its columns are dependent.

Basis and Dimension

A basis of a vector space is a linearly independent set that spans the space: every vector is a unique linear combination of the basis vectors. The dimension of a space is the number of vectors in a basis: it is the same for every basis, which is a theorem, not a definition. The standard basis of the plane is (1, 0) and (0, 1); the standard basis of three-dimensional space is (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), and (0, 0, 1).

The plane has dimension 2, with standard basis (1, 0) and (0, 1): the vector (3, 4) has coordinates 3 and 4, because (3, 4) = 3(1, 0) + 4(0, 1). The set of polynomials of degree at most 2 has dimension 3, with basis 1, x, x^2: the polynomial 2 + 3x + x^2 has coordinates (2, 3, 1). The set of all 2-by-2 matrices has dimension 4. A basis of the plane can also be (1, 1) and (1, -1): the vector (3, 4) in this basis has coordinates (3.5, -0.5), because 3.5(1, 1) - 0.5(1, -1) = (3, 4).

Linear Transformations

A linear transformation is a function between vector spaces that preserves addition and scaling: T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v), and T(cu) = cT(u). Every linear transformation is a matrix: the matrix whose columns are the images of the basis vectors. Linear transformations are the maps of linear algebra: rotations, reflections, scalings, projections, and shears are all linear transformations, and they are all matrices.

A rotation by 90 degrees anticlockwise maps (1, 0) to (0, 1) and (0, 1) to (-1, 0), so its matrix is [[0, -1], [1, 0]]: applied to (1, 1), it gives (-1, 1). A scaling by 2 maps (1, 0) to (2, 0) and (0, 1) to (0, 2), so its matrix is [[2, 0], [0, 2]]. A projection onto the x-axis maps (1, 0) to (1, 0) and (0, 1) to (0, 0), so its matrix is [[1, 0], [0, 0]]: its kernel is the y-axis, and its image is the x-axis. The rank-nullity theorem says that for a transformation from the plane to the plane, the dimension of the kernel plus the dimension of the image is 2: for the projection, 1 + 1 = 2.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

An eigenvector of a matrix is a vector whose direction is unchanged by the matrix: the matrix only scales it, by a factor called the eigenvalue. If Av = λv, then v is an eigenvector and λ is its eigenvalue. Eigenvalues and eigenvectors are the most important idea in linear algebra: they are the principal axes of a dataset, the steady state of a network, and the ranking of web pages.

The matrix [[2, 0], [0, 3]] has eigenvalues 2 and 3, with eigenvectors (1, 0) and (0, 1): it scales the x-direction by 2 and the y-direction by 3. The matrix [[0, 1], [1, 0]] has eigenvalues 1 and -1, with eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -1): it swaps the coordinates. The characteristic polynomial of [[2, 1], [1, 2]] is (2 - λ)^2 - 1 = λ^2 - 4λ + 3, whose roots are λ = 1 and λ = 3: the eigenvalues are 1 and 3, with eigenvectors (1, -1) and (1, 1). Google's PageRank is the eigenvector of the web's link matrix, with eigenvalue 1: the page with the largest entry is the most important.

Diagonalisation

Diagonalisation writes a matrix as A = PDP^-1, where D is diagonal and P is the matrix of eigenvectors. In the basis of eigenvectors, the matrix acts simply: it scales each basis vector by its eigenvalue. Diagonalisation is the key to computing powers of a matrix, and to solving systems of differential equations: the powers of a diagonal matrix are the powers of its entries, and the exponential of a diagonal matrix is the exponential of its entries.

The matrix [[2, 1], [1, 2]] has eigenvalues 3 and 1, with eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, -1), so P = [[1, 1], [1, -1]] and D = [[3, 0], [0, 1]], and A = PDP^-1. The power A^2 is PD^2 P^-1: D^2 = [[9, 0], [0, 1]], and A^2 = [[5, 4], [4, 5]], which can be checked by multiplying A by itself: [[2, 1], [1, 2]] times [[2, 1], [1, 2]] is [[5, 4], [4, 5]]. The Fibonacci numbers are computed by diagonalising the matrix [[1, 1], [1, 0]]: its eigenvalues are phi and psi, and the nth Fibonacci number is (phi^n - psi^n)/sqrt(5).

Inner Products and Orthogonality

An inner product is a way of measuring angles and lengths in a vector space: the dot product of two vectors is the sum of the products of their components. Two vectors are orthogonal when their inner product is zero: they are at right angles. Orthogonality is the geometry of linear algebra: it gives projections, the Gram-Schmidt process, and least squares. A set of vectors that are mutually orthogonal and of unit length is an orthonormal basis.

The vectors (1, 2) and (2, -1) have inner product 1 times 2 + 2 times (-1) = 0, so they are orthogonal. The vectors (3, 4) and (1, 0) have inner product 3, and the projection of (3, 4) onto (1, 0) is 3(1, 0) = (3, 0): the remainder is (0, 4), which is perpendicular to (1, 0). The norm of (3, 4) is sqrt(9 + 16) = 5. The angle between (1, 0) and (1, 1) has cosine 1/sqrt(2), so the angle is 45 degrees. The standard basis is orthonormal: (1, 0) and (0, 1) are orthogonal and of unit length.

The Gram-Schmidt Process

The Gram-Schmidt process turns a set of independent vectors into an orthonormal set: it subtracts from each vector its projections onto the ones before it, and then normalises what remains. The result is an orthonormal basis for the same space, and it is the standard way to build one. Gram-Schmidt is the algorithm behind the QR factorisation, which is the standard way to solve least squares problems.

Apply Gram-Schmidt to (1, 1) and (1, 0): the first vector normalises to (1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2)). The second vector's projection onto the first is ((1, 0) dot (1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2))) times (1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2)) = (1/sqrt(2)) times (1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2)) = (0.5, 0.5). Subtracting gives (1, 0) - (0.5, 0.5) = (0.5, -0.5), which normalises to (1/sqrt(2), -1/sqrt(2)). The result is the orthonormal basis (0.707, 0.707) and (0.707, -0.707): their inner product is 0.5 - 0.5 = 0, and each has length 1.

Least Squares

Least squares is the method of fitting a model to data: it finds the parameters that minimise the sum of the squared errors between the model and the data. For a linear model, the solution is found by the normal equations, A^T A x = A^T b, and geometrically it is the projection of the data onto the column space of A. Least squares is the standard method of statistics and engineering.

Fit the line y = mx + c to the points (0, 1), (1, 3), (2, 5): the points are exactly on the line y = 2x + 1, so the least-squares fit is m = 2, c = 1, with zero residuals. Fit the line to (0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 4): the normal equations give m = 1.5 and c = 0.833, and the residuals are 0.167, -0.333, and 0.167, whose squares sum to 0.167. The fitted values are 0.833, 2.333, and 3.833. A survey of 1,000 people is a least-squares problem: the estimate is the value that minimises the squared errors, and the standard error is the square root of the variance.

Matrix Factorisations

A matrix factorisation writes a matrix as a product of simpler matrices: the LU factorisation writes A as the product of a lower-triangular and an upper-triangular matrix; the QR factorisation writes it as an orthogonal times an upper-triangular matrix; and the singular value decomposition writes it as U Σ V^T, with U and V orthogonal and Σ diagonal. Factorisations are the workhorses of numerical linear algebra: they solve systems, fit models, and compress data.

The LU factorisation of [[2, 1], [1, -1]] is L = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]] and U = [[2, 1], [0, -1.5]]: multiplying L and U gives the original matrix. The QR factorisation of [[1, 1], [1, 0]] is Q = [[0.707, 0.707], [0.707, -0.707]] and R = [[1.414, 0.707], [0, -0.707]]: Q is orthogonal, and R is upper-triangular. The singular value decomposition of a photograph keeps the largest 50 singular values and discards the rest, compressing a million numbers to 100,000. The Cholesky factorisation of [[4, 2], [2, 3]] is L = [[2, 0], [1, 1.414]], because 2 times 2 = 4, 2 times 1 = 2, and 1 times 1 + 1.414 times 1.414 = 3.

Linear Algebra in Geometry

Linear algebra in geometry is the mathematics of shapes: a line is a point plus a direction, a plane is a point plus two directions, and a transformation is a matrix. The dot product measures angles, the cross product measures areas, and the determinant measures volumes. Geometry is linear algebra made visible: every shape is a set of vectors, and every transformation is a matrix.

The line through (1, 2) in the direction (3, 4) is the set (1 + 3t, 2 + 4t): at t = 0 it is (1, 2), and at t = 1 it is (4, 6). The plane 2x + 3y + 6z = 12 meets the axes at (6, 0, 0), (0, 4, 0), and (0, 0, 2). The vectors (1, 0, 0) and (0, 1, 0) have cross product (0, 0, 1): it is perpendicular to both, and its magnitude is 1, the area of the unit square. The parallelepiped spanned by (1, 0, 0), (0, 1, 0), and (0, 0, 1) has volume 1, the determinant of the identity matrix. A rotation by 90 degrees maps (1, 0) to (0, 1).

Linear Algebra in Computer Graphics

Linear algebra in computer graphics is the mathematics of pictures: a 3D scene is a set of vectors, and the camera, the lighting, and the movement are matrices. A rotation is a matrix, a scaling is a matrix, and a projection is a matrix. The rendering of a scene is a sequence of matrix multiplications, performed millions of times a second. Computer graphics is linear algebra made visible.

A point (1, 0, 0) rotated 90 degrees about the z-axis becomes (0, 1, 0): the rotation matrix is [[0, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]], and multiplying it by (1, 0, 0) gives (0, 1, 0). A scaling by 2 in all directions multiplies every coordinate by 2: the point (1, 2, 3) becomes (2, 4, 6). A perspective projection divides by the depth: a point at (1, 1, 2) projects to (0.5, 0.5), because the coordinates are divided by the depth 2. The lighting of a surface with normal (0, 0, 1) and a light from (0, 0, 1) is the dot product, which is 1: the surface is fully lit.

Linear Algebra in Data Science

Linear algebra in data science is the mathematics of datasets: a dataset is a matrix, with rows for observations and columns for features, and the analysis is linear algebra. The principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix; the regression is a least-squares problem; and the recommendation is a singular value decomposition. Data science is linear algebra on large matrices.

A dataset of 1,000 people with 10 features is a 1,000-by-10 matrix, with 10,000 entries. The principal components of a dataset of heights and weights are the directions of greatest variance: the first component is roughly the size, and the second is the shape. A regression of weight on height predicts that each extra centimetre adds about 0.8 kg. A recommendation system factorises the matrix of users and films: the singular value decomposition finds the latent factors, and the prediction is the product of the factors. A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels is compressed by keeping the largest 50 singular values.

Linear Algebra in Quantum Mechanics

Linear algebra in quantum mechanics is the mathematics of the quantum world: the state of a system is a vector in a Hilbert space, the observables are matrices, and the outcomes are the eigenvalues. The Schrödinger equation is a linear differential equation, and its solutions are the eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian. Quantum mechanics is linear algebra, and it is the most successful theory in science.

A qubit is a vector in two dimensions: the state (1/sqrt(2), 1/sqrt(2)) has a probability of 1/2 of being measured as 0, and 1/2 as 1, because the probabilities are the squares of the amplitudes, (1/sqrt(2))^2 = 1/2. The energy levels of a hydrogen atom are the eigenvalues of its Hamiltonian: -13.6/n^2 eV, so the first level is -13.6 eV and the second is -3.4 eV. A spin-1/2 particle has two states, up and down, and the observable for the z-component of spin is the matrix [[1, 0], [0, -1]]/2, with eigenvalues 1/2 and -1/2. The evolution of a state is a unitary matrix: it preserves the total probability, which is the length of the vector, equal to 1.

Singular Value Decomposition

The singular value decomposition writes a matrix A as U Σ V^T, where U and V are orthogonal matrices and Σ is diagonal, with non-negative entries called the singular values. It is the most versatile factorisation in linear algebra: it gives the rank, the least-squares solution, the pseudoinverse, and the best low-rank approximation. The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of A^T A, and they measure the importance of each direction.

The matrix [[3, 0], [0, 2]] has singular values 3 and 2: it is already diagonal, so its singular value decomposition is itself, with U and V the identity. The matrix [[1, 1], [1, 1]] has singular values sqrt(2) times sqrt(2) = 2 and 0: its rank is 1, because only one singular value is non-zero. A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels has 1,000 singular values: keeping the largest 50 compresses a million numbers to 100,000, a factor of 10, with little visible loss. The best rank-1 approximation of a matrix is the first term of its singular value decomposition: the largest singular value times the outer product of the first columns of U and V.

A History of Linear Algebra

Linear algebra has no single founder: it is the coming together of the solving of equations, the geometry of vectors, and the algebra of matrices. The solving of equations is 2,000 years old, the vector is from the 19th century, and the matrix is from 1858. The subject took its modern form in the 20th century, when the vector space and the linear transformation became its foundation, and it is now the most used mathematics in computing.

The timeline: the Nine Chapters, about 200 BCE; Leibniz's determinants, 1693; Cramer's rule, 1750; Grassmann's vectors, 1844; Cayley's matrices, 1858; Peano's vector spaces, 1888; Hilbert's eigenvalues, 1904; the first computers, 1940s; the fast algorithms, 1960s; PageRank, 1998. The span from the Nine Chapters to Cayley's matrices is about 2,000 years. The subject's parts are old, but the subject itself is new: it was the 20th century that brought equations, vectors, and matrices together into one subject, and it is the computer that keeps it growing.

Linear Algebra in the Modern World

Linear algebra in the modern world is everywhere: the search engine, the photograph, the recommendation, and the robot are all linear algebra. The modern world runs on matrices that are computed, not just written: the data are matrices, the models are matrices, and the computation is a factorisation. Linear algebra is the invisible infrastructure of modern life, and it is the first mathematics that a computer scientist learns.

A search engine answers a query in a fraction of a second: it looks up the words in an index, ranks the pages by PageRank, and returns the top ten. A photograph of 1,000 by 1,000 pixels is a matrix with a million entries: it is compressed by keeping the largest 50 singular values, reducing a million numbers to 100,000. A recommendation system predicts what a user will like by factorising the matrix of users and films: the prediction is the product of the user's factors and the film's factors. A robot arm with six joints has six matrices: the position of the hand is the product of the six, applied to the base.
