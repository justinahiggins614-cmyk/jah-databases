Scale Models and Model Kits

JAH-MODEL-000001 · ladder rank 1 · public-harvest

A scale model is a physical replica of a real object, built at a fixed ratio such as 1:72 or 1:24. Model kits — plastic snap-together kits, wood ship models, and die-cast collectibles — are the most common form. Scales are written with the model size first: a 1:48 model is one forty-eighth the size of the real thing. Common scales include 1:72 and 1:48 for aircraft, 1:35 for armor, and 1:24 or 1:25 for cars. Building a kit teaches reading plans, gluing, painting, and patience. Start with a simple snap-fit kit before moving to glue-and-paint kits.

  • Name the common scale ratios used in model kits
  • Explain how scale ratios work
  • Compare snap-fit and glue kits
  • List the tools a beginner modeler needs
  • Describe basic painting and finishing steps

Architectural Models

JAH-MODEL-000002 · ladder rank 2 · public-harvest

Architects build physical scale models of buildings to study form, light, and space before construction. Materials include foam board, basswood, museum board, and 3D-printed plastic. Common architectural scales are 1:200 for whole sites, 1:100 for building massing, and 1:50 or 1:20 for detail studies. A good model shows structure, openings, and material changes clearly rather than decorating every surface. White card models are a classic presentation style because they emphasize form over color.

  • List the materials used in architectural models
  • Match scales to model purposes
  • Explain what a massing model shows
  • Describe presentation-model conventions
  • Explain how models aid design decisions

Fashion Models — The Profession

JAH-MODEL-000003 · ladder rank 3 · public-harvest

Fashion models present clothing, accessories, and footwear for designers, photographers, retailers, and advertisers. Categories include runway, editorial, commercial, plus-size, petite, and fit modeling. Models work through agencies that arrange castings and bookings and take a commission. A portfolio of professional photographs is the model's main calling card. The work requires punctuality, physical stamina, and the ability to take direction from photographers and stylists.

Role Models

JAH-MODEL-000004 · ladder rank 4 · public-harvest

A role model is a person whose behavior, achievements, or character others admire and imitate. Role models can be family members, teachers, athletes, scientists, or historical figures. Research in psychology shows that relatable role models — people who overcame obstacles similar to the observer's — are the most motivating. Being a role model carries responsibility: actions speak louder than stated values. Communities often celebrate role models through awards, mentorship programs, and storytelling.

  • Define what a role model is
  • List common sources of role models
  • Explain why relatable role models motivate
  • Describe the responsibilities of a role model
  • Give examples of community role-model programs

Scientific and Computational Models

JAH-MODEL-000005 · ladder rank 6 · public-harvest

Scientists use models — simplified representations of reality — to explain and predict. A model can be physical (a globe), mathematical (an equation for population growth), or computational (a climate simulation). Good models capture the essential behavior of a system while ignoring irrelevant detail. Models are tested by comparing their predictions against real observations, and refined when they disagree. Famous examples include the Bohr model of the atom, epidemiological SIR models, and general circulation models of climate.

  • Distinguish physical, mathematical, and computational models
  • Explain how models are tested and refined
  • Describe the SIR epidemiological model
  • Explain what a climate circulation model does
  • Give the trade-off between simplicity and accuracy

Demo: SIR model one step

let S=990,I=10,R=0;const b=0.3,g=0.1,N=1000;const nI=b*S*I/N,nR=g*I;S-=nI;I+=nI-nR;R+=nR;J({S:Math.round(S),I:Math.round(I),R:Math.round(R)});assert(I>10&&R>=1,'sir');

Data Models

JAH-MODEL-000006 · ladder rank 7 · public-harvest

A data model describes how information is organized: the entities, their attributes, and the relationships between them. The relational model organizes data into tables with rows and columns linked by keys. The entity-relationship diagram is the classic design tool for relational databases. Other models include document stores, graph databases, and key-value stores, each suited to different shapes of data. Choosing the right data model is one of the most consequential early decisions in building software.

  • Define a data model
  • Explain the relational model
  • Describe entity-relationship diagrams
  • Compare document, graph, and key-value models
  • Explain why the data model choice matters

Demo: Relational join check

Product Model Naming and Model Numbers

JAH-MODEL-000007 · ladder rank 8 · public-harvest

Manufacturers identify product lines with model names and numbers: a car has a model year and trim, a phone has a model number like SM-S918. Model numbers often encode generation, region, and configuration. Keeping a clear model-naming scheme helps customers compare products and helps support teams identify exactly which unit a customer owns. Serial numbers are different: they identify one individual unit, while a model number identifies the whole design. Good model naming is short, consistent, and avoids confusing look-alike characters like O and 0.

  • Distinguish model numbers from serial numbers
  • Explain what model numbers encode
  • Describe good model-naming practice
  • Explain trim levels in vehicles
  • Explain why naming consistency matters

Demo: Model number parsing

Miniatures, Dioramas, and Wargaming Models

JAH-MODEL-000008 · ladder rank 9 · public-harvest

Miniature models — from 6mm to 54mm figures — are used in tabletop wargames, dioramas, and dollhouses. Wargaming miniatures are typically cast in plastic, resin, or white metal and painted by hand. A diorama places models in a scenic setting to tell a story, using ground texture, foliage, and backdrops. Common wargaming scales are 28mm and 32mm for skirmish games and 6–15mm for mass battles. Priming, base-coating, washing, and dry-brushing are the core painting techniques.
