Dialect

A dialect is a variety of a language: the speech of a region, a class, or a group, with its own sounds, words, and grammar. Yorkshire English, African American English, and Bavarian German are dialects, and each is a complete system, not a broken version of a standard. Dialects differ, but no dialect is better than another as a language: the differences are social, not linguistic. Sociolect

A sociolect is the speech of a social group: the variety used by a class, a profession, or an age group, marked by its words, its sounds, and its grammar. Teenagers, lawyers, and sailors each have a sociolect, and the sociolect says who the speaker is and which group they belong to. Sociolects change fast, because groups change fast, and their words spread or die with the group. Register

Register is the language fitted to the situation: the formal language of a courtroom, the informal language of friends, and the technical language of a laboratory are different registers of one language. Speakers shift register as the situation changes, and the shift says what the speaker thinks of the hearer and of the occasion. Register is the most flexible part of language, and it is learned with the situations it fits. Code-Switching

Code-switching is the moving between languages in one conversation: a speaker uses Spanish for one sentence and English for the next, or mixes the two inside a sentence, because the topic, the hearer, or the feeling calls for it. Code-switching is not confusion: it follows rules, it marks identity, and it is the normal speech of bilingual communities. It is studied for what it says about how two languages live in one mind. Diglossia

Diglossia is the use of two varieties of a language for two uses: a high variety for formal occasions and a low variety for everyday talk. In Arabic, the high variety is used in writing and in the mosque, and the low variety is spoken at home, and speakers learn both and know which to use where. Diglossia is stable over centuries, and it shapes education, because children must learn the high variety at school. Social Networks

Social networks are the webs of who talks to whom: the friends, family, and workmates whose speech a person hears every day. A speaker's language is shaped by their network: a dense network, where everyone knows everyone, keeps the local dialect strong, while a loose network, with many outside contacts, lets new forms in. Social networks explain why language changes in some places and stays put in others. Style-Shifting

It is the most everyday form of language variation, and everyone does it. Prestige

Prestige is the value given to a way of speaking: the standard is prestigious because the people who speak it are powerful, and the dialect is stigmatised because its speakers are not. Prestige is social, not linguistic: no variety is better as a language, but some are treated as better, and the treatment affects who is hired, believed, and heard. Prestige shapes which varieties spread and which are hidden. Standard Language

A standard language is the variety chosen as the norm: the one taught in schools, used in writing, and heard on the news. Vernacular

The vernacular is the everyday speech of a community: the language spoken at home, with friends, and in the street, without care for the standard. The vernacular is the most systematic and the most natural form of a language, and it is where change begins: new forms appear in the vernacular long before they reach the standard. Sociolinguists study the vernacular because it shows the language as it is really used. Pidgin

A pidgin is a simple language made for contact: when speakers of different languages must trade or work together and share no language, they build one from the words of one language and the grammar of none, or of very little. Pidgins have small vocabularies, simple grammars, and no native speakers, and they are used only for the contact that made them. When children learn a pidgin as their first language, it becomes a creole. Creole

A creole is a pidgin that became a mother tongue: when children learn a pidgin as their first language, they expand it, giving it the grammar and the vocabulary of a full language. Creoles are complete languages, with native speakers, with literature, and with their own grammar, and they show that a full language can grow from a simple one in a single generation. Shift happens under pressure, from schools, from work, and from the prestige of the new language, and it is the main cause of language death. Shift is studied for why it happens, how fast it goes, and how it can be stopped. Language Maintenance

Language maintenance is the keeping of a language alive: the efforts of a community to keep speaking its language, to teach it to children, and to use it in public life. Ethnography of Communication

It asks what counts as speaking well in a community, and it finds that the rules are different everywhere. The ethnography of communication is the most human part of sociolinguistics, because it studies language as it is lived.
