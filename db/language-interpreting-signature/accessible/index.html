Consecutive Interpreting

Consecutive interpreting is the interpreting done after the speaker pauses: the interpreter listens to a passage, takes notes, and then renders it in the other language. It is used in courtrooms, medical consultations, and small meetings, where accuracy matters more than speed and the interpreter can ask for repetition. The passage is usually a few minutes long, and the notes are the interpreter's memory. Simultaneous Interpreting

Whispered Interpreting

Whispered interpreting, or chuchotage, is the interpreting whispered into the ear of one or two listeners while the speaker speaks. The interpreter sits beside the listener and whispers the rendering, without a booth and without equipment. It is used in small meetings, in courtrooms for a single defendant, and in tours, where only one person needs the other language. Sight Translation

Sight translation is the reading of a written text aloud in another language: the interpreter reads the document in one language and speaks it in the other, at sight, without preparation. It is used for short documents in courtrooms, hospitals, and offices, where a written text must be understood at once and there is no time for a written translation. Conference Interpreting

Conference interpreting is the interpreting done at international conferences, in simultaneous mode from booths, in several languages at once. Conference interpreters are accredited by international bodies and work for the United Nations, the European Union, and world summits. Community Interpreting

Community interpreting is the interpreting done in public services: in hospitals, schools, courts, and welfare offices, for people who do not speak the local language. The interpreter works face to face, in consecutive mode, and must handle sensitive subjects, strong emotions, and the unequal power between the official and the client. It is the largest field of interpreting by number of assignments. Court Interpreting

Court interpreting is the interpreting done in courts of law: for defendants, witnesses, and victims who do not speak the language of the court. The interpreter must render everything exactly, including hesitations and swearing, because the record is the evidence. Court interpreters are sworn, tested, and bound by a strict code, and a wrong rendering can change a verdict. Relay Interpreting

Relay interpreting is the interpreting done through a third language: when no interpreter works directly between two rare languages, one interpreter renders the speech into a common language, and a second renders it from there into the target. It is used at international conferences with many languages, where direct pairs cannot cover every combination. Relay adds a step, and with it a risk of loss, so it is used only when no direct interpreter exists. Note-Taking

Note-taking is the interpreter's written memory: the system of symbols, abbreviations, and layout that lets a consecutive interpreter remember a passage of several minutes. The notes do not record the words, they record the ideas and their links, so the interpreter can render the sense rather than repeat the sentences. Good notes are brief, visual, and personal to the interpreter who made them. Memory in Interpreting

Ethics of Interpreting

The ethics of interpreting are the rules that bind interpreters: accuracy, impartiality, and confidentiality. The interpreter renders everything, takes no side, keeps nothing back, and never adds, omits, or explains. The code exists because the interpreter stands between people who cannot check each other, and trust is the whole of the profession. Accuracy and Fidelity

Accuracy and fidelity are the rendering of the whole message: the meaning, the tone, and the intent, without addition, omission, or softening. A faithful rendering of a threat is a threat, and a faithful rendering of a hesitation keeps the hesitation. Fidelity is measured against the speaker's message, not against the interpreter's judgement of what the hearer should hear. Register in Interpreting

Register in interpreting is the matching of the speaker's level of formality: a judge's formal language must sound formal in the rendering, and a witness's slang must sound like slang, not like a lawyer. The interpreter must hear the register and reproduce it, because the register tells the hearer who the speaker is and how the words should be taken. Turn-Taking

Turn-taking is the management of who speaks when in interpreted talk: the interpreter must let each speaker finish, render the turn, and hand the floor back without losing the thread. In interpreted conversation the turns are longer and the gaps are different, and the interpreter must manage the flow as well as the words, so that neither speaker is left waiting or cut off. Cultural Mediation

Cultural mediation is the bridging of cultures as well as languages: the interpreter explains what a gesture means, why a question is rude, or how a refusal is softened, when the words alone would mislead. The interpreter mediates only where the message would fail without it, and never by adding opinions of their own. Mediation is the most disputed duty in interpreting, because it sits beside the duty of neutrality. Team Interpreting

Team interpreting is the sharing of the work by two interpreters: one interprets while the other listens, feeds terms, and takes over after twenty or thirty minutes. The team works in the booth in simultaneous interpreting, and in long consecutive assignments, where fatigue would break accuracy. The second interpreter is not resting: they are monitoring, noting, and ready to take over at any moment. Booth Practice

Booth practice is the working of the interpreting booth: the headphones, the microphone, the console, and the discipline of speaking while listening. The interpreter must keep the voice steady, the lag short, and the rendering complete, while the partner feeds terms and watches the time. Booth practice is a skill of its own, learned by hours of live practice, and it is tested in every conference examination. Terminology Preparation

Terminology preparation is the getting of the words ready before the job: the interpreter reads the papers, lists the technical terms, and learns them in both languages, so that no term is met for the first time in the booth. Preparation is the difference between an interpreter who knows the subject and one who guesses, and conference interpreters spend as much time preparing as interpreting.
