JAH-R20-MSIM-000001 — Simulation framework — colliding at the micro scale

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Micro-scale collision scenarios computed with standard, working physics (classical scattering, quantum-mechanical formulas as public results, conservation laws). Computed scenarios, not observed events. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

At the micro scale the collider is the scattering experiment. This record defines the framework: each scenario states the projectile, the target, the interaction (electromagnetic, strong, weak), the energy regime (non-relativistic, relativistic, ultra-relativistic), and the observable (scattering angle distribution, cross section, decay products, annihilation photons). The computations use standard results: Rutherford scattering for charged particles off nuclei, the de Broglie wavelength for resolution, E = mc^2 for annihilation energetics, and the public particle masses as inputs.

Three scenario classes. Class A: scattering — Rutherford alpha scattering, electron diffraction, neutron scattering off nuclei. Class B: decay and transformation — alpha/beta decay chains, muon decay, particle lifetimes as public inputs. Class C: creation and annihilation — electron-positron annihilation, pair production, and LHC-energy proton collisions scaled down to single-interaction bookkeeping.

The framework's honesty header: quantum-mechanical formulas are used as established public results (their derivations belong to textbooks, not to this database), inputs are public particle data, and outputs are scenario computations. The generator extends by varying energies and targets within stated ranges — never by inventing particles or forces.

JAH-R20-MSIM-000002 — Scenario: Rutherford scattering — the atom has a nucleus

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Micro-scale collision scenarios computed with standard, working physics (classical scattering, quantum-mechanical formulas as public results, conservation laws). Computed scenarios, not observed events. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class A, recreating the public 1909-1911 Geiger-Marsden experiment as a computed scenario: alpha particles (helium nuclei, ~5 MeV, from natural radioactive decay) fired at a thin gold foil. Inputs: alpha energy 5 MeV, gold nucleus charge Z=79, foil thin enough that multiple scattering is negligible. Assumption: pure Coulomb repulsion, point charges.

Computation: the Rutherford formula gives the scattering probability falling as 1/sin^4(theta/2) — most alphas pass through nearly undeflected, but about 1 in 8,000 bounces back past 90 degrees. That rare backscattering is the whole discovery: it proved the atom's mass sits in a tiny dense nucleus, because only a concentrated charge could hurl an alpha backward. The distance of closest approach for a head-on alpha is ~30 femtometres — setting the scale of the nucleus.

The simulation varies the inputs the original experiment couldn't: different target Z (silver, platinum), different alpha energies, and the modern extension — electron scattering, which maps the nucleus's charge distribution rather than just its existence. All variants use the same public formula; all are labeled scenarios. The historical result they reproduce is public fact.

JAH-R20-MSIM-000003 — Scenario: electron-positron annihilation — mass into light

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Micro-scale collision scenarios computed with standard, working physics (classical scattering, quantum-mechanical formulas as public results, conservation laws). Computed scenarios, not observed events. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class C: an electron meets a positron at rest and both vanish into two gamma-ray photons, each carrying exactly 511 keV — the electron's rest energy. Inputs: electron/positron mass 0.511 MeV/c^2, at rest (kinetic energy zero). Assumption: the lowest-order process; higher-order variants (three photons, or annihilation in flight) are treated as labeled extensions.

Computation: E = mc^2 gives 2 x 0.511 MeV = 1.022 MeV total, split between two photons emitted back-to-back to conserve momentum. Run in reverse — pair production — a photon above 1.022 MeV passing near a nucleus can become an electron-positron pair; the nucleus recoils to conserve momentum. The scenario computes both directions and the threshold energies, then scales up: at LEP, electrons and positrons annihilated at 209 GeV, and the same conservation laws governed — only the debris was richer (Z bosons, quark jets).

The database uses annihilation as the teaching anchor for E = mc^2: mass is frozen energy, and the books balance exactly. Medical connection (public): PET scans detect exactly these 511 keV photon pairs from positrons emitted by tracers — the same physics, in hospitals.

JAH-R20-MSIM-000004 — Scenario: a single LHC proton-proton collision, slowed down

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Micro-scale collision scenarios computed with standard, working physics (classical scattering, quantum-mechanical formulas as public results, conservation laws). Computed scenarios, not observed events. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class C at the energy frontier: one proton-proton collision at 13.6 TeV centre-of-mass, unpacked into slow motion. Inputs: two 6.8 TeV protons, each a bag of quarks and gluons; the actual collision is between one parton from each, carrying a fraction x of the momentum (public parton distributions from HERA set the probabilities). Assumption: leading-order picture; the full event simulation belongs to programs like PYTHIA, referenced as public tools, not reproduced here.

Computation: at 13.6 TeV the collision energy is ~14,500 times the protons' rest energy — nearly all of it is kinetic, available to make new particles. A Higgs-producing collision (gluon fusion) happens roughly once per few billion collisions; the Higgs then decays in ~10^-22 seconds, and the detectors see only the decay products (two photons, four leptons). The scenario walks through the bookkeeping: energy in, particles out, with the trigger system (public LHC figures) keeping ~1,000 of the billion collisions per second.

The point of slowing it down: every record in the CERN database describes these collisions from outside; this scenario describes one from inside, using the same public numbers. Labeled simulation, standard physics throughout.

JAH-R20-MSIM-000005 — Scenario: radioactive decay chains — the slow colliders

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Micro-scale collision scenarios computed with standard, working physics (classical scattering, quantum-mechanical formulas as public results, conservation laws). Computed scenarios, not observed events. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class B: unstable nuclei as one-sided colliders, where the "collision" is internal. Inputs: public half-lives and decay modes. Worked chains: uranium-238 to lead-206 (14 steps, 4.5-billion-year half-life — the clock behind radiometric dating), thorium-232 to lead-208, and the fast chains like radon-222 (3.8-day half-life, the public-health-relevant one).

Computation: each alpha decay ejects a helium nucleus with a characteristic energy (4-9 MeV), each beta decay transmutes a neutron to a proton. The scenario computes the activity of a sample (decays per second), the heat released (the reason Earth's interior is hot — public geophysics), and the secular-equilibrium condition where a long chain's activities equalize. A worked example: 1 kg of U-238 has an activity of ~12 MBq and releases ~0.1 watts of heat — small per kilogram, planetary over 10^24 kg.

The generator extends to labeled what-ifs: what if half-lives were different (they're fixed by nature — the scenario shows how sensitively dating and reactor physics depend on them). All nuclear data is public (NNDC); the scenarios are computed.
