JAH-R20-PMSIM-000001 — Public data: the particle zoo — masses, charges, lifetimes

This public record is the input table for every micro-collider scenario: the established particle properties from the Particle Data Group (PDG), the field's public bible. Stable matter: electron (0.511 MeV/c^2, charge -1, stable), proton (938.272 MeV/c^2, +1, stable), neutron (939.565 MeV/c^2, 0, 880 s lifetime free). Leptons: muon (105.66 MeV, 2.2 µs), tau (1776.9 MeV, 0.29 ps), three neutrinos (near-massless). Quarks: up/down (few MeV), strange (~95 MeV), charm (1.27 GeV), bottom (4.18 GeV), top (172.8 GeV, decays in 10^-25 s — it decays before it can form atoms).

Force carriers: photon (massless, stable), gluon (massless, confined), W (80.38 GeV, 10^-25 s), Z (91.19 GeV, 10^-25 s), Higgs (125.1 GeV, 10^-22 s). Composite benchmarks: pion (139.6/135.0 MeV), kaon (~494 MeV), J/psi (3.097 GeV), Upsilon (9.46 GeV). Antiparticles mirror every entry with opposite charge.

The simulation edition must use these values or state its deviation; invented particles are forbidden by the framework. Uncertainties are quoted where the PDG quotes them (e.g., Higgs mass ±0.11 GeV combined). This table is the reason the micro scenarios can claim standard physics: their inputs are the world's measured constants.

JAH-R20-PMSIM-000002 — Public data: landmark scattering experiments

The micro-collider scenarios reenact public experiments; this record holds the originals. Rutherford/Geiger-Marsden (1909-1911): alpha particles on gold foil, ~1 in 8,000 backscattered — the nucleus discovered. Davisson-Germer (1927): electrons diffracted by nickel crystal — matter waves confirmed, de Broglie's hypothesis verified. Hofstadter (1950s): electron scattering mapped the proton's finite size (~0.84 fm) — Nobel 1961. SLAC deep-inelastic scattering (1968): electrons knocked quarks out of protons — quarks proven real, Nobel 1990 (Friedman, Kendall, Taylor).

Collider era: the J/psi discovery (1974, "November Revolution" — charm quark, Nobel 1976); UA1/UA2's W and Z (1983, Nobel 1984); the top quark at the Tevatron (1995); the tau neutrino at DONUT (2000); the Higgs at the LHC (2012, Nobel 2013). Each entry gives the public essentials: year, laboratory, beam and target, energy, signature, and what it established.

The pattern across a century: higher energy resolves smaller structure, and each new scale reveals new constituents — atoms to nuclei to nucleons to quarks, with the current frontier at 10^-19 m probed by the LHC. The simulation edition's scenarios are computed reenactments of these experiments; the experiments themselves are history, recorded here.

JAH-R20-PMSIM-000003 — Public data: radioactivity — the public decay tables

The decay scenarios' inputs are public nuclear data from the National Nuclear Data Center (NNDC). Key public chains: U-238 -> Pb-206 (14 decays, 4.47 x 10^9 yr half-life); U-235 -> Pb-207 (11 decays, 7.04 x 10^8 yr); Th-232 -> Pb-208 (10 decays, 1.41 x 10^10 yr); K-40 -> Ar-40/Ca-40 (1.25 x 10^9 yr — the potassium-argon dating clock); C-14 -> N-14 (5,730 yr — the radiocarbon clock); Rn-222 -> Po-218 (3.82 days — the indoor-air health hazard, public EPA guidance).

Public applications: radiometric dating (the 4.54-billion-year age of Earth rests on these chains), nuclear medicine (Tc-99m's 6-hour half-life is ideal for imaging; I-131 treats thyroid disease), smoke detectors (Am-241), and RTGs (Pu-238 powers deep-space probes like Voyager). Decay heat: Earth's ~47 TW heat flow is roughly half radiogenic (public geophysics), mostly from U, Th, and K in the crust and mantle.

Units and safety are public too: the becquerel (decays/second), the sievert (biological dose), background radiation (~2.4 mSv/year global average, UNSCEAR). The simulation edition computes with these tables; it does not invent isotopes.
