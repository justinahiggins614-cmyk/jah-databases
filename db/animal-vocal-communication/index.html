What Animal Vocal Communication Is JAH-AVC-000001 · simple

Animal vocal communication is the exchange of information between animals through sounds they produce with their own bodies: calls, songs, roars, howls, chirps, and clicks. A vocal signal carries information only when a receiver responds to it in a way that changes its behaviour, for example by approaching, fleeing, or answering. Researchers therefore study both sides of the exchange, the sound a sender makes and the response it reliably produces in listeners of the same species. This record covers the biophony layer only: sounds made by living organisms. It does not cover wind, water, or weather sounds, which belong to geophony, nor human-made sounds, which belong to anthrophony, and those layers are named here so the distinction stays clear on every record in this pair.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: the sender-receiver definition of communication is the standard frame in animal behaviour textbooks, and playback experiments, in which a recorded call is played back and the response measured, are the accepted test that a sound functions as a signal rather than a mere by-product of movement or breathing.

Math proof

Math proof: if a call lasting 0.5 seconds is repeated 40 times in one minute, its duty cycle is 40 x 0.5 / 60 = 0.333, about 33 percent of the minute filled with sound.

Source

General animal behaviour references: playback-experiment method as described in standard ethology texts (as of October 2026).

Birdsong and Bird Calls: Two Different Jobs JAH-AVC-000002 · simple

Birds use two broad classes of vocalisation. Calls are usually short, simple, and used all year for alarms, contact between flock members, begging by chicks, and keeping a pair together. Songs are longer, more complex, and in many temperate species are sung mainly by males in the breeding season to defend territory and attract mates. The same bird may have both: a robin, for example, sings a complex territorial song in spring and also gives short alarm and contact calls. Learning matters too: many songbirds learn their song from adults, while most calls are far less dependent on learning. This record covers the biophony layer, the sounds of living birds, and keeps it distinct from the wind and rain of geophony and the traffic of anthrophony that form the background a bird must sing through.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: field studies across songbird species consistently show that song playback inside a territory draws a territorial response from the resident male, while alarm-call playback makes nearby birds freeze or flee, confirming that the two classes have different functions.

Math proof

Math proof: a song of 12 syllables sung in 3.0 seconds gives a syllable rate of 12 / 3.0 = 4.0 syllables per second.

Source

General ornithology references: song-versus-call distinction in standard bird behaviour texts (as of October 2026).

Alarm Calls: Warnings That Name the Danger JAH-AVC-000003 · simple

An alarm call warns other animals that a predator is near, and in some species different alarms name different kinds of danger. The best-documented case is the vervet monkey of African savannas, studied by Robert Seyfarth and Dorothy Cheney: vervets give acoustically different alarm calls for leopards, eagles, and snakes, and listeners respond differently to each, climbing trees for a leopard alarm, looking up for an eagle alarm, and standing bipedally to scan the ground for a snake alarm. Playback experiments confirmed the point: playing a leopard alarm with no leopard present still sent listeners up trees. This is biophony, sound made by living animals, and it is studied separately from geophony and anthrophony even though a real savanna soundscape contains all three layers at once.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: Seyfarth and Cheney's playback experiments with vervet monkeys, published in the 1980s, are the classic demonstration that listeners treat different alarm calls as referring to different predator types.

Math proof

Math proof: if 3 alarm types each occur 20 times in a 10-minute watch, the combined alarm rate is 60 / 10 = 6.0 alarms per minute.

Source

Seyfarth and Cheney, vervet monkey alarm-call studies, 1980s (as of October 2026).

Mating Songs: The Humpback Whale's Long Performance JAH-AVC-000004 · simple

Male humpback whales sing long, structured songs on their breeding grounds, sequences of moans, cries, and chirps organised into phrases and themes that can last tens of minutes and are repeated for hours. Roger Payne and Scott McVay first described this organised song structure in the early 1970s, and later work showed that all males in one breeding population tend to sing the same song at any given time, with the song gradually changing across a season and being largely replaced over years. Humpback song energy lies mostly in the low hundreds of hertz up to a few kilohertz, well within the range a hydrophone in the ocean can record. This record covers biophony, the whale's own sound, distinct from ocean geophony such as waves and from anthrophony such as ship noise, which can mask whale song.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: Payne and McVay (1971) documented the hierarchical phrase-and-theme structure of humpback song, and decades of recordings since have confirmed population-wide song sharing and gradual song evolution.

Math proof

Math proof: a song lasting 20 minutes repeated 3 times fills 60 minutes, which is 60 / 60 = 1.0 hour of singing.

Source

Payne and McVay, Science, 1971; subsequent humpback song literature (as of October 2026).

Territorial Calls: Holding Ground With Sound JAH-AVC-000005 · simple

Many animals defend a territory with sound rather than fighting, because a loud call advertises occupancy and fighting ability at low risk. Male red deer roar during the autumn rut, and rivals judge each other partly by roaring rate and roar pitch; howler monkeys of Central and South America produce deep roars audible over long distances through the forest canopy, advertising a troop's presence to neighbouring troops. In both cases the call's job is spacing: it tells a rival that the ground is taken and roughly how strong its holder is, so contests are often settled by calling contests before any physical contact. This record covers the biophony layer. The forest wind and rain behind the roar are geophony, and any human sound nearby is anthrophony, and the three layers are kept distinct even when a listener hears them blended.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: playback and observation studies of red deer roaring contests and howler monkey dawn roaring both show that rivals respond to call rate and pitch as indicators of the caller, and that calling reduces the frequency of physical fights.

Math proof

Math proof: a howler roar of 2.0 seconds repeated every 10 seconds has a duty cycle of 2.0 / 10 = 0.20, or 20 percent.

Source

General animal behaviour references on red deer roaring and howler monkey vocal behaviour (as of October 2026).

Frog Choruses: Many Voices, One Night JAH-AVC-000006 · simple

On warm wet nights, male frogs gather at ponds and call in chorus, each species with its own call pattern of pulse rate, pitch, and timing, so that females can find males of their own species in a mixed chorus. Chorusing is energetically expensive and attracts predators as well as mates, which is why males often time their calls to avoid overlapping a neighbour's call or, in some species, deliberately overlap to compete. Temperature matters: in many frogs the pulse rate of the call rises as body temperature rises, so a recording of call rate can carry information about conditions as well as identity. This record covers biophony, the frogs' own chorus, distinct from the geophony of rain and running water at the same pond and from anthrophony such as nearby road noise, which can mask frog calls.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: long-term field studies of frog choruses show species-specific pulse rates and temperature-dependent call rates, and playback experiments show females prefer calls at the pulse rate typical of their own species.

Math proof

Math proof: a frog calling at 30 pulses per second for 2 seconds produces 30 x 2 = 60 pulses per call.

Source

General herpetology references on anuran chorus behaviour (as of October 2026).

Elephant Infrasound: Voices Below Human Hearing JAH-AVC-000007 · compounded

African and Asian elephants communicate with rumbles whose fundamental frequencies can fall below 20 hertz, into infrasound that humans feel rather than hear, alongside higher harmonics that humans can hear. Because low frequencies travel far and bend around obstacles, elephant rumbles can be detected by other elephants kilometres away through both air and ground vibration, and researchers record them with specialised low-frequency microphones and seismic sensors. Elephant rumbles serve contact, reunion, alarm, and musth-related signalling, and a single rumble can carry identity information in its fine structure. This record covers the biophony layer. Thunder and wind are geophony, and vehicle noise is anthrophony, and both can interfere with elephant communication, which is why recordings must separate the layers.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: research by Joyce Poole, Katharine Payne, and colleagues established that elephant rumbles contain infrasonic components and that elephants respond to playbacks of rumbles from distant, unseen callers.

Math proof

Math proof: a sound wave at 15 hertz has a period of 1 / 15 = 0.0667 seconds, about 67 milliseconds per cycle.

Source

Poole and Payne, elephant infrasonic communication research (as of October 2026).

Bat Echolocation: Calls That Come Back JAH-AVC-000008 · compounded

Many bats navigate and hunt with echolocation: they emit short ultrasonic calls, usually well above 20 kilohertz and in many species between about 20 and 200 kilohertz, and listen for the echoes returning from insects, walls, and foliage. As a bat closes in on prey, its calls typically get shorter and faster, ending in a rapid feeding buzz, and the echo delay tells the bat the target's distance. Different species use different call shapes, constant-frequency calls, frequency-modulated sweeps, or combinations, matched to their hunting style. This record covers biophony, the bat's own calls and echoes, distinct from geophony such as wind and rain, which can clutter echoes, and from anthrophony such as ultrasonic devices, which are human-made.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: classic experiments in which bats with blocked ears failed to avoid obstacles, while bats with open ears succeeded, established echolocation, and modern ultrasonic recording confirms species-typical call frequencies and feeding buzzes.

Math proof

Math proof: by the Nyquist rule, recording a 100 kilohertz bat call faithfully needs a sample rate above 200,000 samples per second; 100,000 x 2 = 200,000.

Source

Bat echolocation literature: obstacle-avoidance experiments and ultrasonic recording studies (as of October 2026).

Wolf Howls: Long-Distance Assembly Calls JAH-AVC-000009 · compounded

Wolves howl to assemble pack members, to advertise territory, and to locate separated packmates, with howls carrying over kilometres in open country. A wolf howl is a long, tonal call whose fundamental frequency lies roughly in the low hundreds of hertz, and a chorus howl, in which several wolves howl together with varied pitches, can sound like more animals than are actually present, which may itself deter rivals. Researchers identify individual wolves partly by howl structure, and playback of a stranger's howl inside a territory typically draws an approach or a counter-howl. This record covers the biophony layer. Wind across a plain is geophony, and distant traffic or machinery is anthrophony, and both shape how far a howl can actually be heard.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: field playback studies show territorial wolves respond to stranger howls with approach or counter-howling, and acoustic analyses show individually distinctive howl features used in monitoring studies.

Math proof

Math proof: two howls at 70 decibels each combine to 10 x log10(10^7 + 10^7) = 73.0 decibels, not 140, because decibels add logarithmically.

Source

Wolf howl field playback and acoustic monitoring studies (as of October 2026).

Prairie Dog Alarm Vocabulary: Describing the Intruder JAH-AVC-000010 · compounded

Gunnison's prairie dogs give alarm calls that vary systematically with the intruder: calls for a coyote differ from calls for a hawk, and calls even vary with features such as the intruder's speed and, in the work of Con Slobodchikoff and colleagues, descriptive features such as the colour or shape of a human intruder. Playback experiments showed that prairie dogs respond differently to different recorded alarm calls even with no intruder visible, running to burrows for some calls and standing alert for others. Whether this counts as a simple vocabulary or as graded variation is debated, but the systematic variation itself is well documented. This record covers biophony only. Prairie wind is geophony, and human voices or vehicles nearby are anthrophony, and the distinction matters because prairie dog colonies live in soundscapes where all three layers mix.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: Slobodchikoff and colleagues' playback studies on Gunnison's prairie dogs documented alarm calls varying with predator type and intruder features, with listeners responding appropriately to playbacks alone.

Math proof

Math proof: if 4 intruder types each get a distinct call type, the number of two-call sequences is 4 x 4 = 16 combinations.

Source

Slobodchikoff et al., prairie dog alarm-call research (as of October 2026).

Dolphin Signature Whistles: Names in Sound JAH-AVC-000011 · compounded

Bottlenose dolphins produce individually distinctive signature whistles, frequency-modulated calls that function somewhat like names: an individual develops its own whistle pattern early in life and uses it in contact and reunion contexts, and other dolphins have been observed copying a companion's signature whistle when addressing it. Researchers identify individuals in the wild partly by recording these whistles, and playback of a dolphin's own signature whistle draws a stronger response than playback of another's. Dolphin whistles generally lie in the range of a few kilohertz up to roughly 20 kilohertz and above, overlapping the upper end of human hearing. This record covers the biophony layer. Ocean waves are geophony, and boat engine noise is anthrophony, and boat noise is a known masker of dolphin whistles.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: long-term studies of bottlenose dolphins, including work by Vincent Janik and colleagues, established signature whistles as individually distinctive and showed vocal copying in addressing behaviour.

Math proof

Math proof: a whistle sweeping from 5,000 to 15,000 hertz covers a bandwidth of 15,000 - 5,000 = 10,000 hertz.

Source

Janik and colleagues, bottlenose dolphin signature-whistle studies (as of October 2026).

Song Learning: How Young Birds Acquire Song JAH-AVC-000012 · complex

Many songbirds must hear adult song during a sensitive period early in life in order to develop normal song: young birds first memorise a tutor's song, then practise in a quiet subsong, and finally crystallise a stable adult song. Birds deafened before the sensitive period, or raised in isolation without a tutor, develop abnormal songs, while birds exposed to a tutor only during that window develop normal species-typical song. The neural machinery for this learning, including brain areas dedicated to song production and perception, is one of the best-studied learning systems in vertebrates. This record covers the biophony layer, the learned songs themselves, distinct from geophony and anthrophony, which are the background sounds a young bird must learn to sing through and, in noisy places, adapt to.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: classic isolation and deafening experiments in songbirds, and tutor-exposure studies, established the sensitive period for song learning and the memorise-practise-crystallise sequence.

Math proof

Math proof: if a tutor sings 200 songs per day for 30 days, the young bird hears 200 x 30 = 6,000 tutor songs in that window.

Source

Songbird song-learning literature: isolation, deafening, and tutor-exposure studies (as of October 2026).

Vocal Dialects: Local Accents in Birds and Whales JAH-AVC-000013 · complex

Just as human speech has regional accents, some animal vocalisations have dialects: populations of the same species in different places sing measurably different versions of the same song. White-crowned sparrows are the classic bird example, with stable song dialects mapped across neighbouring areas, and humpback whale song, though shared within a population, differs between ocean basins. Dialects arise through learning: young animals copy local adults, so local variants persist, and dialect boundaries can reveal how populations are connected or isolated. This record covers the biophony layer. Geophony and anthrophony shape dialect transmission too, because a local soundscape's background noise can favour calls that cut through it, but the dialects themselves are living sounds.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: mapping studies of white-crowned sparrow song dialects, and comparisons of humpback song across ocean basins, document stable geographic variation in learned vocalisations.

Math proof

Math proof: if 5 dialect areas each hold 40 singing males, the total singing population is 5 x 40 = 200 males.

Source

White-crowned sparrow dialect mapping studies; humpback song ocean-basin comparisons (as of October 2026).

Vocal Mimicry and Deception: The Lyrebird's Repertoire JAH-AVC-000014 · complex

Superb lyrebirds of Australia are famous mimics: a male's song can include convincing imitations of other bird species, and also of non-bird sounds heard in its environment, woven into long display songs during courtship. Mimicry can function in mate attraction, with females preferring males with larger or more accurate repertoires, and in some species mimicry also serves deception, as when a bird imitates a predator's alarm call to scatter competitors. Not every sound a lyrebird makes is mimicry, and careful studies separate genuine imitation from species-typical song elements that merely sound similar. This record covers the biophony layer: every mimicked sound, whatever its original source, is a living bird's vocalisation when the lyrebird produces it, while the original environmental sounds belong to their own layers.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: field recordings and spectrogram analyses of superb lyrebirds document imitations of multiple species in single display songs, and playback studies test female preference for repertoire size.

Math proof

Math proof: a repertoire of 12 mimicked species plus 8 own-song elements gives 12 + 8 = 20 repertoire elements in total.

The Lombard Effect: Shouting Over the Noise JAH-AVC-000015 · complex

Animals, like humans, raise their voices in noise: the Lombard effect, first described in humans, is the involuntary increase in vocal loudness, and often pitch and duration, when background noise rises. Songbirds near busy roads sing louder and at higher pitches than the same species in quiet woodland, whales raise call levels in shipping noise, and frogs shift call timing to gaps in chorus noise. The effect has limits: louder calling costs energy and can distort the call, and there is a ceiling beyond which an animal cannot compensate, after which communication simply fails. This record covers the biophony layer, the animal's own adjusted call, while the noise it adjusts to may be geophony, such as wind and surf, or anthrophony, such as traffic, and naming the layer matters for any remedy.

Soundscape layers

biophony

Science proof

Science proof: controlled noise-exposure studies in birds, frogs, and whales consistently show level and pitch increases with rising background noise, matching the human Lombard effect.

Math proof

Math proof: if background noise rises by 10 decibels and a bird compensates by 5 decibels, the signal-to-noise ratio still falls by 10 - 5 = 5 decibels.

Source

Lombard-effect studies in birds, frogs, and whales; human Lombard literature (as of October 2026).
