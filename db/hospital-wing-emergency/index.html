Emergency Severity Index Triage

The Emergency Severity Index sorts patients into five levels, from 1 (needs immediate life-saving intervention) to 5 (stable, needs no resources). Triage nurses assign the level within minutes of arrival using vital signs, symptoms, and expected resource needs. It is the single most important sorting tool in the emergency department.

Trauma Bay Operations

Trauma bays are large resuscitation rooms with overhead lights, wall suction, oxygen, and space for a full trauma team. The most severely injured patients go here for simultaneous assessment by surgery, anesthesia, nursing, and radiology. Every tool needed for airway, breathing, and circulation is within arm's reach.

Code Blue Response

A code blue is called when a patient has no pulse or stops breathing. A team rushes in with a crash cart, starts chest compressions, delivers defibrillator shocks for shockable rhythms, and pushes emergency drugs like epinephrine. Survival depends on starting compressions within seconds.

Crash Cart Contents

A crash cart carries everything for resuscitation: defibrillator, airway tubes, suction, IV supplies, and emergency medications. It is checked and restocked on every shift so nothing is missing in a crisis. Common drugs include epinephrine, amiodarone, atropine, and sodium bicarbonate.

Rapid Sequence Intubation

Rapid sequence intubation places a breathing tube in seconds for patients who cannot protect their airway. The team gives a sedative and a paralytic back to back, then slides the tube past the vocal cords. It is used in trauma, overdose, and severe respiratory failure.

Sepsis Bundle

Sepsis is the body's overwhelming response to infection and kills fast. The bundle means: draw blood cultures, give broad-spectrum antibiotics within one hour, start IV fluids, and measure lactate. Every hour of antibiotic delay raises mortality, so the ED runs this as a timed protocol.

Stroke Code

A stroke code mobilizes the stroke team the moment stroke symptoms are recognized. The patient gets a stat head CT to rule out bleeding, and if eligible, clot-busting tPA is given within 4.5 hours. Many centers can also pull the clot out mechanically up to 24 hours.

STEMI Protocol

A STEMI is a major heart attack shown by ST elevation on the ECG. The protocol's goal is door-to-balloon time under 90 minutes: ED diagnosis, immediate cardiology activation, and direct transfer to the catheterization lab. Aspirin, heparin, and antiplatelet drugs start in the ED.

FAST Ultrasound Exam

The Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma looks for internal bleeding in trauma patients in under two minutes. The probe checks four areas: around the heart, liver, spleen, and bladder. Free fluid means bleeding inside, and the patient may need emergency surgery.

Decontamination Room

Patients exposed to chemicals, radiation, or hazardous materials are decontaminated before entering the main ED. Staff in protective gear rinse the patient with water and remove contaminated clothing. This protects the patient, staff, and the whole department from spread.

Fast Track Unit

Fast Track handles low-acuity patients — sprains, minor cuts, ear infections — with a dedicated provider and nurse. It keeps simple cases moving so they never clog the main treatment bays. Typical door-to-discharge times run under 90 minutes.

Behavioral Health Emergency Care

EDs see patients in psychiatric crisis: suicidal thoughts, psychosis, or severe agitation. They are placed in safe, ligature-resistant rooms with constant observation. Medical causes are ruled out first, then psychiatry evaluates for admission or safe discharge planning.

Pediatric Emergency Zone

Children are not small adults: they need weight-based dosing, smaller equipment, and staff trained in pediatric emergencies. Many EDs have a dedicated pediatric area with child life specialists who reduce fear. The Broselow tape gives instant drug doses by the child's length.

Disaster Surge Plan

When mass casualties arrive, the ED switches to surge mode: triage moves outside, elective areas become treatment space, and staff are recalled. Patients are sorted by survivability so resources save the most lives. Drills keep this plan ready at all times.

Ambulance Bay and EMS Handoff

Ambulances arrive at a dedicated bay where paramedics give a structured handoff: age, mechanism, injuries, vitals, treatment. This SBAR-style report lets the ED team act in seconds. Pre-arrival radio alerts let trauma teams assemble before the patient rolls in.

Observation Unit

The observation unit holds patients for 12 to 24 hours when the ED team needs more time to decide. Chest pain, mild asthma, and dehydration are common observation diagnoses. It prevents unnecessary full admissions while keeping patients safe under monitoring.

EMTALA Duty

The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act requires every ED to screen and stabilize anyone who comes in, regardless of ability to pay. No patient can be turned away or transferred until stable. It is the legal backbone of emergency care access.

Point-of-Care Testing

Bedside analyzers run troponin, lactate, blood gases, and pregnancy tests in minutes instead of sending samples to the central lab. Faster results mean faster decisions on heart attacks, sepsis, and trauma. Quality control checks keep bedside results as reliable as lab results.
