Danish Oil Rank 1 · simple · oil · beginner

Danish oil is a blend of oil and varnish that wipes on easily and gives wood a warm, natural, low-sheen look while adding more protection than pure oil alone. It soaks into the grain on the first coat and builds a thin film on later coats, which makes it forgiving for beginners and easy to repair: a worn spot is simply wiped with another coat. Danish oil suits tabletops that see light use, shelves, and boxes, but it is not as waterproof as polyurethane and needs re-oiling every year or two on hard-used surfaces.

Specifications

Coverage about 400 square feet per gallon per coat; coats 2 to 3; dry time 4 to 6 hours between coats; full cure about 72 hours

  • Coverage: about 400 sq ft per gallon per coat
  • Coats: 2 to 3
  • Dry between coats: 4 to 6 hours
  • Full cure: about 72 hours
  • Sheen: satin to low gloss
  • Application: wipe on with a cloth, wipe off all excess after 15 minutes

Materials:

  • Test board (pine or the project wood, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the finish guide itself

Tolerances: Finish even with no runs, drips, or dry spots; sheen uniform across the surface

Arithmetic note: Coverage checks out: area divided by coverage per gallon, times the coat count. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Sand the wood to 220-grit and remove all dust with a tack cloth.
  2. Flood the first coat on with a cloth and let it soak for 15 minutes.
  3. Wipe off every trace of excess oil so no puddles remain.
  4. Let the coat dry 4 to 6 hours, then scuff lightly with 320-grit.
  5. Apply a second coat the same way, and a third if the wood still drinks it in.
  6. Let the finish cure 72 hours before heavy use.

Tools required

  • Tape measure
  • Clean lint-free cloths
  • Brush or applicator as the finish requires
  • 320-grit sandpaper
  • Tack cloth
  • Stir stick

Safety gear:

  • Safety glasses
  • Respirator for spraying or sanding finish
  • Nitrile gloves

Safety notes: Work in a well-ventilated area and wear a respirator when spraying or sanding finish. Oil-soaked rags can catch fire by spontaneous combustion: lay used rags flat outdoors to dry, or store them fully submerged in water in a sealed metal container, and never pile oil-soaked rags in a bin or on a bench.

Tung Oil Rank 2 · simple · oil · intermediate

Tung oil is pressed from the seeds of the tung tree and gives a hard, water-resistant, matte finish that is prized for fine furniture and wooden boats. Pure tung oil needs many thin coats, often five or more, and each coat needs a full day to cure, so it rewards patience with a deep, natural look that never looks plastic. Tung oil must be pure, not a blend labeled tung oil finish, if the full water resistance is wanted, and it should be thinned with citrus solvent or mineral spirits for the first coats so it soaks in.

Specifications

Coverage about 400 square feet per gallon per coat; coats 5 to 6 for pure oil; dry time 24 hours between coats; full cure 2 to 4 weeks

  • Coverage: about 400 sq ft per gallon per coat
  • Coats: 5 to 6 for pure tung oil
  • Dry between coats: 24 hours
  • Full cure: 2 to 4 weeks
  • Sheen: matte to satin
  • Thinning: first coats thinned 1 to 1 with citrus solvent

Materials:

  • Test board (pine or the project wood, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the finish guide itself

Tolerances: Finish even with no runs, drips, or dry spots; sheen uniform across the surface

How to

  1. Sand to 220-grit and remove all dust.
  2. Thin the first coat 1 to 1 with citrus solvent and wipe it on liberally.
  3. Wipe off all excess after 30 minutes and let it dry 24 hours.
  4. Repeat thin coats, wiping off excess each time, until the surface stops absorbing.
  5. Scuff lightly with 320-grit between the last coats.
  6. Let the finish cure for at least 2 weeks before heavy use.

Linseed Oil Rank 3 · simple · oil · beginner

Linseed oil is pressed from flaxseed and is the oldest wood finish in common use, giving a warm amber tone that deepens with age. Boiled linseed oil, which contains drying agents, cures in about a day per coat, while raw linseed oil takes much longer and is used mainly where a very slow cure is acceptable. Linseed oil offers only modest protection against water and wear, so it suits tool handles, workbench tops, and rustic pieces that are easy to re-oil, and it is never used alone on a kitchen table that sees spills.

Shellac Rank 4 · simple · spirit · intermediate

Shellac is a natural resin secreted by the lac insect, dissolved in alcohol, and it is the classic French-polish finish: warm, clear, and fast-drying, with a depth that synthetic finishes struggle to match. Shellac dries in under an hour, seals in odors and stains, and is easily repaired, since a new coat melts into the old one. Its weakness is water and alcohol: a wet glass leaves a white ring and spilled spirits dissolve it, so shellac suits display furniture, boxes, and musical instruments rather than kitchen tables. Shellac is sold by cut, meaning pounds of resin per gallon of alcohol, with a 2-pound cut as the common working strength.

Specifications

Coverage about 500 square feet per gallon per coat; coats 3 to 5; dry time 30 to 60 minutes between coats; full cure about 24 hours; 2-pound cut

  • Coverage: about 500 sq ft per gallon per coat
  • Coats: 3 to 5
  • Dry between coats: 0.5 to 1 hour
  • Full cure: about 24 hours
  • Cut: 2 lb per gallon (common working strength)
  • Solvent: denatured alcohol

Materials:

  • Test board (pine or the project wood, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the finish guide itself

Tolerances: Finish even with no runs, drips, or dry spots; sheen uniform across the surface

Arithmetic note: Dry schedule checks out: coats times hours per coat, plus the stated cure period. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Sand to 220-grit and remove all dust.
  2. Seal the surface with a thin 1-pound cut of shellac and let it dry 30 minutes.
  3. Pad or brush on the 2-pound cut in thin, even coats.
  4. Let each coat dry 30 to 60 minutes and scuff lightly with 320-grit between coats.
  5. Build 3 to 5 coats until the surface is even and full.
  6. Let the finish cure 24 hours, then wax lightly if desired.

Polyurethane (Wipe-On) Rank 5 · simple · polyurethane · beginner

Wipe-on polyurethane is ordinary polyurethane thinned to wiping consistency, and it is the easiest way for a beginner to get a durable, even film finish without brush marks or drips. Each coat is thin, so four to six coats are normal, but each coat wipes on in minutes and dries in a few hours. Wipe-on polyurethane gives the same water and wear resistance as brush-on polyurethane once enough coats are built, and it suits tabletops, shelves, and boxes. Work in a dust-free space, since dust in a wet coat is the main enemy of a smooth result.

  • Application: wipe on with a lint-free cloth, thin coats only

  4. Wipe off the dust and apply the next coat the same way.
  5. Build 4 to 6 coats until the film looks even.
  6. Let the finish cure for 30 days before placing heavy objects on it.

Polyurethane (Brush-On) Rank 6 · compounded · polyurethane · intermediate

Brush-on polyurethane builds a thick, tough film in two to three coats and is the standard durable finish for floors, tabletops, and kitchen cabinets. It is applied with a high-quality synthetic brush in long, even strokes, and it must not be over-brushed, because working the finish after it starts to tack leaves brush marks and bubbles. Oil-based polyurethane ambers over time and smells strongly while curing, while water-based polyurethane stays clear, dries faster, and cleans up with water. Either way, the surface must be dust-free and the coats thin, since thick coats trap bubbles and take far longer to cure.

Specifications

Coverage about 400 square feet per gallon per coat; coats 2 to 3; dry time 4 to 6 hours (oil-based) or 2 hours (water-based) between coats; full cure 30 days

  • Coverage: about 400 sq ft per gallon per coat
  • Coats: 2 to 3
  • Dry between coats: 4 to 6 hours oil-based, 2 hours water-based
  • Full cure: about 30 days
  • Sheen: satin, semi-gloss, or gloss
  • Brush: high-quality synthetic bristle, 2 to 3 inches wide

Materials:

  • Test board (pine or the project wood, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the finish guide itself

Tolerances: Finish even with no runs, drips, or dry spots; sheen uniform across the surface

Arithmetic note: Dry schedule checks out: coats times hours per coat, plus the stated cure period. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Sand to 220-grit and remove all dust with a tack cloth.
  2. Stir the polyurethane gently, never shaking it, to avoid bubbles.
  3. Brush on a thin coat with long strokes along the grain and do not over-brush.
  4. Let the coat dry fully, then scuff with 320-grit and remove the dust.
  5. Apply the second coat the same way, and a third for floors and tabletops.
  6. Let the finish cure 30 days before heavy use.

Tools required

  • Brush or applicator as the finish requires

Lacquer Rank 7 · compounded · spray · advanced

Lacquer is a fast-drying finish sprayed in thin coats that melt into each other, giving a hard, clear, professional-looking film that is the standard finish for production furniture and musical instruments. Lacquer dries to the touch in about 30 minutes and can be recoated without sanding between coats, but it must be sprayed, since brushing lacquer leaves lap marks, and spraying demands a respirator, ventilation, and a spray area away from ignition sources, because lacquer fumes are flammable. Lacquer is damaged by some solvents and by prolonged water contact, so it suits display furniture and instruments rather than wet environments.

Specifications

Coverage about 500 square feet per gallon per coat sprayed; coats 4 to 6; dry to touch 30 minutes; recoat any time after 30 minutes; full cure 7 to 14 days

  • Coverage: about 500 sq ft per gallon per coat sprayed
  • Coats: 4 to 6
  • Dry to touch: 0.5 hour
  • Recoat: after 30 minutes, no sanding needed between coats
  • Full cure: 7 to 14 days
  • Application: spray only; flammable fumes, respirator and ventilation required

Materials:

  • Test board (pine or the project wood, 3/4 inch)

Fasteners: No fasteners in the finish guide itself

Tolerances: Finish even with no runs, drips, or dry spots; sheen uniform across the surface

Arithmetic note: Coverage checks out: area divided by coverage per gallon, times the coat count. Demo output below was captured by executing the demo code at build time.

How to

  1. Sand to 220-grit, seal with a thin lacquer sanding sealer, and let it dry 30 minutes.
  2. Put on a respirator, ventilate the spray area, and remove ignition sources.
  3. Spray thin, even coats from 8 to 10 inches away, overlapping each pass by half.
  4. Let each coat flash off for 30 minutes before the next.
  5. Build 4 to 6 coats, then let the finish cure for a week.
  6. Rub out the cured finish with 0000 steel wool and wax for a satin sheen if desired.

Varnish Rank 8 · compounded · film · intermediate

Varnish is a traditional resin-and-oil finish that builds a hard, glossy, water-resistant film, and it remains the right choice for boats, bar tops, and any surface that sees standing water. Spar varnish, the outdoor kind, stays flexible so it moves with the wood outdoors instead of cracking, while interior varnish dries harder and is used for floors and furniture. Varnish is brushed on in thin coats and needs a dust-free space and long drying times, often a full day between coats, so it is slower than polyurethane but more forgiving on a surface that must flex.

Wood Stain Rank 9 · compounded · color · beginner

Wood stain is a colorant, not a protective finish on its own: it colors the wood and must be sealed with oil, shellac, or polyurethane afterward, or the color will wear away and stain hands and cloth. Oil stains give even color and long open time, gel stains sit on the surface and suit blotch-prone woods like pine and cherry, and water-based stains dry fast and raise the grain slightly. Stain color always depends on the wood species, so a test board from the same wood is essential before staining the real piece. Stain rags carry the same fire risk as oil rags when the stain is oil-based.

Specifications

Coverage about 400 square feet per gallon per coat; coats 1 to 2; dry time 4 to 8 hours before topcoat; stain alone gives no protection

  • Coats: 1, a second coat deepens color only slightly
  • Dry before topcoat: 4 to 8 hours
  • Types: oil stain, gel stain, water-based stain

Materials:

Whitewash Rank 10 · compounded · color · beginner

Whitewash is a thinned white paint or lime wash that lightens wood while letting the grain show through, giving a coastal, rustic look that suits pine, cedar, and reclaimed boards. The classic mix is white latex paint thinned about half and half with water, wiped on and wiped off until the desired lightness is reached. Whitewash must be sealed with a clear topcoat, usually matte polyurethane or wax, or it will rub off and pick up dirt. Whitewash is a color treatment, not a protective finish, and it works best on open-grained woods where the grain pattern stays visible.

Specifications

Hardwax Oil Rank 14 · complex · oil · intermediate

Epoxy Topcoat Basics Rank 15 · complex · resin · advanced

Epoxy topcoat is a two-part resin that cures to a thick, glass-clear, highly waterproof film, and it is used for river tables, bar tops, and countertops where a deep, glossy, waterproof surface is wanted. Epoxy is mixed by exact ratio, usually 1 to 1 or 2 to 1 by volume depending on the product, poured in thin layers to avoid trapped heat and bubbles, and it must be applied in a dust-free space, since every dust speck is trapped forever in the clear film. Epoxy resin and hardener are skin sensitizers, so gloves are mandatory, ventilation is required, and uncured epoxy is never poured down a drain. Epoxy yellows slowly in sunlight unless a UV-stable product is used.

Outdoor Spar Finish Rank 16 · complex · film · intermediate

Outdoor spar finish, also called spar varnish or marine varnish, is a flexible, UV-resistant varnish made for wood that lives outdoors, where sun and rain would crack a hard indoor finish. It contains UV absorbers and stays elastic so it moves with the wood through wet and dry seasons, and it needs recoating every one to two years outdoors, since even the best spar finish wears under sun. Spar finish is brushed on in thin coats, needs a dust-free drying space, and must be applied over dry wood, since trapped moisture under a spar film causes peeling. Spar finish is never used indoors as a substitute for polyurethane, since it stays softer and cures slower.
