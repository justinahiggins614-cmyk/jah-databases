What Meteorology Is

Meteorology is the science of the atmosphere: the air, the weather, and the climate, and how they change. The meteorologist studies the temperature, the pressure, the wind, and the rain, and they forecast the weather, so the people know what is coming. Meteorology is the science of the sky: the clouds, the storms, and the seasons, and the physics that makes them.

The atmosphere is about 100 kilometres thick, and most of the weather is in the lowest 10 kilometres. The temperature falls by about 6 degrees for every kilometre of height: at 5 kilometres, it is about 30 degrees colder than at the ground. The pressure at sea level is about 1,013 hectopascals, and it falls with height: at 5 kilometres, it is about half. A forecast is made for 1, 3, and 7 days ahead, and the 1-day forecast is the most accurate. Meteorology is the science of the sky, and it is 2,000 years old, from Aristotle to the satellite.

The Atmosphere

The atmosphere is the air around the Earth: the layer of gases, held by gravity, that we breathe, and in which the weather happens. The atmosphere is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, with the water vapour, the carbon dioxide, and the dust. The atmosphere has layers: the troposphere, where the weather is, the stratosphere, with the ozone, and above them, the thinner layers, to space.

The atmosphere is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, and 1 percent other gases: 78 plus 21 plus 1 is 100. The troposphere is about 10 kilometres thick, and the stratosphere is about 40 kilometres thick, from 10 to 50. The temperature falls in the troposphere, by about 6 degrees per kilometre, and it rises in the stratosphere, because of the ozone. The pressure at sea level is about 1,013 hectopascals, and at 10 kilometres it is about 265: about a quarter. The atmosphere is the air we breathe, and the weather we live in.

Temperature

Temperature is the measure of how hot or cold the air is: the average energy of the air molecules, measured in degrees. The temperature is measured by the thermometer, in Celsius or Fahrenheit, and it is the most familiar weather measurement. Temperature decides the weather: the warm air rises, and the cold air sinks, and the difference drives the wind, the cloud, and the storm.

Water freezes at 0 Celsius, which is 32 Fahrenheit, and it boils at 100 Celsius, which is 212 Fahrenheit. The conversion is Fahrenheit equals Celsius times 9 over 5, plus 32: 20 Celsius is 68 Fahrenheit, and 30 Celsius is 86 Fahrenheit. The temperature falls by about 6 degrees per kilometre of height: at 5 kilometres, it is about 30 degrees colder. The hottest place on Earth is about 58 Celsius, and the coldest is about minus 89: the range is 147 degrees. The daily range is the maximum minus the minimum: 25 minus 15 is 10 degrees.

Air Pressure

Air pressure is the weight of the air above: the force of the atmosphere pressing down, measured in hectopascals. The pressure is high where the air is sinking, and low where it is rising, and the wind blows from the high to the low. Air pressure is the driver of the weather: the high brings the fair weather, and the low brings the cloud and the rain.

The sea-level pressure is about 1,013 hectopascals, and it falls with height: at 5 kilometres, it is about half, or 540. The isobars are drawn every 4 hectopascals: 1,000, 1,004, 1,008, and so on. A high of 1,030 is 17 above the average, and a low of 980 is 33 below: the difference is 50 hectopascals. The wind blows from the high to the low, and the closer the isobars, the stronger the wind. Torricelli invented the barometer in 1643, and the pressure is 383 years old as a measurement in 2026.

Wind

Wind is the moving air: the flow of the atmosphere from the high pressure to the low, driven by the difference in pressure, and turned by the Earth's rotation. Wind is measured by its speed and its direction: the speed in kilometres per hour, and the direction from which it blows. Wind is the mover of the weather: it carries the cloud, the rain, and the warmth, and it makes the storm.

The wind blows from the high to the low: a difference of 50 hectopascals over 1,000 kilometres makes a strong wind. The jet stream flows at about 200 kilometres per hour, and a plane flying with it saves hours: a flight of 5,000 kilometres at 800 kilometres per hour takes about 6 hours, and with a 200 tailwind, it takes about 5. The Beaufort scale has 13 forces, from 0 to 12: force 0 is the calm, and force 12 is the hurricane, at over 118 kilometres per hour. A wind of 100 kilometres per hour is about 28 metres per second, and a wind of 36 kilometres per hour is 10 metres per second.

Humidity and Dew Point

Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air: the invisible water, which makes the air feel damp, and which makes the cloud and the rain. The dew point is the temperature at which the air becomes saturated, and the vapour condenses into the dew, the fog, or the cloud. Humidity and dew point are the measure of the wetness of the air, and they decide the comfort, the fog, and the rain.

The air at 20 degrees can hold about 17 grams of water per cubic metre, and at 30 degrees, about 30 grams: the warmer air holds more. If the air holds 17 grams at 20 degrees, the relative humidity is 100 percent, and the air is saturated. If it holds 8.5 grams, the relative humidity is 50 percent. The dew point is the temperature at which the air saturates: if the temperature is 20 and the dew point is 10, the air is dry, and if the dew point is 19, the air is wet, and the fog is near. The fog forms when the temperature falls to the dew point, in the night.

Clouds

A cloud is the visible water in the air: the tiny droplets, or the ice crystals, floating in the sky, made when the moist air rises and cools, and the vapour condenses. Clouds are the sign of the weather: the cirrus means the change, the cumulus means the fair, and the cumulonimbus means the storm. Clouds are the sky's writing, and the meteorologist reads them.

Clouds are classified by height: the low clouds are below 2 kilometres, the middle from 2 to 7, and the high above 7. The cirrus is at about 10 kilometres, and it is made of ice, at minus 40 degrees. The cumulonimbus towers to about 12 kilometres, and it brings the storm. The cloud amount is measured in oktas, the eighths of the sky: 0 is clear, and 8 is overcast, and 4 is half the sky. Luke Howard classified the clouds in 1803, and the classification is 223 years old in 2026.

Precipitation

Precipitation is the water that falls from the sky: the rain, the snow, the sleet, and the hail, which fall from the cloud to the ground. Precipitation is the water of the weather: it fills the rivers, waters the crops, and makes the floods. Precipitation is measured by the rain gauge, in millimetres, and it is the most important weather measurement after the temperature.

One millimetre of rain is 1 litre per square metre: a garden of 100 square metres gets 100 litres from 1 millimetre. A heavy rain falls at 10 millimetres per hour, and a light rain at 1: the heavy is 10 times the light. The wettest place on Earth gets about 12,000 millimetres a year, and the driest gets less than 1: the wettest gets 12,000 times as much. A snow of 10 millimetres of rain is about 100 millimetres of snow: the snow is 10 times as deep. The rain gauge is the measure: the millimetres, every day, and the year is added up.

Thunderstorms

A thunderstorm is the storm of the thunder and the lightning: the tall cumulonimbus cloud, which rises, and brings the heavy rain, the hail, and the wind. The thunderstorm is made by the warm, moist air rising, and the cold air above, and the lightning is the spark between the charges. Thunderstorms are the most common storm: they happen every day, somewhere on Earth, and they are the weather's drama.

Lightning strikes the Earth about 100 times a second, which is 8,640,000 times a day. The thunder travels at about 343 metres per second, so the flash 3 seconds before the thunder is about 1 kilometre away, and 9 seconds is about 3 kilometres. The cumulonimbus towers to about 12 kilometres, and the rain falls at 10 millimetres per hour, or more. Franklin showed lightning is electricity in 1752, and it is 274 years ago in 2026. The thunderstorm is the most common storm: it happens every day, somewhere, and the warning is given, hours ahead.

Tornadoes

A tornado is the spinning column of air: the funnel from the cloud to the ground, which destroys what it touches, in minutes. The tornado is the most violent wind on Earth: the winds reach 500 kilometres per hour, and the damage is the path. Tornadoes are the storm of the plain: they form in the supercell, and they last minutes, and they are the weather's terror.

A tornado lasts about 10 minutes, and it travels about 10 kilometres, at about 60 kilometres per hour. The winds reach 500 kilometres per hour in the strongest, and 100 in the weakest: the strongest is 5 times the weakest. The Fujita scale has 6 levels, from F0 to F5, and the F5 is the strongest, with winds over 400 kilometres per hour. The United States has about 1,200 tornadoes a year, which is about 3 a day. The warning is given minutes ahead, and the people shelter: the basement, or the safe room, away from the windows.

Hurricanes and Cyclones

A hurricane is the great tropical storm: the rotating system of cloud and rain, hundreds of kilometres wide, with winds over 118 kilometres per hour, which forms over the warm sea. The hurricane is called the hurricane in the Atlantic, the typhoon in the Pacific, and the cyclone in the Indian Ocean: the same storm, with different names. Hurricanes are the greatest storm on Earth: they bring the wind, the rain, and the surge, and the damage is the coast.

A hurricane forms over water warmer than 26 degrees, and it is hundreds of kilometres wide: a typical hurricane is about 500 kilometres wide. The Saffir-Simpson scale has 5 categories: category 1 has winds of 119 to 153 kilometres per hour, and category 5 has winds over 250. The eye is about 50 kilometres wide, and the eyewall has the strongest wind. A hurricane moves at about 20 kilometres per hour, so in 24 hours it moves about 480 kilometres. The hurricane is forecast days ahead, and the evacuation is the life saved.

Fronts and Air Masses

A front is the boundary between two air masses: the warm and the cold, which meet, and the weather changes. An air mass is a large body of air, with the same temperature and humidity, formed over the sea or the land. Fronts and air masses are the makers of the mid-latitude weather: the warm front brings the steady rain, and the cold front brings the showers and the storms.

A warm front moves at about 20 kilometres per hour, and a cold front at about 30: the cold front is faster, and it catches the warm front, and the occlusion forms. The warm front brings the rain for about 12 hours, and the cold front for about 2: the warm rain is steady, and the cold rain is sharp. An air mass is about 1,000 kilometres wide, and it forms over the sea or the land, in days. Bjerknes found the fronts in the 1920s, and the front is about 100 years old as a concept in 2026. The front is the boundary, and the weather changes when it passes.

Weather Maps

A weather map is the picture of the weather: the isobars, the fronts, and the symbols, drawn on the map of the country, showing the pressure, the wind, and the rain. The weather map is the meteorologist's picture: it shows the weather now, and the forecast is the map of the weather to come. Weather maps are the language of the forecast: the reader who knows the symbols can read the weather.

A weather map has about 100 stations, and each station model shows the temperature, the wind, and the cloud. The isobars are drawn every 4 hectopascals: 1,000, 1,004, 1,008, and so on, and a map with 10 isobars covers 40 hectopascals. The warm front is drawn in red, with the semicircles, and the cold front in blue, with the triangles. The wind barb shows the speed: a full barb is 10 knots, and a half barb is 5, so 2 full barbs and 1 half is 25 knots. The map is updated every hour, and the forecast map is drawn for 12, 24, and 48 hours ahead.

Weather Forecasting

Weather forecasting is the prediction of the weather: the temperature, the wind, and the rain, for the hours and days ahead, made from the observations, the maps, and the computer models. The forecast is the product of meteorology: it is what the people use, every day, to decide what to wear, and what to do. Weather forecasting is the science of the future weather, and it is the most used science in the world.

The 1-day forecast is right about 90 percent of the time, and the 7-day forecast about 70 percent: the skill falls with the days. The nowcast is for the next 6 hours, and it is made from the radar and the satellite. The ensemble runs the model 50 times, with slightly different starts: if 45 of the 50 agree, the confidence is high, and if 25 agree, it is low. FitzRoy made the first forecasts in the 1860s, and forecasting is about 160 years old in 2026. The forecast is the product: the people use it, every day, to decide.

Numerical Weather Prediction

Numerical weather prediction is the forecasting by the computer: the atmosphere is divided into boxes, and the physics is calculated, in each box, for each step, and the future weather is predicted. The model is the mathematics of the atmosphere: the equations of the motion, the heat, and the moisture, solved by the supercomputer. Numerical weather prediction is the forecast of the modern age, and it is the most powerful tool in meteorology.

Richardson took weeks to predict 6 hours by hand, in 1922, and the computer does it in minutes today: the model is 1,000 times faster. The model is run 4 times a day, and the forecast is made for 10 days ahead: 4 runs a day, and 40 forecasts a day, for the planet. The grid boxes are about 10 kilometres wide, and the atmosphere is divided into 100 layers: the model calculates in millions of boxes. The ensemble runs 50 times, and the spread shows the uncertainty: if 45 agree, the confidence is high.

Satellites

Weather satellites are the eyes of meteorology in space: they photograph the clouds, measure the temperature, and watch the storms, from above, for the whole planet. The satellite sees what the ground cannot: the hurricane over the ocean, the cloud over the desert, and the weather of the whole Earth, at once. Satellites are the greatest advance in meteorology: they made the global forecast possible, and they save lives, by the warning.

TIROS-1 was launched in 1960, and the satellite age is 66 years old in 2026. The geostationary satellite is at 36,000 kilometres, and it watches every 15 minutes, so 96 images a day. The polar satellite orbits twice a day, and it measures the temperature, and the ice. The satellite sees the hurricane days before the landfall: the storm is tracked, and the coast is warned, and the people evacuate. The satellite is the eye: the whole planet is seen, at once, and the forecast is global.

Radar

Weather radar is the eye of meteorology on the ground: it sends the radio waves, and they bounce off the rain, and the radar sees the rain, the storm, and the tornado, in real time. The radar sees what the eye cannot: the rain over the hills, the storm at night, and the rotation of the tornado. Radar is the warning tool: it sees the storm, and the warning is given, minutes ahead, and the people shelter.

The radar sees about 250 kilometres, and it scans every 5 minutes, so 12 scans an hour, and 288 a day. The rain is shown by the reflectivity: the light rain is green, and the heavy rain is red, and the hail is purple. The Doppler radar sees the rotation of the tornado, minutes before it touches down, and the warning is given. The radar network covers the whole country, with about 20 radars, each seeing 250 kilometres. The nowcast is for the next 6 hours, from the radar and the satellite, and the rain is predicted, minute by minute.

Climate and Weather

Climate and weather are the two timescales of the atmosphere: the weather is the day, and the climate is the 30 years. The weather changes daily, and the climate changes slowly, over decades, and the climate is the average of the weather. Climate and weather are the short and the long: the rain today is the weather, and the rain of 30 years is the climate.

The climate is the average over 30 years: the temperature of 30 Januaries, added and divided by 30, is the January normal. The Earth has warmed by about 1 degree since the 19th century, and the rain has changed: the wet places are wetter, and the dry places are drier. A day 5 degrees above the normal is the warm anomaly, and a day 5 below is the cold anomaly. The climate zones are the desert, the rainforest, and the tundra, each with its average. The weather is the day, and the climate is the 30 years, and the change is the warming.

Severe Weather Warnings

Severe weather warnings are the alerts for the dangerous weather: the storm, the flood, the heat, and the cold, which threaten life and property. The warning is the meteorologist's duty: it is given before the weather arrives, so the people can prepare, shelter, and survive. Severe weather warnings are the reason meteorology matters most: the warning saves lives, and the forecast is the protection.

The warning has 3 levels: yellow, amber, and red, and the red is the highest, and the rarest. FitzRoy gave the storm warnings in the 1860s, and the warning is about 160 years old in 2026. The tornado warning is given minutes ahead, and the hurricane warning days ahead: the tornado is minutes, and the hurricane is days. The flood warning is given hours ahead, and the heat warning days ahead. The alert is sent to the phone, and the people are warned: the warning saves lives, and the forecast is the protection.

The Water Cycle

The water cycle is the movement of water around the Earth: from the sea, to the air, to the land, and back to the sea, by the evaporation, the condensation, and the rain. The water cycle is the circle of the weather: the sun evaporates the sea, the vapour becomes the cloud, and the rain falls on the land, and the rivers return it to the sea. The water cycle is the source of our water: the rain, the rivers, and the groundwater.

The water cycle moves about 500,000 cubic kilometres of water a year, from the sea to the air. The ocean holds about 97 percent of the water, and the freshwater is about 3 percent, and the glaciers hold about 2 percent. The rain falls about 1,000 millimetres a year, on average, over the land, and the evaporation takes it back. A river flows to the sea: the Amazon carries about 200,000 cubic metres a second, and it is the largest river. Perrault measured the cycle in the 17th century, and the cycle is 350 years old as a measurement in 2026.

Seasons

Seasons are the four parts of the year: the spring, the summer, the autumn, and the winter, made by the tilt of the Earth, and its journey around the Sun. The seasons are the rhythm of the weather: the warmth of the summer, and the cold of the winter, and the change between them. Seasons are the year, divided by the Sun, and they are the oldest calendar of all.

The Earth is tilted 23.5 degrees, and the year is 365 days: the seasons are 4, and each is about 3 months, or about 91 days. The summer solstice is about 21 June, and the winter solstice about 21 December, 6 months apart. The equinoxes are about 21 March and 21 September, and the day and the night are 12 hours each. The longest day in Britain is about 16 hours, and the shortest about 8: the difference is 8 hours. The seasons are the oldest calendar: the farmer plants in the spring, and harvests in the autumn.

El Niño and La Niña

El Niño and La Niña are the warm and the cold phases of the Pacific: the sea warms, and the weather changes, around the world, and the rain, the drought, and the storm follow. El Niño is the warming, and La Niña is the cooling, and they alternate, every few years, and they are the greatest natural drivers of the year's weather. El Niño and La Niña are the Pacific's rhythm, and the world's weather follows it.

El Niño and La Niña alternate every 2 to 7 years, and each lasts about 1 year: in 10 years, there are about 2 or 3 events. The sea warms by about 2 degrees in a strong El Niño, and it cools by about 2 degrees in a strong La Niña. The strong El Niños of 1982 and 1997 were 15 years apart, and they brought the floods and the droughts around the world. The trade winds weaken in El Niño, and the warm water spreads east, and the rain follows it. The forecast is months ahead: the sea is watched, and the phase is predicted, and the weather follows.

A History of Meteorology

Fahrenheit made his scale in 1714, 71 years after Torricelli, and FitzRoy made the first forecasts in the 1860s, about 150 years after Fahrenheit.
