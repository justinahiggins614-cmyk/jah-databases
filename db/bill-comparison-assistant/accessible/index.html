  1. A Bill Total Worked Out in Plain Steps

    Record 1 of 24. simple. Feasibility: buildable-now. The design uses only web pages, forms, and counters that exist today; only the design work stands in the way.

    The bill comparison assistant reads household bills side by side in plain words, shows where the money goes, and estimates what a different plan could cost. All figures are educational estimates, never financial advice, and no real account data is ever used. A bill total worked out in plain steps lets a household see where the money goes: the power bill, the water bill, and the phone bill each on their own line, then the total at the end. The steps must be plain, because a total with no steps is a number nobody can check. Without the steps, the household must trust a single figure, and a single figure hides a mistake that plain steps would show. This record states the design in plain sentences so a reader can judge it on its own merits, and its demo checks the arithmetic behind the design. Worked example: in a website built this way, a bill total worked out in plain steps runs as a small, complete feature a visitor can try in one sitting, with the fair-play and moderation rules of this database applied throughout.

    History: Households have always compared bills on a kitchen table, lining up the power bill, the water bill, and the phone bill to see where the money goes. The website form grows out of that habit, adding a side-by-side view anyone can read and an estimate that shows what a change could be worth. Worked totals are an old habit of household books, where every figure was written on its own line so the sum could be checked twice.

    Proposed solution: Name the bill review and what a visitor does in it; Write the rules in plain sentences a new visitor can follow; Give every bill review the same fair start: the same clues, the same time, the same scoring; Let a visitor try one bill review without signing in; Check the bill review works the same way on a phone and on a computer.

    Dependencies: What must exist first: a bill entry form, a side-by-side comparison view, an estimate calculator, and a plain-words explanation of every figure. Nothing else is needed to open the first bill review.

Without an index, the simple records that teach the design are buried beside the complex records that assume it, and a visitor who wants one idea must read the whole database to find it. The index solves this by listing every record in ladder order, with its place in the ladder, so a reader can start at the beginning and climb, or jump straight to the record they need. Worked example: a reader who wants the simplest starting point opens record 1, A Bill Total Worked Out in Plain Steps, and a reader who wants the hardest problem opens record 23, The Bill Comparison Championship Format.

    History: Households have always compared bills on a kitchen table, lining up the power bill, the water bill, and the phone bill to see where the money goes. The website form grows out of that habit, adding a side-by-side view anyone can read and an estimate that shows what a change could be worth. Field guides and indexes are as old as books themselves: a table of contents that tells a reader where everything lives. This index follows that habit, listing the twenty-three content records of this database in the order a reader should meet them.
