JAH-R20-CERN-000001 — CERN — the organization

CERN, the European Organization for Nuclear Research, is one of the world's largest and most respected centres for scientific research. Founded in 1954 by twelve founding member states, it sits across the Franco-Swiss border near Geneva, with its main site in Meyrin, Switzerland, and facilities extending into Saint-Genis-Pouilly, France. As of 2026 it has 23 member states. Its founding convention established it as a place where scientists of many nations could work together on fundamental physics, and CERN has operated in that spirit ever since: results are published openly and shared with the world.

CERN's mission is to find out what the universe is made of and how it works. Its tool for doing that is particle physics: accelerating particles to nearly the speed of light and smashing them together so that physicists can study what comes out of the collisions. The laboratory operates a chain of accelerators, the largest of which is the Large Hadron Collider. CERN also runs a broad program beyond colliders: antimatter research, neutrino physics, fixed-target experiments, detector research and development, and a computing grid that processes collision data worldwide.

CERN is also famous for things beyond physics. The World Wide Web was invented at CERN in 1989-1990 by Tim Berners-Lee, working with Robert Cailliau, as a way for physicists to share information. CERN released the Web into the public domain in 1993, free for everyone, forever. The laboratory's open-science ethos continues today: CERN's data from the Large Hadron Collider is released through the CERN Open Data portal for anyone to analyze.

JAH-R20-CERN-000002 — The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) — machine facts

The Large Hadron Collider is the world's largest and most powerful particle accelerator. It is a 27-kilometre ring of superconducting magnets buried up to 175 metres beneath the Franco-Swiss border, reusing the tunnel built for its predecessor, the Large Electron-Positron Collider. Inside the ring, two high-energy particle beams travel at close to the speed of light in opposite directions before they are made to collide at four points around the ring, where the big experiments sit.

Key public machine figures: the ring contains 1,232 dipole magnets, each 15 metres long, which bend the beams around the ring with magnetic fields of up to 8.33 tesla — about 100,000 times the Earth's magnetic field. The magnets are cooled with superfluid helium to 1.9 kelvin, colder than outer space. Protons circulate the ring about 11,245 times every second. In Run 3, which began in 2022, the LHC collides protons at a centre-of-mass energy of 13.6 teraelectronvolts (TeV) — 6.8 TeV per beam — the highest collision energies ever produced by humanity. The machine also collides lead ions, and in 2023 it set a record lead-lead luminosity.

The LHC's beam is astonishingly intense and precise: each beam contains about 2,800 bunches of roughly 100 billion protons, and at the collision points the beams are squeezed to a width of about 16 micrometres — roughly a quarter of a human hair — so that collisions happen as often as possible. The collision rate at full luminosity reaches about one billion proton-proton collisions per second. Sophisticated trigger systems select only the most interesting few hundred per second to record.

The LHC program runs in multi-year "runs" separated by long shutdowns for upgrades. Run 1 (2010-2012) delivered the Higgs boson discovery at 7-8 TeV. Run 2 (2015-2018) ran at 13 TeV and produced the bulk of the LHC's published dataset. Run 3 (2022-2026) runs at 13.6 TeV. After Long Shutdown 3, the High-Luminosity LHC upgrade will increase the collision rate tenfold, aiming to collect ten times more data by the early 2040s.

JAH-R20-CERN-000003 — ATLAS and CMS — the general-purpose detectors

ATLAS and CMS are the two general-purpose experiments at the Large Hadron Collider, built independently by worldwide collaborations so that any discovery can be confirmed by a second detector using different technology. Both are gigantic instruments: ATLAS is 46 metres long, 25 metres in diameter, and weighs about 7,000 tonnes; CMS is 21 metres long, 15 metres in diameter, and weighs about 14,000 tonnes — twice the weight of the Eiffel Tower's iron — largely because of its massive steel return yoke.

Both detectors are layered like onions. Closest to the collision point sit silicon tracking detectors that record the curved paths of charged particles in a magnetic field, letting physicists measure their momentum. Around them sit calorimeters that absorb particles and measure their energy: electromagnetic calorimeters catch electrons and photons, hadronic calorimeters catch particles made of quarks. The outermost layers are muon chambers, because muons pass through almost everything and are a clean signature of many interesting processes. ATLAS uses a 2-tesla solenoid plus huge toroidal magnets; CMS uses a single enormous 3.8-tesla solenoid, the largest superconducting magnet ever built for this purpose at the time.

ATLAS and CMS were the two experiments that jointly announced the discovery of a Higgs-like boson on 4 July 2012, each seeing the signal independently at about five standard deviations. Since then they have measured the Higgs boson's properties with ever-greater precision — its mass near 125 GeV, its spin and parity, and its couplings to other particles — and they lead the searches for physics beyond the Standard Model: supersymmetry, extra dimensions, dark-matter candidates, and new forces. Each collaboration publishes hundreds of papers per year, and much of their data is released publicly through the CERN Open Data portal.

JAH-R20-CERN-000004 — ALICE and LHCb — the specialist experiments

ALICE and LHCb are the LHC's two large specialist experiments, each optimized for a different physics program. ALICE (A Large Ion Collider Experiment) studies the quark-gluon plasma: the hot, dense state of matter in which quarks and gluons roam freely instead of being locked inside protons and neutrons, believed to have filled the universe in the first microseconds after the Big Bang. To make it, ALICE collides heavy lead ions at energies that recreate temperatures over 100,000 times hotter than the Sun's core — trillions of degrees. Its Time Projection Chamber, one of the largest of its kind, tracks thousands of particles streaming from each collision.

LHCb studies the subtle difference between matter and antimatter by examining particles containing beauty (bottom) quarks. The Standard Model predicts nearly equal amounts of matter and antimatter should have been created in the Big Bang, yet the universe is made almost entirely of matter; LHCb hunts for the tiny asymmetries — CP violation — that could explain why. Unlike ATLAS and CMS, which surround the collision point completely, LHCb is a forward spectrometer: it instruments only a cone around the beam direction, because beauty quarks at the LHC are produced mostly flying forward. LHCb has made world-leading measurements of rare decays and discovered new hadrons, including pentaquark candidates.

Both experiments share the LHC's beam with the general-purpose detectors but ask narrower, deeper questions. ALICE's lead-ion program maps the phase diagram of nuclear matter; LHCb's precision flavor program tests the Standard Model at the level of one part in billions. Together with the smaller experiments TOTEM, LHCf, MoEDAL, and FASER, they make the LHC a far broader facility than a Higgs factory alone.

JAH-R20-CERN-000005 — Discovery of the Higgs boson — 4 July 2012

On 4 July 2012, in a packed CERN auditorium, the ATLAS and CMS collaborations announced they had each observed a new particle with a mass near 125 GeV — consistent with the long-sought Higgs boson. Both experiments reported signals at about five standard deviations, the particle-physics threshold for claiming a discovery, meaning the chance of a statistical fluke was roughly one in three and a half million. The announcement confirmed a prediction made nearly fifty years earlier.

The Higgs boson is the quantum of the Higgs field, proposed in 1964 by Peter Higgs and, independently, by Robert Brout and Francois Englert and others. The Higgs field fills all of space; particles acquire mass by interacting with it, the way a swimmer feels drag in water. Without it, the equations of the Standard Model predict massless particles — and a universe nothing like ours. Finding the boson proved the field exists. Peter Higgs and Francois Englert received the 2013 Nobel Prize in Physics for the theoretical prediction.

The discovery channels were the boson's decays into two photons and into four leptons via Z bosons — clean, unmistakable signatures standing out from the enormous background of ordinary collisions. In the decade since, ATLAS and CMS have measured the particle's spin (zero), parity (even), mass (about 125 GeV with sub-percent precision), and couplings to W and Z bosons, top quarks, bottom quarks, and tau leptons — all matching Standard Model predictions so far. The Higgs program continues at the High-Luminosity LHC, where physicists will measure its rarest decays and its self-interaction, the shape of the field's own potential.

JAH-R20-CERN-000006 — The CERN accelerator chain — from Linac to LHC

The LHC does not start from rest: it is the final link in a chain of accelerators, each boosting particles to the energy the next one needs. The chain begins at Linac4, a linear accelerator that fires negative hydrogen ions to 160 MeV. The ions pass through a stripping foil that removes their electrons, leaving bare protons, which enter the Proton Synchrotron Booster (PSB) and are accelerated to 2 GeV. Next comes the Proton Synchrotron (PS), CERN's oldest operating synchrotron — running since 1959 — which takes the beam to 26 GeV and shapes it into the bunch structure the LHC needs.

Then the Super Proton Synchrotron (SPS), a 7-kilometre ring commissioned in 1976, accelerates protons to 450 GeV and injects them into the LHC in both directions. The whole chain must work in sequence: a fault anywhere stops the LHC's fill. The same complex also feeds other programs — the SPS sends beam to fixed-target experiments in the North Area, and a separate chain (Linac3, LEIR) prepares lead ions for the LHC's heavy-ion program.

Each machine in the chain was once the frontier itself. The PS discovered the neutral current's neighborhood and fed the experiments of the 1960s; the SPS as a proton-antiproton collider discovered the W and Z bosons in 1983, earning Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer the 1984 Nobel Prize. This reuse of machines across decades is a CERN hallmark: the LHC itself lives in the LEP tunnel, and the High-Luminosity LHC will reuse the LHC's tunnel in turn.

JAH-R20-CERN-000007 — Antimatter at CERN — the Antimatter Factory

CERN hosts the world's only dedicated antimatter research facility, the Antimatter Factory. Antimatter is real, well-understood physics: every particle has an antiparticle twin with opposite charge. When matter meets antimatter they annihilate into pure energy, which is why antimatter must be trapped in electromagnetic bottles — Penning traps — suspended in ultrahigh vacuum, never touching ordinary matter.

The factory's chain starts with the Antiproton Decelerator (AD), which slows antiprotons made by smashing protons into a metal target, and ELENA (Extra Low ENergy Antiproton ring), a small synchrotron that decelerates them further to energies the experiments can catch. Experiments then do extraordinary things with them. ALPHA, the flagship, combines antiprotons with positrons to make antihydrogen — the antimatter twin of the simplest atom — and holds it in a magnetic trap to measure its spectrum, testing whether antimatter obeys the same laws as matter. In 2023 ALPHA-g measured antihydrogen's gravitational behavior directly: it falls down, like ordinary matter, ruling out antigravity at the experiment's precision. BASE measures the antiproton's magnetic moment with record precision, comparing matter and antimatter to better than a part per billion.

Why bother? The universe should have made equal matter and antimatter, yet antimatter is vanishingly rare today. Every precision comparison between the two is a probe of that cosmic mystery. CERN's antimatter program turns one of science fiction's favorite substances into careful, quantitative laboratory science.

JAH-R20-CERN-000008 — ISOLDE, n_TOF, and CERN's fixed-target program

Not all of CERN's science happens in colliders. ISOLDE (Isotope Separator On Line DEvice), running since 1967, is the world's leading facility for producing exotic radioactive nuclei: a 1.4 GeV proton beam from the PS Booster strikes a thick target, creating hundreds of short-lived isotopes that are ionized, mass-separated, and delivered to experiments. ISOLDE nuclei have probed the limits of nuclear existence, refined atomic clocks, and produced medical isotopes — its techniques feed directly into targeted cancer therapies studied at CERN's MEDICIS facility.

n_TOF (neutron Time-Of-Flight) makes the world's most intense pulsed neutron beams by spilling protons onto a lead target; the neutrons' energies are measured by timing their 185-metre flight. Its data on neutron-induced reactions underpins models of how heavy elements are forged in stars (the s-process) and informs the design of safer nuclear systems. The CLOUD experiment fires a pion beam into a cloud chamber to study how cosmic rays seed cloud formation — a controlled laboratory probe of a climate-relevant process. HiRadMat tests how materials survive direct hits from intense beam pulses, essential for designing future accelerators.

These facilities show CERN's breadth: the same accelerator complex that feeds the LHC also serves nuclear physics, astrophysics, climate science, and medicine. The laboratory's fixed-target North Area hosts a dozen experiments studying everything from kaon decays (NA62) to the structure of the proton (COMPASS/AMBER).

JAH-R20-CERN-000009 — CERN computing — the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid

The LHC produces about 90 petabytes of data per year — far more than any single laboratory could store or analyze. CERN's answer is the Worldwide LHC Computing Grid (WLCG): a global collaboration of more than 170 computing centres in over 40 countries, federated into a single infrastructure that stores, distributes, and processes LHC data around the clock. Born from the same open-collaboration ethos as the Web, the Grid turns thousands of institutions' computers into one virtual supercomputer for particle physics.

The data flow starts at CERN's Tier-0 centre, which records the raw collision data and distributes copies to thirteen Tier-1 centres on four continents for reprocessing and archiving, then onward to roughly 160 Tier-2 centres where physicists run their analyses. This hierarchy balances the load: no single site is a bottleneck, and a physicist in Tokyo analyzes the same datasets as one in Geneva. The Grid routinely delivers hundreds of thousands of processor cores and exabytes of managed storage to the LHC experiments.

CERN's computing legacy runs deeper than the Grid. Tim Berners-Lee's 1989 proposal for "a web of notes with links" was written to solve physicists' information-sharing problem, and the first web server ran at CERN. Today CERN's open-data releases — hundreds of terabytes of real LHC collision data with analysis examples — let students and independent researchers reproduce published measurements. The laboratory that invented the Web now uses its descendant technologies to sift a billion collisions per second for new physics.

JAH-R20-CERN-000010 — CERN's discovery timeline — a public record of breakthroughs

CERN's public record of discovery stretches back to the 1950s and reads like a history of modern particle physics. In 1973 the Gargamelle bubble chamber found neutral currents — the first evidence of the weak neutral force, a cornerstone of the electroweak theory. In 1983 the UA1 and UA2 experiments at the SPS proton-antiproton collider discovered the W and Z bosons, the carriers of the weak force, confirming the theory and earning the 1984 Nobel Prize. In 1989-2000 the LEP collider measured the Z boson's properties so precisely that it pinned down the number of light neutrino families to three.

The 1990s brought the World Wide Web (1989-1993) as a CERN invention rather than a physics result, and the direct detection of antimatter nuclei: antihydrogen atoms were first created at CERN's LEAR facility in 1995. The new millennium's headline was the Higgs boson in 2012, but the quieter program mattered too: ISOLDE mapped hundreds of exotic nuclei, the SPS heavy-ion program found evidence for a new state of matter (announced 2000), and neutrino beams from CERN to Gran Sasso (OPERA, ICARUS) measured neutrino oscillations over 730 km of rock.

Each discovery followed the same public pattern: open publication, independent confirmation, and data preserved for reanalysis. CERN's timeline is public precisely because the laboratory treats its results as humanity's property — the papers, the datasets, and increasingly the analysis code are all released. That is why a database of CERN can be built from public records alone: the laboratory publishes everything it finds.
