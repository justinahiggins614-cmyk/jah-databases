The Wheel

ID: JAH-IVNT-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The wheel is a circular object that turns around a central axle, letting a load roll instead of being dragged. It is one of the oldest and most important inventions in human history, and almost every machine built since depends on it in some form. A wheel works by turning sliding friction, the resistance of dragging something across the ground, into rolling friction, which is far smaller, so the same load can be moved with much less effort. The first wheels were probably potter's wheels, used to shape clay, and the first wheels for transport appeared in Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq, around 3500 BCE, though the exact date is uncertain. From there the wheel spread around the world, changing transport, farming, pottery, and eventually industry.

History: The wheel was invented more than once, or at least in more than one place, but the earliest evidence comes from Mesopotamia. Clay tablets and pictures from around 3500 BCE show wheeled carts, and a clay potter's wheel from the same period has been found at Uruk, in modern Iraq. The wheel then spread to Europe and Asia, and by 2000 BCE wheeled chariots were used in warfare and ceremony from Egypt to China. The ancient Greeks and Romans used wheels for carts, mills, and water-lifting devices, and the waterwheel, which turns flowing water into power, became one of the great machines of the ancient world. The wheel did not reach the Americas before European contact, and the peoples of the Americas, though they made wheeled toys, did not use wheels for transport, probably because they had no large draft animals to pull carts. In the 19th century the wheel changed again: the bicycle, the railway, and the automobile all depended on it, and the invention of the rubber tyre and the ball bearing made wheels faster, smoother, and more reliable. Today wheels are everywhere, from the gears in a watch to the turbines in a power station.

Key concepts: An axle is the rod or shaft at the centre of a wheel, around which the wheel turns; the load is carried by the axle, and the wheel rolls around it. Friction is the resistance that opposes motion when two surfaces rub together; a wheel reduces friction by rolling instead of sliding. A bearing is a device that lets a wheel turn smoothly around its axle with little friction; modern ball bearings use small steel balls to do this. Torque is the turning force applied to a wheel, for example by a horse pulling a cart or an engine turning a car's wheels. A gear is a wheel with teeth that meshes with other gears to transfer motion and change speed or force; gears are wheels adapted for machines. A flywheel is a heavy wheel that stores energy by spinning, smoothing out the motion of an engine.

How it works: A wheel works because rolling is far easier than dragging. When a heavy load is dragged across the ground, the whole weight presses on the ground and friction resists the motion. When the same load is put on a wheel, the wheel rolls, and only a small patch of the wheel touches the ground at any moment, so the friction is much less — typically a hundred times less for a good wheel on a hard surface. The axle carries the weight, and the wheel turns around it, so the load moves forward as the wheel rotates. The size of the wheel matters: a larger wheel rolls over bumps more easily, which is why carts and wagons had large wheels, while a smaller wheel turns faster, which is why gears and turbines use small wheels. In a machine, wheels transfer power: a belt or a gear connects one wheel to another, and the motion is passed along, changed in speed or direction as needed. The wheel is so basic that it appears in almost every machine, from a simple hand cart to a jet engine.

Worked examples: The potter's wheel is the oldest example: a flat disc that spins, letting a potter shape clay into a symmetrical pot with both hands, a technique that spread across the ancient world and is still used today. The chariot is a military example: two-wheeled war chariots, pulled by horses, were used by the Egyptians, the Hittites, and the Chinese, and they changed warfare by giving armies speed and mobility. The waterwheel is an energy example: a wheel turned by a stream drove mills that ground grain, sawed wood, and powered early factories, and it was the main source of mechanical power in Europe for a thousand years. The railway wheel is an industrial example: steel wheels on steel rails let trains carry enormous loads with little friction, which is why railways could move coal, iron, and people cheaply. The bicycle wheel is a personal example: two light wheels, a chain, and gears let one person travel faster and further than on foot, and the bicycle changed how people lived and worked.

Real applications: The wheel's first application was transport: carts, wagons, and chariots let people move goods and travel further than they could on foot or with pack animals, and trade expanded as a result. Its second application was manufacturing: the potter's wheel, the spinning wheel, and the grinding wheel all use rotation to shape materials, and they made production faster and more consistent. Its third application was power: waterwheels and windmills turned the wheel into an engine, grinding grain and driving machinery for centuries before steam power arrived. Its fourth application is in machines: gears, pulleys, flywheels, and turbines are all wheels, and they are the building blocks of engines, clocks, and factories. Finally, the wheel is the basis of modern transport: cars, trains, and aircraft all depend on wheels, whether for rolling on the ground or for the turbines and gears inside their engines. It is hard to name a machine that does not contain a wheel.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the wheel? No one person did; it was invented in Mesopotamia around 3500 BCE, and possibly independently elsewhere, and the name of the inventor is lost. Why did the wheel take so long to invent? Because it is not obvious: a wheel needs a round shape, an axle, and a hard surface to roll on, and it is useless without a road or a draft animal to pull it. Did the ancient Americans have the wheel? They made wheeled toys, but they did not use wheels for transport, probably because they had no horses or oxen to pull carts, and because the terrain was mountainous. Is the wheel the most important invention ever? Many historians rank it among the most important, alongside fire, agriculture, and writing, because it made transport and machines possible. What is the difference between a wheel and a gear? A gear is a wheel with teeth, designed to mesh with other gears and transfer motion in a machine, while a plain wheel is designed to roll on the ground.

Paper

ID: JAH-IVNT-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Paper is a thin material made from plant fibres, used for writing, printing, packaging, and many other purposes. It is made by soaking fibres in water, spreading them into a thin sheet, and drying them, so that the fibres lock together into a flexible surface that takes ink. Paper was invented in China, and the traditional date is 105 CE, when the court official Cai Lun is said to have presented an improved method of making paper to the emperor, though paper-like materials existed in China before that, and the attribution to Cai Lun is traditional rather than certain. Paper replaced heavier and more expensive writing materials such as bamboo, silk, and animal skin, and it made books, records, and letters cheap enough for ordinary use, which changed how knowledge spread.

History: The earliest paper-like material was papyrus, made in Egypt from the papyrus plant from about 3000 BCE, but true paper, made from pulped fibres, was a Chinese invention. Fragments of paper from the 2nd century BCE have been found in China, made from hemp and other fibres, so the craft existed before Cai Lun. The traditional story is that in 105 CE, Cai Lun, a court official of the Han dynasty, improved the process by using tree bark, rags, and old fishing nets, and presented the result to Emperor He. Papermaking spread slowly: it reached Korea and Japan by the 7th century, the Arab world by the 8th century, after Chinese papermakers were captured at the Battle of Talas in 751 CE, according to the traditional account, and Europe by the 12th century, through Spain and Italy. In Europe, paper mills used water power to pulp rags, and by the 15th century paper was cheap enough for the printing press, which needed it in large quantities. In the 19th century, wood pulp replaced rags, making paper far cheaper still, and today paper is made from wood, recycled paper, and other plant fibres in vast quantities.

Key concepts: Pulp is the wet mass of fibres from which paper is made, produced by breaking down wood, rags, or other plant material in water. A fibre is a long, thin thread of plant material; paper's strength comes from the fibres locking together as the sheet dries. Sizing is the treatment that makes paper resist ink soaking through, so that writing stays sharp on the surface. A watermark is a faint design pressed into paper while it is wet, used to identify the maker or to prevent forgery. Rag paper is paper made from cotton or linen rags, which is strong and long-lasting; wood-pulp paper is cheaper but yellows and becomes brittle with age. Recycling is the process of making new paper from old paper, which saves trees, water, and energy.

How it works: Paper is made by separating plant fibres, suspending them in water, and then letting the water drain away through a fine screen, leaving a thin mat of fibres that is pressed and dried. The fibres are mostly cellulose, a long molecule that forms the walls of plant cells, and when wet fibres dry together, hydrogen bonds form between them, locking the sheet into a flexible, strong material. In a modern paper mill, wood is chipped and cooked in chemicals to separate the fibres, the pulp is cleaned and bleached, and it is spread onto a moving wire mesh, where the water drains away; the sheet is then pressed between rollers, dried with steam-heated cylinders, and wound into large rolls. Additives are mixed in to control the paper's properties: clay makes it smooth for printing, sizing makes it resist ink, and dyes give it colour. The process is continuous and fast: a modern paper machine can produce a sheet several metres wide at speeds of more than 100 kilometres per hour.

Worked examples: The paper of Cai Lun's time was used for writing imperial records, letters, and books, and it replaced bamboo slips, which were heavy, and silk, which was expensive. In Japan, papermaking became an art: washi, traditional Japanese paper, is made from the bark of the mulberry tree and is strong enough to be used for sliding doors, lanterns, and clothing. In Europe, the first paper mills, such as the one at Fabriano in Italy in the 13th century, made paper from linen rags and produced the paper that Gutenberg's press would print on. The newspaper is a modern example: cheap wood-pulp paper made it possible to print millions of copies a day, and the daily newspaper became the main way people learned the news. The banknote is another example: paper money, first used in China in the 7th century and later around the world, depends on special paper that is hard to forge.

Real applications: Paper's first application was writing and record-keeping: it let governments, merchants, and scholars keep records cheaply, and it made bureaucracy and trade possible on a large scale. Its second application was printing: the printing press needed cheap paper, and paper needed the press, and together they made books affordable and spread knowledge across Europe and then the world. Its third application is packaging: paper and cardboard protect goods, carry labels, and are used for boxes, bags, and wrapping, and they are recyclable. Its fourth application is hygiene and medicine: paper is used for tissues, towels, and medical dressings. Finally, paper is used in art and design: drawing, painting, origami, and model-making all depend on it. Even in the digital age, paper remains essential, and about 400 million tonnes are produced each year.

Common questions and misconceptions: Did Cai Lun really invent paper? He is traditionally credited with it, but paper fragments from before his time have been found, so he probably improved an existing craft rather than inventing it from nothing; the date of 105 CE is traditional, not certain. Why did paper take so long to reach Europe? Because the craft spread slowly along trade routes, and because Europe had parchment, made from animal skin, which was durable and already in use; paper only became common in Europe when it became cheaper than parchment. Is paper bad for the environment? Paper production uses trees, water, and energy, but paper is renewable, recyclable, and biodegradable, and much of the world's paper now comes from managed forests and recycled material. Will paper disappear? Probably not soon: even as screens replace printed pages, paper is still used for packaging, hygiene, art, and legal documents. What is the difference between paper and papyrus? Papyrus is made by pressing strips of the papyrus plant together, while paper is made from pulped fibres; paper is stronger and more flexible.

The Compass

ID: JAH-IVNT-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The compass is an instrument that shows direction, usually by pointing to the Earth's magnetic north. The simplest compass is a magnetised needle that turns freely and aligns itself with the Earth's magnetic field, so one end points north and the other south. The compass was invented in China, where lodestone, a naturally magnetic rock, was used first for fortune-telling and then for navigation, and by the 11th century Chinese ships were using magnetic compasses at sea. The compass changed navigation: sailors could find their way even when the sun and stars were hidden, and it made long ocean voyages possible, including the voyages of exploration that connected the world. Today the magnetic compass is joined by the gyrocompass and the satellite compass, but the principle is the same: find north, and you can find your way.

History: The compass begins with lodestone, a mineral that is naturally magnetic and was known in China by the 3rd century BCE. Early Chinese compasses were spoon-shaped pieces of lodestone that turned on a bronze plate marked with directions, and they were used in divination, the art of telling fortunes, before they were used for navigation. By the 11th century, during the Song dynasty, Chinese writers described a magnetised iron needle floating in water, and by the 12th century Chinese ships were using compasses at sea. The compass reached Europe by the 12th or 13th century, probably through Arab traders, and European sailors improved it: they mounted the needle on a pivot, put it in a box, and added a wind rose, a card marked with the directions. The compass made the great voyages of exploration possible: without it, Columbus, da Gama, and Magellan could not have sailed out of sight of land with confidence. In the 19th and 20th centuries, the gyrocompass, which uses a spinning wheel to find true north, replaced the magnetic compass on large ships, and today satellite navigation has joined it, but the magnetic compass remains a standard instrument on ships, aircraft, and in the hands of hikers.

Key concepts: Magnetism is the force by which certain materials attract iron; the Earth itself is a giant magnet, with a magnetic field that runs from south to north. Lodestone is a naturally magnetic mineral, magnetite, that was the first magnetic material known to humans. Magnetic north is the direction a compass needle points; it is not the same as true north, the direction of the North Pole, and the difference between them is called magnetic declination. A wind rose is a circular card marked with the directions, north, south, east, and west, and the points between them, which sits under the compass needle. A gyrocompass is a compass that uses a spinning gyroscope to find true north, and it is not affected by the ship's iron or by magnetic storms. Declination is the angle between magnetic north and true north, which varies from place to place and changes slowly over time.

How it works: A magnetic compass works because the Earth has a magnetic field, generated by the movement of molten iron in its core. A magnetised needle is a small magnet, and like any magnet it has a north-seeking end and a south-seeking end; when it is free to turn, the Earth's field twists it until it lines up with the field, so the needle points toward magnetic north. The needle is mounted on a pivot or floated in liquid so that friction is low and it can turn easily. The compass card, marked with the directions, turns with the needle, and the sailor or hiker reads the direction from the card. The compass is not perfect: nearby iron, such as a ship's hull, can deflect the needle, and the difference between magnetic north and true north must be corrected for, using tables of declination. A gyrocompass works differently: a fast-spinning wheel resists changes in its direction, and the Earth's rotation makes it settle pointing true north, which is why it is used on steel ships, where a magnetic compass would be unreliable.

Worked examples: The Chinese spoon compass is the earliest example: a piece of lodestone carved into a spoon shape that turned on a bronze plate, used for divination in the Han dynasty. The Song dynasty needle compass is the first navigational example: a magnetised needle floating in a bowl of water, described by the scholar Shen Kuo in the 11th century, and used by Chinese sailors. The European dry compass is the medieval example: a needle mounted on a pivot in a wooden box, with a wind rose card, which became standard on European ships by the 14th century. The gyrocompass is the modern example: invented in the early 20th century, it is used on large ships and submarines because it points to true north and is not affected by the ship's magnetism. The smartphone compass is the everyday example: a tiny electronic sensor measures the Earth's magnetic field and shows the direction on the screen, and it is used by millions of people for navigation and maps.

Real applications: The compass's first application was navigation at sea: it let sailors cross oceans out of sight of land, and it made possible the voyages of exploration, the spice trade, and the mapping of the world. Its second application was land navigation: surveyors used compasses to map land, soldiers used them to march, and explorers used them to cross deserts and forests. Its third application was in science: the study of the compass led to the discovery of the Earth's magnetic field, and to the science of magnetism itself. Its fourth application is in aircraft and spacecraft: compasses and their descendants guide planes and help spacecraft orient themselves. Finally, the compass is a symbol: it appears on maps, in logos, and in the phrase "moral compass", and it is one of the Four Great Inventions of China, along with paper, gunpowder, and printing.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the compass? The Chinese, who first used lodestone for divination and then for navigation; the exact inventor is unknown, and the compass reached Europe through trade, not through a single inventor. Does a compass point to the North Pole? No, it points to magnetic north, which is in northern Canada and moves slowly; the difference from true north is called declination, and navigators correct for it. Why does a compass needle point north? Because the needle is a magnet, and the Earth's magnetic field pulls its north-seeking end toward the magnetic north pole. Can a compass be wrong? Yes: nearby iron, magnets, and electrical equipment can deflect the needle, and magnetic storms can disturb the Earth's field. Is the compass still used today? Yes, on ships, aircraft, and by hikers, and as a backup to satellite navigation, which can fail or be jammed.

Gunpowder

ID: JAH-IVNT-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Gunpowder is an explosive mixture of saltpetre, charcoal, and sulfur that burns very quickly and produces a large volume of hot gas. It was the first chemical explosive, and it changed warfare, mining, and celebration around the world. Gunpowder was invented in China, where alchemists searching for an elixir of life mixed the ingredients and discovered that the mixture burned with a flash and a bang; the earliest written formula dates to the 9th century, during the Tang dynasty. The Chinese first used gunpowder for fireworks and signals, then for weapons, and the knowledge spread west along trade routes, reaching Europe by the 13th century. Gunpowder remained the main explosive for more than a thousand years, until it was replaced by more powerful explosives in the 19th century.

History: The story of gunpowder begins with Chinese alchemy: in the 9th century, alchemists mixing saltpetre, the mineral potassium nitrate, with sulfur and charcoal found that the mixture exploded, and a text from about 850 CE warns alchemists not to mix these ingredients because they had burned down houses. By the 10th century, the Chinese were using gunpowder in fire arrows, bombs, and fireworks, and by the 13th century they had the first guns, tubes that fired projectiles with gunpowder. The knowledge spread to the Arab world and then to Europe, where the first European guns appeared in the 14th century; the English monk Roger Bacon wrote a formula for gunpowder in about 1267, though it was already known. In the centuries that followed, gunpowder changed warfare: castles fell to cannon, knights in armour became obsolete, and empires rose and fell on the strength of their guns. Gunpowder was also used for mining, quarrying, and building roads and canals, and for fireworks, which remain its most peaceful use. In the 19th century, new explosives such as dynamite and TNT replaced gunpowder for most purposes, but it is still used in fireworks, in some ammunition, and in historical re-enactments.

Key concepts: Saltpetre, or potassium nitrate, is the key ingredient of gunpowder: it supplies oxygen, so the mixture can burn quickly even without air. Charcoal is the fuel: it is carbon, and it burns to produce hot gas. Sulfur lowers the temperature at which the mixture catches fire and makes it burn more steadily. Combustion is rapid burning; gunpowder does not detonate like a modern explosive, it deflagrates, meaning it burns very fast, producing gas that pushes a bullet or a rocket. A firework is a device that uses gunpowder to produce light, colour, and sound for entertainment. A cannon is a large gun that fires a heavy projectile using gunpowder, and it was the dominant weapon of siege warfare for centuries.

How it works: Gunpowder works because it carries its own oxygen. Ordinary burning needs oxygen from the air, but gunpowder's saltpetre supplies oxygen when it is heated, so the charcoal and sulfur can burn very quickly, even inside a closed tube. When gunpowder is ignited, the saltpetre breaks down and releases oxygen, the charcoal burns to carbon dioxide, and the sulfur burns to sulfur dioxide; the reaction produces a large volume of hot gas in a fraction of a second. In a gun, that gas is trapped behind a bullet, and the pressure pushes the bullet out of the barrel at high speed. In a firework, the gas lifts the firework into the sky, and other chemicals produce the colours. In a mine, the gas breaks rock apart. The classic mixture is about 75 percent saltpetre, 15 percent charcoal, and 10 percent sulfur, and the ingredients must be ground finely and mixed evenly; the better the mixture, the faster and more powerful the burn. Modern explosives are more powerful because they release their energy even faster, but gunpowder's principle — a mixture that carries its own oxygen — is the same.

Worked examples: The fire arrow is an early example: a rocket attached to an arrow, used by Chinese armies in the 10th century to set fire to enemy positions. The fire lance is another: a tube filled with gunpowder that shot flames and projectiles, an early form of gun. The cannon is the great military example: by the 15th century, cannon could knock down castle walls, and the fall of Constantinople in 1453 was achieved with huge guns built by the Ottoman sultan Mehmed II. Fireworks are the peaceful example: Chinese fireworks, made with gunpowder and metal salts for colour, spread around the world and are now part of celebrations everywhere. Gunpowder in mining is an industrial example: in the 17th century, miners began using gunpowder to break rock, which made it possible to dig deeper mines and to build canals and roads through hills.

Real applications: Gunpowder's first application was warfare: guns, cannon, and bombs changed how wars were fought, made castles and armour obsolete, and helped empires expand. Its second application was mining and engineering: gunpowder let miners break rock, builders cut through hills, and engineers dig canals and tunnels. Its third application was celebration: fireworks, invented in China, are now used around the world for festivals, national holidays, and New Year. Its fourth application was in science: the study of gunpowder led to the science of chemistry, and to the development of better explosives. Finally, gunpowder is still used today in fireworks, in some types of ammunition, and in the study of history, where re-enactors use it to show how early guns worked. Its importance is not that it is still used, but that it was the first explosive, and it opened the age of gunpowder warfare that lasted for six centuries.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented gunpowder? Chinese alchemists in the 9th century, during the Tang dynasty; no single inventor is known, and the discovery was probably accidental. Why were alchemists mixing these ingredients? They were searching for an elixir of life, a potion that would make them immortal, and they mixed many substances in the hope of finding it. Is gunpowder still used? Yes, in fireworks, in some ammunition, and in historical firearms, but modern explosives have replaced it for mining and military use. What is the difference between gunpowder and dynamite? Gunpowder burns quickly but does not detonate, while dynamite and TNT detonate, releasing their energy in a shock wave that is far more powerful. Did gunpowder change the world? Yes, profoundly: it changed warfare, made empires possible, and led to the science of explosives, though its effects were often destructive.

The Printing Press

ID: JAH-IVNT-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The printing press is a machine that transfers ink from movable type onto paper, producing many copies of a text quickly and cheaply. Before it, books were copied by hand, one at a time, and were so expensive that only churches, universities, and the wealthy owned them. The press with movable metal type was developed in Europe by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440, in Mainz, Germany, though movable type had been invented earlier in China and Korea, and woodblock printing had existed in China for centuries. Gutenberg's press made it possible to print hundreds of copies of a book in the time it once took to copy one, and it is often called the most important invention of the second millennium, because it spread knowledge, science, and new ideas across Europe and then the world.

History: Printing began in China: woodblock printing, in which a whole page is carved into a block of wood, was used from the 7th century, and the world's earliest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE, was made this way. In the 1040s, the Chinese inventor Bi Sheng made movable type from clay, and later the Koreans made movable metal type, printing the Jikji in 1377, the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type. In Europe, Gutenberg combined several existing ideas — the screw press used for wine and oil, movable metal type, oil-based ink, and paper — into a working system around 1440. His first great work was the Gutenberg Bible, printed around 1455, of which about 180 copies were made. Within fifty years, printing presses were operating in more than 200 European cities, and about 20 million books had been printed, more than all the books copied by hand in the previous thousand years. The press spread ideas that changed Europe: the Renaissance, the Reformation, and the Scientific Revolution all depended on printed books, and the press made it possible for ordinary people to read the Bible, learn science, and follow the news.

Key concepts: Movable type is type in which each letter is a separate piece of metal that can be arranged to form a page, used, and then rearranged for the next page; it is the key idea of Gutenberg's press. A typeface is a set of letters designed in a consistent style; Gutenberg's type imitated the handwriting of scribes. Ink is the liquid used to print; Gutenberg's oil-based ink stuck to metal type better than the water-based ink used by scribes. A press is a machine that squeezes the paper against the inked type with even pressure; Gutenberg adapted the screw press used for wine. An edition is all the copies of a book printed at one time; the press made large editions possible. A pamphlet is a short printed work, often only a few pages, which became a way to spread news and ideas quickly.

How it works: A printing press works in a series of steps. First, the printer sets the type: individual metal letters are arranged in a frame, called a chase, to form the page, with spaces between the words and lines. Second, the type is inked: rollers spread a thin layer of oil-based ink over the raised faces of the letters. Third, a sheet of damp paper is laid over the type, and the press squeezes them together, so the ink transfers from the type to the paper. Fourth, the paper is removed and hung up to dry, and the process is repeated for the next sheet. A skilled team could print several hundred sheets a day, and because the type could be reused, the same press could print many different books. The press itself was a large wooden frame with a screw that drove a flat plate, called the platen, down onto the paper; later presses used levers and then steam power, but the principle — inked type pressed against paper — remained the same until the 20th century.

Worked examples: The Gutenberg Bible, printed around 1455, is the most famous example: about 180 copies were made, of which about 50 survive, and it is one of the most valuable books in the world. The Jikji, printed in Korea in 1377 with movable metal type, is an earlier example that shows the idea existed outside Europe. Martin Luther's pamphlets are a social example: in the 1520s, Luther's writings were printed in thousands of copies and spread across Germany in weeks, something that would have been impossible by hand copying. The scientific book is another example: Copernicus's "On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres", printed in 1543, and Newton's "Principia", printed in 1687, spread new ideas about the universe because they could be printed and read by scholars across Europe. The newspaper is a later example: from the 17th century, presses printed news sheets that became the daily newspaper, the main way people learned the news for three centuries.

Real applications: The press's first application was books: it made them cheap enough for students, merchants, and ordinary readers, and the number of books in Europe grew from thousands to millions in a century. Its second application was religion: the press spread the Bible in local languages and made the Reformation possible, as reformers could print their ideas faster than the Church could suppress them. Its third application was science: scientists could publish their findings and have them read, criticised, and built upon by others, which made modern science possible. Its fourth application was news and politics: pamphlets and newspapers let governments and citizens share information and debate, and they laid the ground for democracy. Finally, the press changed language itself: printed books fixed spelling and grammar, and they helped standardise national languages such as English, French, and German.

Common questions and misconceptions: Did Gutenberg invent printing? He invented the European system of movable metal type and the press, but printing with woodblocks existed in China centuries earlier, and movable type was invented in China and Korea before him; his achievement was combining the ideas into a practical system. Why did printing spread so fast? Because it was enormously cheaper than copying by hand, and because there was a large demand for books, especially religious books, in Europe. What was the first printed book? The earliest dated printed book is the Diamond Sutra, printed in China in 868 CE by woodblock; the first major European printed book was the Gutenberg Bible, around 1455. Did the press cause the Reformation? It did not cause it alone, but it made it possible: Luther's ideas spread in print faster than they could be stopped. Is the printing press still used? Yes, though digital printing and the internet have replaced much of it; offset printing, the modern descendant of Gutenberg's press, still prints most books and newspapers.

Definition: The plow is a farming tool that cuts into the soil, turns it over, and prepares it for planting. It is one of the oldest and most important inventions in agriculture, and it is placed here out of strict chronological order: the plow is far older than the printing press that precedes it in this timeline, because it was invented in the Neolithic period, thousands of years before writing. History: The plow was invented in the Neolithic period, around 4000 BCE or earlier, in the Near East, where the first farmers used a simple wooden ard to scratch furrows in the soil. The plow is pulled through the field in straight lines, and each pass creates a furrow; the farmer then plants seeds in the furrows, where the soil is loose and the seeds can take root. Plowing does several things at once: it breaks up compacted soil so that air and water can reach the roots, it buries weeds so they do not compete with the crop, it mixes nutrients and manure into the soil, and it levels the field. The heavy moldboard plow needs a strong pull, which is why it was drawn by teams of oxen or horses, and why the invention of the horse collar, which lets a horse pull with its shoulders instead of its throat, was so important. Modern plows are pulled by tractors and can plow many furrows at once.

The heavy plow of medieval Europe is a transformative example: pulled by eight oxen, it could cut the thick clay soils of the north, and it made possible the large open fields that fed the growing towns. John Deere's steel plow of 1837 is a modern example: made of polished steel, it cut through the sticky prairie soil of the American Midwest without clogging, and it helped open the prairies to farming.

Real applications: The plow's first application was farming: it let people cultivate more land, grow more food, and support larger populations, and it is one of the foundations of civilisation. Its second application was settlement: plowed fields let farmers stay in one place and build villages and towns, instead of moving to find new land. Its third application was trade: surplus grain from plowed fields could be stored, traded, and taxed, and it supported craftsmen, soldiers, and priests who did not farm. Its fourth application was in the spread of agriculture: the plow travelled with farmers to new lands, from the Near East to Europe, Asia, and the Americas. Finally, the plow is a symbol: it appears in the Bible, in poetry, and in the phrase "beating swords into plowshares", and it remains the emblem of farming around the world.

The Telescope

Definition: The telescope is an instrument that gathers light from distant objects and makes them appear larger and brighter, letting us see things that are too far away or too faint for the naked eye. The first telescopes were made in the Netherlands in 1608, when the spectacle-maker Hans Lippershey applied for a patent for a device that made distant objects appear closer, and within a year Galileo Galilei in Italy had built a better one and pointed it at the sky. The telescope changed astronomy: it showed that the Moon has mountains, that Jupiter has moons, and that the Milky Way is made of stars, and it helped prove that the Earth moves around the Sun. Since then, telescopes have grown from small tubes held in the hand to giant mirrors on mountaintops and in space, and they are the main tool of astronomy.

History: The telescope was invented in 1608 in the Netherlands, a country famous for its lens-makers. Hans Lippershey, a spectacle-maker in Middelburg, applied for a patent on 2 October 1608 for an instrument "for seeing things far away as if they were nearby", and two other Dutchmen, Jacob Metius and Zacharias Janssen, made similar claims; who was truly first is disputed. The news spread quickly, and in 1609 Galileo Galilei, a professor in Padua, built his own telescope, improved it to magnify about 30 times, and turned it on the heavens. In 1610 he published "Sidereus Nuncius", the "Starry Messenger", reporting the mountains of the Moon, the moons of Jupiter, and the phases of Venus, discoveries that supported the idea that the Earth moves around the Sun. In the 17th century, Johannes Kepler improved the design, and Isaac Newton built the first reflecting telescope in 1668, using a mirror instead of a lens. In the 19th and 20th centuries, telescopes grew larger, and in 1990 the Hubble Space Telescope was launched, followed by the James Webb Space Telescope in 2021, which sees further and fainter than any telescope before it.

Key concepts: A lens is a piece of glass that bends light; a telescope uses lenses or mirrors to gather light and form an image. A refracting telescope uses lenses to bend light, while a reflecting telescope uses mirrors to collect it; most large modern telescopes are reflectors. Magnification is how much larger an object appears through the telescope; Galileo's telescope magnified about 30 times, while modern telescopes magnify thousands of times. Aperture is the diameter of the lens or mirror, which determines how much light the telescope gathers and how faint an object it can see. Resolution is the ability to see fine detail; a larger aperture gives better resolution. An observatory is a place where telescopes are kept and used, often on a mountaintop where the air is clear and dark.

How it works: A telescope works by gathering light. The human eye has a small pupil, only a few millimetres wide, so it collects little light; a telescope has a large lens or mirror, sometimes metres wide, so it collects far more light and can see fainter objects. The lens or mirror focuses the light to form an image, and an eyepiece magnifies that image so the eye can see it. In a refracting telescope, light passes through a large lens at the front, which bends it to a focus, and the eyepiece magnifies the image. In a reflecting telescope, light strikes a large curved mirror at the back, which reflects it to a focus, and the eyepiece magnifies it. The key point is that the telescope's power comes mainly from its aperture, not its magnification: a large mirror gathers more light and shows more detail, while magnification alone only makes a dim image bigger. Modern telescopes also use cameras, spectrographs, and computers to record and analyse the light, and some, like the James Webb, work in infrared light, which the eye cannot see.

Worked examples: Galileo's telescope of 1609 is the founding example: with it he saw the mountains and craters of the Moon, the four largest moons of Jupiter, the phases of Venus, and the stars of the Milky Way, discoveries that changed astronomy. Newton's reflecting telescope of 1668 is a design example: by using a mirror instead of a lens, Newton avoided the colour distortion of early lenses, and the reflecting design became the standard for large telescopes. The Hubble Space Telescope is a modern example: launched in 1990, it orbits above the atmosphere, and its images of distant galaxies, nebulae, and the deep field have become famous. The James Webb Space Telescope is the latest example: launched in 2021, it sees in infrared light, and it has observed the earliest galaxies and the atmospheres of planets around other stars. The radio telescope is a different example: it gathers radio waves instead of light, and the Arecibo telescope in Puerto Rico, before its collapse in 2020, was one of the largest.

Real applications: The telescope's first application was astronomy: it let humans see the planets, the stars, and the galaxies, and it made modern astronomy possible. Its second application was navigation and surveying: telescopes were used in theodolites, instruments that measure angles, and in the sights of ships and guns. Its third application was in science: the telescope's discoveries about the solar system led to the scientific revolution, and to the laws of motion and gravity. Its fourth application is in space exploration: telescopes guide spacecraft, study planets, and search for planets around other stars. Finally, the telescope is a tool of culture: it has changed how humans see their place in the universe, from the centre of creation to one planet among many, and its images are among the most widely shared in the world.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the telescope? Hans Lippershey applied for the first patent in 1608, but Jacob Metius and Zacharias Janssen made similar claims, and the true inventor is disputed; Galileo was the first to use it for astronomy. Did Galileo invent the telescope? No, he improved it and was the first to point it at the sky systematically, and his discoveries made it famous. Why are modern telescopes so big? Because a larger mirror gathers more light and shows fainter and more distant objects, and because the atmosphere limits what can be seen from the ground, telescopes are built on mountaintops and in space. Can I see the planets with a small telescope? Yes: even a small telescope shows the rings of Saturn, the moons of Jupiter, and the craters of the Moon. What is the difference between a refracting and a reflecting telescope? A refractor uses lenses, a reflector uses mirrors; reflectors can be made larger and cheaper, so most large telescopes are reflectors.

The Microscope

ID: JAH-IVNT-000008 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The microscope is an instrument that makes very small things appear larger, letting us see what the naked eye cannot. The first microscopes were made in the Netherlands in the 1590s, probably by the spectacle-makers Hans and Zacharias Janssen, though the exact inventor is disputed, and they magnified objects a few times. Within a century, the microscope had revealed a new world: the Dutchman Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using a simple single-lens microscope in the 1670s, saw living creatures in a drop of water, the first observation of microbes, and the Englishman Robert Hooke, in his book "Micrographia" of 1665, described the cells of a cork and the structure of a flea. The microscope changed biology and medicine: it showed that living things are made of cells, that diseases are caused by microbes, and that the world of the very small is as rich as the world we can see.

History: The microscope, like the telescope, came from the lens-makers of the Netherlands. Hans and Zacharias Janssen, father and son, are traditionally credited with making the first compound microscope in the 1590s, though the claim is disputed and the earliest microscopes may have been made by others. In the 1660s, Robert Hooke in London used a compound microscope to study cork, insects, and plants, and his book "Micrographia", published in 1665, with its detailed drawings of a flea and a louse, astonished readers. At the same time, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek in Delft made simple microscopes with a single tiny lens, ground by hand, that magnified up to 300 times, and with them he saw bacteria, protozoa, and sperm cells, which he called "animalcules", little animals. In the 19th century, better lenses and the germ theory of disease made the microscope essential to medicine, and in the 20th century the electron microscope, invented in the 1930s, used beams of electrons instead of light to see objects thousands of times smaller, including viruses. Today microscopes are used in every laboratory, hospital, and factory, and the most powerful can see individual atoms.

Key concepts: Magnification is how much larger an object appears through the microscope; Leeuwenhoek's microscopes magnified about 300 times, while modern light microscopes magnify about 1,500 times and electron microscopes millions of times. Resolution is the ability to see two close objects as separate; a light microscope cannot resolve objects smaller than about 200 nanometres, because of the wavelength of light. A compound microscope uses two or more lenses: an objective lens near the specimen and an eyepiece that magnifies the image further. A cell is the basic unit of life; Hooke coined the word "cell" after the box-like structures he saw in cork. A microbe is a microscopic living thing, such as a bacterium or a protozoan; Leeuwenhoek was the first to see them. An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light to form an image, and it can see far smaller objects.

How it works: A light microscope works by bending light through lenses. The specimen, a thin slice of tissue or a drop of water, is placed on a glass slide and lit from below; the objective lens, close to the specimen, gathers the light and forms a magnified image, and the eyepiece magnifies that image again, so the eye sees the specimen much larger than it is. The total magnification is the objective's magnification multiplied by the eyepiece's, typically 400 times for a school microscope. The limit of a light microscope is set by the wavelength of light: objects smaller than about half the wavelength cannot be resolved, which is why the finest detail needs an electron microscope. An electron microscope works differently: it fires a beam of electrons at the specimen in a vacuum, and the electrons, which have a much shorter wavelength than light, form an image on a screen; it can magnify millions of times and see viruses and the surface of cells. Modern microscopes also use fluorescence, in which the specimen is stained with dyes that glow, and computers to build three-dimensional images.

Worked examples: Hooke's cork is the founding example: in 1665 he looked at a thin slice of cork and saw box-like structures, which he called cells, the first use of the word for the basic unit of life. Leeuwenhoek's "animalcules" are the microbiological example: in the 1670s he saw living creatures in rainwater, in plaque from his teeth, and in pepper water, and he wrote to the Royal Society in London describing them. The germ theory is a medical example: in the 19th century, Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch used microscopes to show that microbes cause disease, and Koch identified the bacteria that cause tuberculosis and cholera. The electron microscope's image of a virus is a modern example: in the 20th century, electron microscopes revealed the shapes of viruses, including the coronavirus, which could not be seen with light. The scanning probe microscope is the latest example: it can image individual atoms, and it lets scientists move atoms one by one.

Real applications: The microscope's first application was biology: it showed that living things are made of cells, and it founded the science of cell biology. Its second application was medicine: it let doctors see microbes, diagnose diseases, and test treatments, and it is essential to every hospital laboratory. Its third application was in industry: microscopes are used to inspect materials, to check the quality of products, and to build tiny devices such as computer chips. Its fourth application was in forensics: microscopes are used to examine fibres, hair, and other evidence in criminal investigations. Finally, the microscope is a tool of education: school microscopes introduce students to the world of cells and microbes, and they are one of the most common scientific instruments in the world.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the microscope? Hans and Zacharias Janssen are traditionally credited, but the claim is disputed, and the first microscopes may have been made by others in the Netherlands in the 1590s. What did Leeuwenhoek see? He saw bacteria, protozoa, sperm cells, and red blood cells, which he called "animalcules", and he was the first person to see microbes. Why can't a light microscope see a virus? Because viruses are smaller than the wavelength of light, so they cannot be resolved; an electron microscope is needed. Is the microscope still important? Yes, it is essential to biology, medicine, and industry, and new kinds of microscope, such as the super-resolution microscope, continue to be invented. What is the difference between a microscope and a telescope? A microscope makes small, near objects appear larger, while a telescope makes distant objects appear larger; both use lenses, but for opposite purposes.

The Steam Engine

ID: JAH-IVNT-000009 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The steam engine is a machine that turns heat into motion, by boiling water to make steam and using the steam's pressure to push a piston or turn a wheel. It was the first engine that could provide power anywhere, not only beside a river or in the wind, and it drove the Industrial Revolution: it pumped water from mines, powered factories, drove trains, and moved ships. The first practical steam engine was built by Thomas Newcomen in 1712 in England, to pump water from coal mines, and James Watt improved it greatly in the 1770s, making it efficient enough for general use. The steam engine was the dominant source of power for more than a century, until it was replaced by the internal combustion engine and electricity, but its principles — heat, pressure, and motion — are still used in power stations today.

History: The idea of steam power is ancient: in the 1st century CE, Hero of Alexandria described the aeolipile, a spinning ball driven by steam, but it was a curiosity, not a machine. In the 17th century, inventors in Europe began to experiment with steam, and in 1698 Thomas Savery built a steam pump, though it was inefficient and dangerous. In 1712 Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger in Devon, built the first practical steam engine: it used steam to create a vacuum that pulled a piston down, and it pumped water from coal mines, where it was used for more than fifty years. In the 1760s James Watt, a Scottish instrument-maker, realised that Newcomen's engine wasted most of its heat by cooling and reheating the cylinder, and he added a separate condenser, which made the engine far more efficient; his first commercial engine was built in 1776. Watt's engines powered mines, mills, and factories, and in 1804 Richard Trevithick built the first steam locomotive, and in 1807 Robert Fulton launched the first commercially successful steamboat. By the mid-19th century, steam engines drove trains across continents and ships across oceans, and the steam age had arrived.

Key concepts: Steam is water in its gaseous form, made by boiling water; it expands to about 1,600 times the volume of the water, and its pressure can do work. A piston is a disc that slides inside a cylinder, pushed by steam pressure on one side; the piston's motion drives the machine. A condenser is a chamber where steam is cooled back into water; Watt's separate condenser kept the main cylinder hot and made the engine efficient. A boiler is a vessel in which water is heated to make steam; early boilers were a source of danger, because they could explode. A flywheel is a heavy wheel that smooths the engine's motion, storing energy between strokes. Horsepower is a unit of power, defined by Watt as the work of a horse, and still used to describe engines.

How it works: A steam engine works by turning heat into motion in a cycle. First, a fire heats water in a boiler, making steam at high pressure. Second, the steam is admitted into a cylinder, where it pushes a piston, and the piston's rod drives a beam, a wheel, or a crank, which turns the motion into useful work, such as pumping water or turning a train's wheels. Third, the steam is cooled in a condenser, where it turns back into water, creating a vacuum that helps pull the piston back. Fourth, the water is pumped back into the boiler, and the cycle repeats. In Newcomen's engine, the steam was used mainly to create a vacuum, and the atmosphere pushed the piston down; in Watt's engine, the steam itself pushed the piston, and the separate condenser saved heat. In a steam locomotive, the piston drives the wheels through rods, and in a steam turbine, the steam spins blades at high speed, a design that is still used in power stations, where the turbine drives a generator to make electricity.

Worked examples: Newcomen's engine of 1712 is the founding example: it pumped water from coal mines in Staffordshire, and it was used for decades, even though it was inefficient. Watt's engine of 1776 is the improving example: with its separate condenser, it used far less coal, and it was soon used in mills, factories, and mines across Britain. Trevithick's locomotive of 1804 is the transport example: it hauled iron along a tramway in Wales, the first time a steam engine pulled a load on rails. Fulton's steamboat, the Clermont, of 1807, is the shipping example: it carried passengers up the Hudson River from New York to Albany, and it showed that steamships could be commercially successful. The steam turbine is the modern example: invented by Charles Parsons in 1884, it uses steam to spin a turbine at high speed, and it is still the main way electricity is generated in coal, gas, and nuclear power stations.

Real applications: The steam engine's first application was mining: it pumped water from deep coal mines, which made it possible to dig deeper and mine more coal, and the coal in turn fuelled more engines. Its second application was manufacturing: steam engines powered mills and factories, freeing industry from rivers and letting factories be built in towns, where workers lived. Its third application was transport: steam locomotives and steamships moved people and goods faster and cheaper than ever before, and they connected continents. Its fourth application was in agriculture and construction: steam tractors plowed fields and steam cranes built bridges and buildings. Finally, the steam engine's principles live on: every thermal power station, whether coal, gas, or nuclear, uses steam to drive a turbine and generate electricity, so the steam age, in a sense, never ended.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the steam engine? No one person did: Savery, Newcomen, and Watt each contributed, and Newcomen built the first practical engine in 1712, while Watt made it efficient in the 1770s. Why did the steam engine matter so much? Because it was the first engine that could provide power anywhere, at any time, and it freed industry from water power and made the Industrial Revolution possible. Is the steam engine still used? The piston steam engine is now rare, but the steam turbine is still the main way electricity is generated in most power stations. What is the difference between Newcomen's and Watt's engines? Newcomen's used steam to make a vacuum, while Watt's used steam pressure directly and added a separate condenser, which made it far more efficient. Why did steam engines explode? Because early boilers were made of weak materials and worked at high pressure; boiler explosions were a real danger until safety valves and better materials were introduced.

Definition: Photography is the art and science of making pictures by recording light. A camera gathers light from a scene through a lens and records it, first on a chemical film and now on an electronic sensor, producing an image that can be kept, copied, and shared. The first permanent photograph was made by Joseph Nicéphore Niépce in France in 1826 or 1827, using a pewter plate coated with a light-sensitive material, and the exposure took about eight hours. Photography changed how people see the world: it made portraits affordable, it recorded history as it happened, it let scientists see what the eye could not, and it became one of the great art forms. Today billions of photographs are taken every day, most of them on phones.

History: Photography began with the camera obscura, a dark room with a small hole that projected an image of the outside world onto a wall, known since ancient times and used by artists in the Renaissance. The problem was how to fix the image permanently. In the 1820s, Niépce in France coated a pewter plate with bitumen, a tar-like substance that hardened in light, and after a long exposure he washed away the unhardened parts, leaving a permanent image: the "View from the Window at Le Gras", made in 1826 or 1827, is the oldest surviving photograph. In 1839, Louis Daguerre, Niépce's partner, announced the daguerreotype, a process that made sharp images on silvered copper plates in minutes, and in the same year William Henry Fox Talbot in England announced the calotype, which made a negative from which many prints could be made. In the following decades, photography spread: portraits became affordable, the American Civil War and later wars were photographed, and photographers travelled the world. In 1888 George Eastman introduced the Kodak camera, with the slogan "You press the button, we do the rest", and photography became a popular hobby. In the 20th century, colour film, the 35mm camera, and photojournalism made photography universal, and in the 1990s digital cameras replaced film.

Key concepts: Exposure is the amount of light that reaches the film or sensor, controlled by the shutter speed and the aperture; too little light gives a dark image, too much gives a pale one. A daguerreotype is an early photographic process that made a single sharp image on a silvered plate, with no negative. A lens is a piece of glass that focuses light to form an image; the camera's lens determines how sharp and how wide the picture is. Film is a material coated with light-sensitive chemicals; digital sensors replace film with electronic cells that record light as data. A pixel is the smallest unit of a digital image; a modern phone camera records tens of millions of pixels.

How it works: A camera works by gathering light and recording it. Light from the scene passes through the lens, which focuses it onto the film or the sensor at the back of the camera. The shutter opens for a fraction of a second, letting the light in, and the aperture, an adjustable opening, controls how much light enters. In a film camera, the light causes a chemical change in the silver halide crystals on the film; when the film is developed, the crystals that received light turn dark, forming a negative, and a print is made by shining light through the negative onto photographic paper. In a digital camera, the sensor is covered in millions of tiny cells, each of which records the brightness and colour of the light that falls on it, and the camera's processor turns those readings into a digital image file. The lens, the shutter, and the aperture work together: a wide aperture and a fast shutter suit a moving subject, while a narrow aperture and a slow shutter suit a still landscape.

Worked examples: Niépce's "View from the Window at Le Gras" is the founding example: a grainy image of rooftops and a tree, made in 1826 or 1827 with an exposure of about eight hours, and the oldest surviving photograph. The daguerreotype portrait is a social example: in the 1840s and 1850s, studios in every city made portraits that ordinary people could afford, for the first time in history. The photographs of the American Civil War, by Mathew Brady and others, are a historical example: they showed the public the reality of war, and they changed how people understood it. The Kodak camera of 1888 is a popular example: it was simple, cheap, and came with film already loaded, and it made photography a hobby for millions. The "Earthrise" photograph of 1968, taken by the Apollo 8 astronauts, is a cultural example: it showed the Earth rising over the Moon, and it became one of the most famous images ever made.

Real applications: Photography's first application was portraiture: it made likenesses affordable, and it replaced painted portraits for most people. Its second application was journalism and history: photographs recorded wars, elections, disasters, and daily life, and they became the main way people learned what was happening in the world. Its third application was science: photographs recorded the stars, the Moon, and microscopic life, and they let scientists study what the eye could not see. Its fourth application was art: photographers such as Ansel Adams and Henri Cartier-Bresson made photography into a fine art, and their images are in museums around the world. Finally, photography is now universal: billions of photographs are taken every day on phones, shared on social media, and used for identification, medicine, and commerce.

No one person did: Niépce made the first permanent photograph, Daguerre made the process practical, and Talbot invented the negative, and all three contributed. What is the oldest photograph? Niépce's "View from the Window at Le Gras", made in 1826 or 1827, is the oldest surviving photograph; the exact year is uncertain. Why did early photographs take so long? Because the light-sensitive materials were slow, and the exposure had to last for minutes or hours to record enough light. Is film photography dead? No, it is still used by artists and enthusiasts, though digital cameras have replaced it for most purposes. What is the difference between a negative and a print? The negative reverses light and dark, and the print, made from the negative, restores the scene; digital cameras have no negative, they record the image directly as data.

The Telephone

Definition: The telephone is a device that carries the human voice over a distance, by turning sound into an electrical signal, sending it along a wire or through the air, and turning it back into sound at the other end. The first practical telephone was patented by Alexander Graham Bell in 1876 in the United States, though the Italian inventor Antonio Meucci had built a similar device earlier, and the American Elisha Gray filed a patent on the same day as Bell, so the question of who invented the telephone is disputed. The telephone changed how people communicate: for the first time, two people could talk in real time across a city, a country, or an ocean, and it became the foundation of the modern communications network, leading to the mobile phone and the internet.

History: The telephone grew out of the telegraph, which sent coded messages along wires. In the 1870s, several inventors were trying to send the human voice along a wire, including Antonio Meucci in New York, who built a "telettrofono" in the 1850s and filed a caveat, a notice of intent, in 1871, and Elisha Gray in Chicago, who filed a patent caveat on 14 February 1876, the same day Bell filed his patent. Bell's patent was granted on 7 March 1876, and on 10 March he made the first intelligible telephone call, to his assistant Thomas Watson, saying, "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you." The first telephone exchange opened in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1878, and within a few years telephones were spreading across the United States and Europe. In 1915 the first transcontinental call was made, from New York to San Francisco, and in 1927 the first transatlantic call was made by radio. In the 20th century, the telephone network grew to cover the world, and in the 1970s and 1980s the mobile phone appeared, freeing the telephone from its wire. In 2002 the United States Congress passed a resolution recognising Meucci's contribution, though Bell is still generally credited with the invention.

Key concepts: A transmitter is the part of the telephone that turns sound into an electrical signal; in Bell's telephone, a thin metal diaphragm vibrated with the voice and varied the current. A receiver is the part that turns the electrical signal back into sound, using an electromagnet to vibrate a diaphragm. An exchange is a central office that connects callers to each other; early exchanges were staffed by operators who plugged in cords, and later they were automated. A trunk line is a long-distance connection between exchanges, which lets a call travel from one city to another. A mobile phone is a telephone that sends its signal by radio to a nearby base station, instead of along a wire. A network is the system of wires, exchanges, and radio links that connects all telephones together.

How it works: A telephone works by turning sound into electricity and back again. When a person speaks into the transmitter, the sound waves vibrate a thin diaphragm, and the diaphragm varies the electrical current in the wire, so the current rises and falls in the same pattern as the sound. That current travels along the wire, or by radio in a mobile phone, to the receiver at the other end, where an electromagnet turns the varying current back into vibrations of a second diaphragm, which makes sound waves that the listener hears. The exchange connects the two telephones: when a caller dials a number, the exchange finds the line of the person being called and rings it, and when the call is answered, the exchange connects the two lines. In a modern mobile phone, the voice is turned into digital data, sent by radio to a base station, carried across the network, and turned back into sound at the other end. The principle is the same as Bell's: sound becomes signal, signal travels, signal becomes sound.

Worked examples: Bell's first call of 10 March 1876 is the founding example: "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you", spoken in Bell's laboratory in Boston to Watson in the next room. The first telephone exchange, opened in New Haven in 1878, is a network example: it had 21 subscribers, and an operator connected their calls by plugging cords into a switchboard. The first transcontinental call of 1915 is a distance example: Bell, in New York, called Watson in San Francisco, 5,500 kilometres away, and repeated his first words, "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you." The first mobile phone call of 1973, made by Martin Cooper of Motorola, is a wireless example: he called a rival at Bell Labs from a handheld phone on a street in New York. The smartphone is the modern example: it is a telephone, a camera, a computer, and an internet terminal in one device, used by billions of people.

Real applications: The telephone's first application was business: it let merchants, banks, and factories communicate instantly, and it sped up commerce. Its second application was personal: it let families and friends talk across distances, and it became a part of daily life. Its third application was emergency services: the telephone made it possible to call for help, and the emergency number, 911 in the United States and 999 in Britain, was built on it. Its fourth application was in government and the military: telephones connected offices, bases, and ships, and they were essential to the conduct of war and diplomacy. Finally, the telephone network became the foundation of the internet: the same wires that carried voices were used to carry data, and the mobile phone became the main way people access the internet today.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the telephone? Alexander Graham Bell is generally credited, because his patent was granted first, but Antonio Meucci built a similar device earlier, and Elisha Gray filed on the same day; the question is disputed, and the United States Congress recognised Meucci in 2002. What were Bell's first words? "Mr. Watson, come here, I want to see you", spoken to his assistant Thomas Watson on 10 March 1876. Why is the telephone important? Because it was the first device that let two people talk in real time over a distance, and it became the basis of the modern communications network. Is the landline telephone dead? No, it is still used in homes, offices, and for emergency services, though mobile phones have replaced it for most personal calls. What is the difference between a telephone and a telegraph? The telegraph sent coded messages that had to be written down, while the telephone carries the voice itself, in real time.

Definition: The electric light bulb is a device that produces light by passing an electric current through a filament, a thin wire that glows when it is heated, inside a glass bulb from which the air has been removed. The first practical incandescent bulb was made by Thomas Edison in the United States in 1879, though the Englishman Joseph Swan had made a working bulb earlier, and others had tried before them, so the invention is shared and disputed. The light bulb changed the world: it made light cheap, safe, and available at the flick of a switch, it extended the working day into the night, and it made electric power useful in every home. Today the incandescent bulb is being replaced by the LED, which is far more efficient, but the bulb remains one of the great symbols of invention.

History: The idea of electric light is older than Edison: in the early 19th century, Humphry Davy made an arc lamp, which produced light from an electric spark, and in the 1840s several inventors tried to make a bulb with a heated filament, but the filaments burned out quickly. In 1878 Joseph Swan in England made a bulb with a carbonised paper filament that worked, and in 1879 Thomas Edison in the United States, after testing thousands of materials, made a bulb with a carbonised cotton filament that burned for about 13 hours; by 1880 his bulbs lasted for hundreds of hours. Edison and Swan, after a legal dispute, formed a joint company in Britain, and Edison built the first power stations to supply his bulbs, opening the Pearl Street station in New York in 1882. In the 20th century, the tungsten filament, introduced in 1906, made bulbs brighter and longer-lasting, and the light bulb spread to every home, factory, and street. In the 21st century, the LED, a semiconductor that produces light efficiently, has replaced the incandescent bulb in most uses, and many countries have phased out incandescent bulbs to save energy.

Key concepts: A filament is a thin wire that glows when an electric current passes through it; in Edison's bulb it was carbonised cotton, and later it was tungsten. Incandescence is the production of light by heating a material until it glows; the hotter the filament, the brighter the light, and the shorter its life. A vacuum is a space with no air; Edison's bulb had the air removed so the filament would not burn up in oxygen. A socket is the fitting that holds the bulb and connects it to the power supply; Edison's screw socket is still used today. How it works: An incandescent bulb works by heating a filament until it glows. When an electric current passes through the filament, the filament's resistance turns the electrical energy into heat, and the filament becomes so hot, about 2,500 degrees Celsius, that it gives off light. The filament is inside a glass bulb from which the air has been removed, or which is filled with an inert gas such as argon, so that the hot filament does not burn up in oxygen. The bulb is screwed into a socket, which connects the filament to the power supply, and when the switch is turned on, the current flows and the bulb lights. The problem with the incandescent bulb is that most of the energy becomes heat, not light: only about 5 percent of the electricity becomes visible light, and the rest is wasted as heat. An LED works differently: it is a semiconductor, and when current passes through it, electrons and holes combine and release energy as light, with far less heat, which is why LEDs use about 80 percent less energy than incandescent bulbs.

Worked examples: Edison's bulb of 1879 is the founding example: a carbonised cotton filament in a vacuum that burned for about 13 hours, and then for hundreds of hours as Edison improved it. The Pearl Street station of 1882 is a system example: Edison's power station in New York supplied electricity to about 400 bulbs in the first district, and it showed that electric light needed a whole system, not just a bulb. The tungsten bulb of 1906 is an improving example: a tungsten filament lasted longer and gave more light, and it became the standard bulb for most of the 20th century. The LED is the modern example: a small semiconductor that produces bright light with little heat, and which now lights homes, streets, and screens.

Real applications: The light bulb's first application was the home: it replaced candles, oil lamps, and gas lights, which were dim, dirty, and dangerous, and it made the home brighter and safer. Its second application was the factory: electric light let factories work at night, and it made shift work possible, which increased production. Its third application was the street: electric street lights made cities safer at night and changed urban life. Its fourth application was in transport: headlights, signals, and station lights made travel safer. Finally, the light bulb made electric power useful: the demand for light drove the building of power stations and grids, which then supplied motors, radios, and all the other electrical devices that followed. The bulb is also a symbol: a light bulb above the head means an idea, and it is the universal image of invention.

Thomas Edison is generally credited, but Joseph Swan made a working bulb earlier, and many others contributed; Edison's achievement was making a practical, long-lasting bulb and building the system to power it. Yes, though LEDs have replaced it in most homes, incandescent bulbs are still used in some places, and the screw socket Edison designed is still standard. What is the difference between a bulb and an LED? A bulb makes light by heating a filament, while an LED makes light with a semiconductor, which is far more efficient.

The Airplane

ID: JAH-IVNT-000014 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The airplane is a machine that flies through the air, using wings to produce lift and an engine to provide thrust. The first powered, controlled, sustained flight was made by the Wright brothers, Wilbur and Orville Wright, on 17 December 1903 at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina, in their Flyer, a wood-and-fabric biplane with a petrol engine. The flight lasted 12 seconds and covered 36 metres, but it proved that a heavier-than-air machine could fly, and within a few decades the airplane had changed transport, warfare, and the world. Today airplanes carry billions of passengers a year, and they are the fastest way to travel long distances. The claim of the first flight is disputed: the Brazilian Alberto Santos-Dumont made a public flight in Paris in 1906, and others claimed earlier flights, but the Wrights' 1903 flight is the one generally accepted as the first powered, controlled flight.

History: Humans dreamed of flying for centuries, and the first attempts were gliders and balloons. In the 19th century, Otto Lilienthal in Germany made hundreds of glider flights, and Samuel Langley in the United States built powered models, but no one had flown a powered machine under control. The Wright brothers, bicycle makers from Ohio, studied Lilienthal's work, built a wind tunnel, and tested gliders at Kitty Hawk, where the winds were steady. On 17 December 1903, Orville Wright flew the Flyer for 12 seconds, and Wilbur flew it later that day for 59 seconds, covering 260 metres. The news spread slowly, and it took years for the world to accept the achievement, but by 1908 the Wrights were demonstrating their airplane in Europe and the United States. In the First World War, airplanes were used for reconnaissance and then for fighting, and in the 1920s and 1930s they began to carry mail and passengers. In 1927 Charles Lindbergh flew solo across the Atlantic, and in the 1950s the jet airliner made air travel fast and common. Today, airplanes from small propeller planes to giant jets carry people and cargo around the world.

Key concepts: Lift is the upward force produced by a wing as air flows over it; it must be greater than the airplane's weight for the plane to fly. Thrust is the forward force produced by the engine, whether a propeller or a jet; it must overcome drag. Drag is the resistance of the air against the airplane's motion. A wing is a shaped surface, called an airfoil, that produces lift because air moves faster over its curved top than under its flat bottom, creating lower pressure above. A propeller is a rotating blade that pulls or pushes the airplane forward; a jet engine produces thrust by expelling hot gas backwards. Control surfaces are the moving parts of the wings and tail — ailerons, elevators, and rudders — that let the pilot steer the airplane.

How it works: An airplane flies because its wings produce lift. As the engine drives the airplane forward, air flows over the wings; the wing's shape makes the air move faster over the top than underneath, which lowers the pressure above the wing and raises it below, and the difference pushes the wing up. The engine provides thrust, which overcomes the drag of the air and keeps the airplane moving forward; the faster it moves, the more lift the wings produce, until the lift equals the weight and the airplane flies level. The pilot controls the airplane with the control surfaces: the ailerons on the wings roll the plane, the elevators on the tail pitch it up or down, and the rudder turns it left or right. In a jet airplane, the engine sucks in air, compresses it, burns fuel in it, and expels the hot gas backwards at high speed, and the reaction pushes the airplane forward. The airplane's systems — fuel, navigation, communication, and landing gear — all support the basic task: lift, thrust, and control.

Worked examples: The Wright Flyer of 1903 is the founding example: a biplane with a 12-horsepower engine, a wingspan of 12 metres, and a flight of 12 seconds that changed history. The Spirit of St. Louis is a distance example: in 1927 Charles Lindbergh flew it alone from New York to Paris, 5,800 kilometres, in 33 hours, and became world-famous. The Douglas DC-3 is a commercial example: introduced in the 1930s, it was the first airliner to make passenger flight profitable, and it is still flying today. The Boeing 707 is a jet example: introduced in the late 1950s, it made jet travel common and cut the time of long flights in half. The Airbus A380 is a size example: the largest passenger airplane, with two full decks and room for more than 500 passengers, it shows how far the airplane has come from the Wright Flyer.

Real applications: The airplane's first application was warfare: in the First World War, airplanes were used for observation and fighting, and in the Second World War they became decisive, bombing cities and carrying troops. Its second application was transport: airplanes carry passengers and cargo around the world, and they are the fastest way to travel long distances. Its third application was in mail and communication: airmail connected continents, and the airplane made the world smaller. Its fourth application was in science and exploration: airplanes survey the land, study the atmosphere, and fight forest fires. Finally, the airplane changed culture: it made tourism possible on a global scale, it connected families across oceans, and it became a symbol of the modern age. Its costs — pollution and noise — are also part of its story, and they drive research into cleaner aircraft.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the airplane? The Wright brothers are generally credited with the first powered, controlled, sustained flight, on 17 December 1903, though Santos-Dumont and others made claims, and the question is disputed in Brazil, where Santos-Dumont is honoured as the inventor. How does a wing produce lift? Because air moves faster over the curved top of the wing than under it, creating lower pressure above, which lifts the wing; the full explanation also involves the wing pushing air downwards, and scientists still debate the simplest way to describe it. How fast can an airplane fly? A passenger jet cruises at about 900 kilometres per hour, and the fastest airplane, the SR-71, flew at more than 3,500 kilometres per hour. Why is the sky the limit? Because the airplane needs air for lift and for its engines, and above the atmosphere it cannot fly; spacecraft take over where airplanes stop. Is flying safe? Yes, by the numbers: air travel is one of the safest forms of transport, with far fewer deaths per kilometre than road travel.

Radio

ID: JAH-IVNT-000015 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Radio is a way of sending information through the air using electromagnetic waves, without wires. A transmitter turns sound or data into radio waves, an antenna sends them out, and a receiver picks them up and turns them back into sound or data. Radio was developed in the 1890s, and the Italian inventor Guglielmo Marconi is generally credited with making the first practical wireless system, though the Russian Alexander Popov, the German Heinrich Hertz, and the Serbian-American Nikola Tesla all contributed, so the invention is shared and disputed. Radio changed the world: it let ships call for help, it brought news and music into every home, and it became the basis of television, mobile phones, and the wireless internet.

History: Radio began with the science of electromagnetism: in the 1860s James Clerk Maxwell predicted that electromagnetic waves exist, and in the 1880s Heinrich Hertz proved it by making and detecting them in his laboratory. In the 1890s, several inventors tried to use the waves for communication, including Alexander Popov in Russia, who demonstrated a receiver in 1895, and Guglielmo Marconi in Italy, who sent signals over increasing distances, reaching across the English Channel in 1899 and across the Atlantic in 1901, when he received the letter "S" in Morse code in Newfoundland. In the 1900s, radio was used by ships, and in 1912 the distress calls from the Titanic were heard by nearby ships, though the nearest ship's operator had gone to bed. In the 1920s, broadcasting began: stations in the United States and Britain started regular programmes of news, music, and drama, and the radio became the main medium of mass communication. In the 1930s and 1940s, radio was the voice of war and of politics, and in the second half of the 20th century, television, FM radio, and then mobile phones and Wi-Fi all grew from it.

Key concepts: An electromagnetic wave is a wave of electric and magnetic energy that travels through space at the speed of light; radio waves are one kind, with long wavelengths. A transmitter is a device that turns sound or data into radio waves and sends them out through an antenna. A receiver is a device that picks up radio waves with an antenna and turns them back into sound or data. Frequency is how many waves pass a point each second, measured in hertz; radio stations are identified by their frequency. Amplitude modulation, or AM, varies the strength of the wave to carry sound, while frequency modulation, or FM, varies the frequency; FM gives clearer sound. Morse code is a system of dots and dashes that represents letters, used in early radio telegraphy.

How it works: Radio works by turning information into electromagnetic waves. In a transmitter, a microphone turns sound into an electrical signal, and the signal is used to modulate, or vary, a radio wave, which is a wave of a chosen frequency; in AM radio, the wave's strength is varied, and in FM radio, its frequency is varied. The modulated wave is fed to an antenna, which radiates it into space, where it travels at the speed of light in all directions. At the receiver, an antenna picks up the wave, a tuner selects the frequency of the station, and a demodulator extracts the original signal, which is amplified and sent to a loudspeaker, where it becomes sound again. The range of a radio signal depends on its power and its frequency: long waves travel far and follow the Earth's curve, while short waves bounce off the ionosphere, a layer of the upper atmosphere, and can travel around the world. Modern radio uses digital signals, in which the sound is turned into numbers and sent as a stream of data, which is how digital radio, mobile phones, and Wi-Fi work.

Worked examples: Marconi's transatlantic signal of 1901 is the founding example: the letter "S" in Morse code, sent from Cornwall in England and received in Newfoundland, proved that radio waves could cross an ocean. The Titanic's distress calls of 1912 are a rescue example: the ship's radio operator sent "CQD" and "SOS", and the nearby ship Carpathia heard them and rescued survivors, though the Californian, which was closer, had switched its radio off. The first broadcast stations of the 1920s are a mass-media example: KDKA in Pittsburgh, which began in 1920, and the BBC, which began in 1922, brought news, music, and drama into homes. The wartime broadcasts of the 1940s are a political example: leaders such as Winston Churchill and Franklin Roosevelt spoke to their nations by radio, and the BBC's broadcasts were heard across occupied Europe. Wi-Fi is a modern example: it is radio, used to connect computers and phones to the internet without wires.

Real applications: Radio's first application was at sea: it let ships communicate with the shore and with each other, and it saved lives by carrying distress calls. Its second application was broadcasting: radio brought news, music, drama, and sport into every home, and it became the first mass medium. Its third application was in the military: radio connected armies, navies, and air forces, and it was essential to the conduct of war. Its fourth application was in navigation and science: radio beacons guide ships and aircraft, and radio telescopes study the universe. Finally, radio is the basis of modern wireless communication: mobile phones, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and satellite links are all radio, and they carry the internet to billions of people. Without radio, there would be no mobile phone, no Wi-Fi, and no wireless world.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented radio? Guglielmo Marconi is generally credited with the first practical system, but Alexander Popov, Nikola Tesla, and Heinrich Hertz all contributed, and the invention is shared and disputed. What is the difference between AM and FM? AM varies the strength of the radio wave, while FM varies its frequency; FM gives clearer sound and is less affected by interference, while AM travels further. How far can radio waves travel? They travel at the speed of light, and with enough power they can cross oceans and reach spacecraft; the signals from the Voyager probes, billions of kilometres away, are still received. Why is radio still used? Because it is simple, reliable, and free to receive, and because it works where other systems fail, in emergencies and in remote areas. Is Wi-Fi radio? Yes, Wi-Fi is radio waves used to carry digital data between devices and the internet.

The Refrigerator

ID: JAH-IVNT-000016 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The refrigerator is a machine that keeps food cold, by removing heat from an insulated box and releasing it outside. It is one of the most important inventions of the home, because it lets people store perishable food safely for days or weeks, instead of eating it at once or preserving it with salt, smoke, or ice. The first domestic electric refrigerators appeared in the 1910s: Fred W. Wolf built the first in 1913, and the Kelvinator company sold the first widely in 1918. Before the refrigerator, people used iceboxes, cooled by blocks of ice, and before that, cellars, wells, and winter ice. Today the refrigerator is in almost every home in the developed world, and it is essential to the food system, from farms and factories to shops and kitchens.

History: Keeping food cold is ancient: the Romans stored food in snow, the Chinese harvested ice in winter, and in the 19th century the ice trade, in which ice was cut from frozen lakes and shipped around the world, was a major industry. The first machines to make cold artificially appeared in the 19th century: in 1834 Jacob Perkins patented a vapour-compression refrigerator, and in the 1850s James Harrison in Australia built a machine that made ice for breweries and meat packing. The first domestic refrigerator was built by Fred W. Wolf in 1913, and it was a large, expensive machine; in 1918 the Kelvinator company began selling refrigerators for the home, and in the 1920s Frigidaire, owned by General Motors, made them more affordable. The early refrigerators used dangerous gases, such as ammonia and sulfur dioxide, which could leak and poison people, and in the 1930s the safer Freon was introduced, though it was later found to damage the ozone layer and was replaced. By the 1950s the refrigerator was standard in American homes, and it spread around the world in the following decades, changing how people shopped, cooked, and ate.

Key concepts: Refrigeration is the removal of heat from a space, making it colder than its surroundings. A refrigerant is a fluid that carries heat: it evaporates inside the refrigerator, absorbing heat, and condenses outside, releasing it. A compressor is a pump that squeezes the refrigerant gas, raising its temperature and pressure. An evaporator is a coil inside the refrigerator where the refrigerant evaporates and absorbs heat from the food. A condenser is a coil at the back or underneath, where the refrigerant condenses and releases heat into the room. An icebox is a box cooled by a block of ice, the refrigerator's predecessor.

How it works: A refrigerator works by moving heat, not by making cold. Inside the refrigerator, a refrigerant, a fluid that boils at a low temperature, evaporates in the evaporator coils, and as it evaporates it absorbs heat from the food and the air inside, making the box cold. The compressor then pumps the refrigerant gas and squeezes it, which raises its temperature and pressure, and the hot gas flows through the condenser coils at the back, where it releases its heat into the room and condenses back into a liquid. The liquid then passes through a narrow valve, which drops its pressure and temperature, and it returns to the evaporator to begin the cycle again. The cycle repeats continuously, powered by electricity, and the thermostat switches the compressor on and off to keep the temperature steady, usually about 4 degrees Celsius in the fridge and minus 18 in the freezer. The refrigerator does not destroy heat; it moves it from inside the box to outside, which is why the back of a refrigerator feels warm.

Worked examples: The icebox is the predecessor example: a wooden box with a block of ice, delivered by the iceman, which kept food cold for a few days and was standard in homes before the 1920s. The Kelvinator of 1918 is a domestic example: one of the first refrigerators sold for the home, it was expensive and noisy, but it showed that electric refrigeration could work in a kitchen. The Frigidaire of the 1920s is a mass-market example: made by General Motors, it brought the price down and made the refrigerator common in American homes. The modern frost-free refrigerator is a convenience example: it defrosts itself automatically, and it has separate compartments for fresh food and frozen food. The walk-in cold room is an industrial example: large refrigerators in warehouses, supermarkets, and food factories keep tonnes of food cold, and they are essential to the modern food system.

Real applications: The refrigerator's first application was the home: it let families store milk, meat, and vegetables safely, shop less often, and waste less food. Its second application was in the food industry: refrigerated transport, in ships, trains, and trucks, let fresh food travel around the world, and it made possible the modern supermarket. Its third application was in medicine: refrigerators store vaccines, blood, and medicines at the right temperature, and they are essential to hospitals and to vaccination programmes. Its fourth application was in science: laboratories use refrigerators and freezers to store samples, and ultra-cold freezers keep biological material at minus 80 degrees. Finally, the refrigerator changed how people eat: it made possible the frozen meal, the chilled drink, and the weekly shop, and it is one of the appliances that define modern life.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the refrigerator? No one person did: Jacob Perkins patented the first vapour-compression machine in 1834, James Harrison built early commercial machines in the 1850s, and Fred W. Wolf built the first domestic refrigerator in 1913. Why is the back of a refrigerator warm? Because the refrigerator moves heat from inside the box to outside, and the condenser coils at the back release that heat into the room. Is the refrigerator bad for the environment? Early refrigerants damaged the ozone layer, and modern ones can contribute to warming if they leak, so new refrigerants are being developed, and old refrigerators must be disposed of carefully. How cold should a refrigerator be? About 4 degrees Celsius in the fridge and minus 18 in the freezer, which keeps food safe without freezing it. Will the refrigerator ever be replaced? Probably not soon: keeping food cold is a basic need, and the refrigerator is the simplest way to do it, though its design continues to improve.

Television

ID: JAH-IVNT-000017 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Television is a system for sending moving pictures and sound through the air or along a cable, so that people can watch events as they happen, far away. The word comes from the Greek "tele", meaning far, and the Latin "visio", meaning sight. Television was invented in the 1920s: the Scottish engineer John Logie Baird demonstrated the first working mechanical television in 1926, and the American Philo Farnsworth demonstrated the first fully electronic television in 1927, so the invention is shared and the question of who was first is disputed. Television changed the world: it brought news, entertainment, and sport into the home, it became the main medium of mass communication for much of the 20th century, and it shaped politics, culture, and daily life.

History: Television grew out of radio and photography: the idea was to send pictures as radio sent sound. In the 1920s, John Logie Baird in London built a mechanical television that used a spinning disc with holes to scan an image, and in January 1926 he demonstrated it to the Royal Institution, showing the face of a ventriloquist's dummy; in 1928 he sent the first television pictures across the Atlantic. At the same time, Philo Farnsworth in the United States, working alone, built the first fully electronic television, using a camera tube he called the image dissector, and in 1927 he transmitted the first electronic image, a line. In the 1930s, the BBC in Britain began the world's first regular television service, in 1936, and in the United States the first regular broadcasts began in 1939. Television spread slowly at first, because sets were expensive, but after the Second World War it grew rapidly: by the 1960s, most homes in the developed world had a television. In the 1950s colour television appeared, in the 1960s satellite television let signals cross oceans, and in the 1990s digital television and the flat screen replaced the old cathode-ray tube. Today television is watched on sets, computers, and phones, and it is streamed over the internet.

Key concepts: A camera tube is a device that turns light into an electrical signal; Farnsworth's image dissector was the first, and later tubes such as the iconoscope were used in studios. A cathode-ray tube, or CRT, is a vacuum tube in which a beam of electrons paints the picture on a screen; it was the display of most televisions for 70 years. Scanning is the process of breaking an image into lines and sending them one by one; early television used 30 lines, and modern television uses more than 1,000. Broadcasting is the sending of television signals from a transmitter to many receivers at once. A satellite is a spacecraft that relays television signals around the world. Streaming is the sending of television over the internet, watched on demand rather than at a scheduled time.

How it works: Television works by turning a picture into a signal, sending it, and turning it back into a picture. In the camera, light from the scene falls on a sensor, which turns it into an electrical signal; the signal describes the brightness and colour of each point in the image, line by line, many times a second. The signal is sent by radio waves, by cable, or over the internet to the receiver, where the television's circuits decode it and rebuild the image on the screen, line by line, so quickly that the eye sees a moving picture. In the old cathode-ray tube, a beam of electrons swept across the screen, lighting up phosphors that glowed in the right colours; in a modern flat screen, millions of tiny liquid crystals or LEDs change their brightness and colour to form the image. The sound is sent alongside the picture, as a separate signal, and the television's speakers play it in time with the images. The frame rate, usually 25 or 30 pictures a second, is fast enough that the eye sees smooth motion.

Worked examples: Baird's demonstration of 1926 is the founding example: a mechanical television that showed a face, using a spinning disc and 30 lines, in a small room in London. Farnsworth's transmission of 1927 is the electronic example: the first fully electronic image, a straight line, sent in his laboratory in San Francisco. The BBC's service of 1936 is a broadcasting example: the world's first regular, high-definition television service, broadcast from Alexandra Palace in London. The coronation of Queen Elizabeth II in 1953 is a mass-audience example: it was watched by millions in Britain, and it made television the nation's main medium. The Moon landing of 1969 is a global example: about 600 million people watched the Apollo 11 astronauts walk on the Moon, the largest television audience in history to that point.

Real applications: Television's first application was entertainment: it brought drama, comedy, music, and sport into the home, and it became the main leisure activity of the 20th century. Its second application was news: television showed events as they happened, from wars to Moon landings, and it changed how people understood the world. Its third application was education: schools and universities used television to teach, and children's programmes became a part of growing up. Its fourth application was advertising: television commercials became the main way companies reached customers. Finally, television changed politics: leaders spoke directly to voters on television, and the televised debate between Kennedy and Nixon in 1960 is often said to have decided the American election. Today television is joined by streaming, but the moving picture remains the most powerful medium.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented television? John Logie Baird demonstrated the first mechanical television in 1926, and Philo Farnsworth demonstrated the first electronic television in 1927; both contributed, and the question of who was first is disputed. What is the difference between mechanical and electronic television? Mechanical television used a spinning disc to scan the image, while electronic television used a camera tube and a cathode-ray tube, which gave a better picture and became the standard. Why is television called the "idiot box"? It is a nickname, used by critics who thought television made people passive, though others argue it educated and informed them. Is television dying? The old scheduled broadcast is declining, but the moving picture is more popular than ever, watched on phones, computers, and streaming services. What was the first television programme? The BBC's first regular service in 1936 included news, drama, and variety, and the first programme is usually given as a variety show on 2 November 1936.

Penicillin

ID: JAH-IVNT-000018 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: Penicillin is an antibiotic, a medicine that kills bacteria, and it was the first antibiotic to be used widely. It is made from a mould, Penicillium, and it was discovered by Alexander Fleming in London in 1928, when he noticed that a mould growing on a dish of bacteria had killed the bacteria around it. Penicillin changed medicine: before it, a small cut or a sore throat could kill, and diseases such as pneumonia, blood poisoning, and infected wounds were often fatal; after it, those diseases could be treated, and millions of lives were saved. Penicillin was the first of the antibiotics, and it opened the age of modern medicine, though the rise of bacteria that resist it is now a serious problem.

History: The story begins in September 1928, when Alexander Fleming, a bacteriologist at St Mary's Hospital in London, returned from holiday and found that a dish of Staphylococcus bacteria had been contaminated by a mould, Penicillium notatum, and that the bacteria around the mould had been killed. Fleming named the active substance penicillin, and he published his findings in 1929, but he could not make enough of it to use as a medicine, and the discovery was largely forgotten. In the late 1930s, a team at Oxford University, led by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, took up the work: they found a way to produce penicillin in quantity, tested it on animals, and in 1941 treated the first patient, a policeman named Albert Alexander, whose infection improved dramatically before the supply ran out and he died. The team then worked with American companies to mass-produce penicillin, using a different mould, Penicillium chrysogenum, found on a melon in Illinois, and by 1944 penicillin was available to Allied troops in the Second World War, saving countless lives. Fleming, Florey, and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945. Since then, penicillin and its relatives have been used to treat millions of people, and other antibiotics have followed.

Key concepts: An antibiotic is a medicine that kills or stops the growth of bacteria; penicillin was the first to be used widely. A bacterium is a single-celled organism; some bacteria cause disease, and penicillin kills them by stopping them from building their cell walls. A mould is a fungus that grows in a fuzzy layer; Penicillium is the mould that produces penicillin. A cell wall is the rigid outer layer of a bacterium; penicillin weakens it, so the bacterium bursts. Antibiotic resistance is the ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic, which develops when the medicine is overused; it is a growing problem. A clinical trial is a test of a medicine on patients; the first trials of penicillin were done at Oxford in 1941.

How it works: Penicillin works by attacking the cell wall of bacteria. Bacteria are surrounded by a rigid wall made of a material called peptidoglycan, which protects them and keeps their shape. When a bacterium grows and divides, it must build new wall, and penicillin blocks the enzymes that build it, by binding to proteins called penicillin-binding proteins. Without a proper wall, the bacterium cannot hold its shape, and the pressure inside it makes it burst and die. Human cells have no cell wall, only a membrane, so penicillin does not harm them in the same way, which is why it can kill bacteria without killing the patient. Penicillin is most effective against bacteria that are actively growing, and it is used to treat infections such as pneumonia, strep throat, and syphilis. The problem is resistance: some bacteria have evolved enzymes, called beta-lactamases, that break down penicillin, and they pass the resistance to their offspring, which is why doctors are careful about how they prescribe antibiotics.

Worked examples: Fleming's dish of 1928 is the founding example: a mould-contaminated dish in which the bacteria had been killed, a chance observation that Fleming recognised as important. The Oxford team's work of 1938-1941 is a development example: Florey and Chain found a way to purify penicillin and proved it worked in animals and then in people, turning a laboratory curiosity into a medicine. The first patient, Albert Alexander, is a human example: a policeman with a severe infection who improved on penicillin in 1941, though he died when the supply ran out, showing both the drug's power and the need for mass production. The mass production in the United States is an industrial example: by 1944, American companies were making penicillin in large fermenters, and it was available to troops in the Second World War. The rise of resistance is a modern example: the bacterium MRSA, which resists penicillin and related drugs, is a major problem in hospitals, and it shows why antibiotics must be used carefully.

Real applications: Penicillin's first application was in treating infections: it made pneumonia, blood poisoning, and infected wounds treatable, and it saved millions of lives. Its second application was in surgery: with antibiotics, surgeons could operate more safely, because infections after surgery could be treated. Its third application was in childbirth: antibiotics reduced the deaths of mothers and babies from infection. Its fourth application was in public health: penicillin was used to treat syphilis and other diseases on a large scale, and it helped to control epidemics. Finally, penicillin opened the age of antibiotics: it led to the discovery of other antibiotics, such as streptomycin and tetracycline, and it changed medicine from a craft that could do little for infection into a science that could cure it. The lesson of penicillin is also a warning: the overuse of antibiotics creates resistant bacteria, and the world must use them carefully.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who discovered penicillin? Alexander Fleming discovered it in 1928, but Howard Florey and Ernst Chain developed it into a medicine in the 1940s; all three shared the Nobel Prize in 1945. Why did it take so long to use penicillin? Because Fleming could not produce enough of it, and it took the Oxford team a decade to find a way to purify and mass-produce it. Is penicillin still used? Yes, it is still one of the most widely used antibiotics, though some bacteria have become resistant to it. Why are some people allergic to penicillin? Because their immune system reacts to the drug, and the allergy can be serious; doctors ask about it before prescribing. What is antibiotic resistance? It is the ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic, which develops when the medicine is overused; it is one of the greatest challenges in modern medicine.

Vaccination

Definition: Vaccination is a way of protecting people from infectious disease, by exposing the body to a weakened or harmless form of the germ, so that the immune system learns to recognise it and can fight it off if the real disease arrives. The first vaccine was for smallpox, developed by Edward Jenner in England in 1796, when he showed that a person infected with cowpox, a mild disease of cattle, was protected against smallpox, a deadly disease. Vaccination is placed here out of strict chronological order: it is far older than the television and penicillin that precede it in this timeline, because Jenner's vaccine came in 1796, more than a century before penicillin. Vaccination has saved more lives than any other medical invention except clean water: it has wiped out smallpox, nearly wiped out polio, and it protects billions of people from measles, tetanus, and many other diseases.

History: The idea of vaccination is older than Jenner: in China and India, people practised variolation, in which material from a smallpox sore was scratched into the skin, giving a mild case of the disease and protection afterwards, and the practice reached Europe in the early 18th century through Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, who had seen it in Turkey. In 1796, Edward Jenner, a country doctor in Gloucestershire, tested a safer idea: he took material from a cowpox sore on the hand of a milkmaid, Sarah Nelmes, and scratched it into the arm of an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps; when he later exposed the boy to smallpox, the boy did not become ill. Jenner called the process vaccination, from the Latin "vacca", meaning cow, and he published his findings in 1798. In the 19th century, Louis Pasteur developed vaccines for anthrax and rabies, and in the 20th century vaccines were made for polio, measles, and many other diseases. In 1980 the World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated, the first disease in history to be wiped out, and in the 2020s vaccines against COVID-19 were developed in less than a year, the fastest in history.

Key concepts: A vaccine is a preparation that trains the immune system to recognise a disease, usually made from a weakened or killed germ, or from a part of it. The immune system is the body's defence against disease; it remembers germs it has met, so it can fight them faster the next time. Immunity is the protection against a disease that comes from vaccination or from having had the disease. Herd immunity is the protection of a whole community when enough people are vaccinated that the disease cannot spread. Eradication is the complete wiping out of a disease; smallpox is the only human disease to have been eradicated. A booster is an extra dose of a vaccine, given later to strengthen immunity.

How it works: Vaccination works by teaching the immune system. When a vaccine is given, it introduces a harmless form of the germ, or a part of it, into the body, and the immune system responds by making antibodies, proteins that recognise the germ, and memory cells, which remember it. If the real germ arrives later, the memory cells recognise it at once, and the immune system makes antibodies quickly, destroying the germ before it can cause serious illness. The vaccine itself does not cause the disease, because the germ in it is weakened, killed, or only a fragment, but it is enough for the immune system to learn from. Different vaccines work in different ways: some use a live but weakened germ, some use a killed germ, some use a protein from the germ, and the newest, such as the COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, use a message that tells the body's cells to make a harmless piece of the germ, which the immune system then learns to recognise. When enough people in a community are vaccinated, the disease cannot find new hosts, and even people who cannot be vaccinated are protected, which is herd immunity.

Worked examples: Jenner's smallpox vaccine of 1796 is the founding example: a boy, James Phipps, was given cowpox and then exposed to smallpox without becoming ill, and the experiment, though it would not meet modern ethical standards, proved the principle. Pasteur's rabies vaccine of 1885 is a second example: a boy bitten by a rabid dog was treated with the vaccine and survived, and Pasteur became famous. The polio vaccine is a mass example: Jonas Salk's vaccine of 1955 and Albert Sabin's of 1961 were given to hundreds of millions of children, and polio, which once crippled thousands each year, has been nearly wiped out. The smallpox eradication is a global example: a worldwide campaign, led by the World Health Organization, vaccinated whole populations, and in 1980 smallpox was declared eradicated. The COVID-19 vaccines are a modern example: developed in less than a year, they were given to billions of people and saved millions of lives.

Real applications: Vaccination's first application was against smallpox: it turned a deadly disease into a preventable one, and then wiped it out entirely. Its second application was in childhood: vaccines against measles, mumps, rubella, diphtheria, tetanus, and polio protect children from diseases that once killed or disabled them. Its third application was in travel and work: vaccines protect soldiers, travellers, and health workers from diseases they may meet. Its fourth application was in animals: vaccines protect livestock and pets, and they have wiped out rinderpest, a cattle disease, the second disease to be eradicated. Finally, vaccination is a tool of public health: mass vaccination campaigns protect whole populations, and they are one of the most cost-effective ways to save lives. Vaccination is a matter of history in this record, not medical advice: the science of how vaccines work is described here, and decisions about vaccination are for individuals and their doctors.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented vaccination? Edward Jenner developed the first vaccine, for smallpox, in 1796, building on the older practice of variolation; Louis Pasteur later developed the general principle. Why is vaccination listed after penicillin in this timeline? Because this timeline follows the order given for the program, which is rough rather than exact; vaccination is in fact far older than penicillin, and its history is given here in its true order. Is smallpox really gone? Yes, it was declared eradicated in 1980, and the last natural case was in 1977; samples of the virus are kept in two laboratories for research. Are vaccines safe? Vaccines are tested for safety before they are used, and they are monitored afterwards; like all medicines, they can have side effects, but the diseases they prevent are far more dangerous. What is herd immunity? It is the protection of a community when enough people are vaccinated that the disease cannot spread, which also protects people who cannot be vaccinated.

The Jet Engine

ID: JAH-IVNT-000020 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The jet engine is an engine that produces thrust by taking in air, compressing it, burning fuel in it, and expelling the hot gas backwards at high speed, which pushes the engine, and the aircraft it is attached to, forwards. The jet engine was invented in the 1930s by two men working independently: Frank Whittle in Britain, who patented his design in 1930, and Hans von Ohain in Germany, whose engine powered the first jet flight, of the Heinkel He 178, on 27 August 1939. The jet engine changed aviation: it made airplanes faster, higher, and more reliable than propeller aircraft, and it made the modern airliner possible. Today jet engines power almost all large aircraft, and they are among the most powerful machines ever built.

History: The idea of jet propulsion is ancient: Hero of Alexandria's aeolipile, in the 1st century CE, spun by steam, and in the 18th century the English inventor John Barber described a turbine. In the 1930s, Frank Whittle, a young officer in the Royal Air Force, realised that a gas turbine could power an aircraft, and he patented his design in 1930, though he struggled to get support. At the same time, Hans von Ohain in Germany, working with the Heinkel company, built a jet engine, and on 27 August 1939 his engine powered the Heinkel He 178 on the first jet flight, a short hop that lasted a few minutes. In 1941 Whittle's engine powered the Gloster E.28/39, the first British jet flight, and during the Second World War both Britain and Germany built jet fighters, the Gloster Meteor and the Messerschmitt Me 262. After the war, jet engines were fitted to airliners: the de Havilland Comet, which flew in 1949, was the first jet airliner, and the Boeing 707, which entered service in 1958, made jet travel common. Since then, jet engines have become larger, quieter, and more efficient, and they power aircraft from small business jets to the largest airliners.

Key concepts: Thrust is the forward force produced by a jet engine, by expelling gas backwards at high speed. A compressor is a set of rotating blades that squeezes the incoming air, raising its pressure and temperature. A combustion chamber is where fuel is burned in the compressed air, making hot gas. A turbine is a set of blades turned by the hot gas, which drives the compressor. A turbofan is a jet engine with a large fan at the front, which moves a large volume of air around the core, making the engine quieter and more efficient; most airliners use turbofans. A bypass ratio is the amount of air that goes around the core compared with the amount that goes through it; a high bypass ratio means a quieter, more efficient engine.

How it works: A jet engine works in a continuous cycle. First, air enters the front of the engine, and the fan and the compressor squeeze it, raising its pressure to many times that of the outside air. Second, fuel is sprayed into the compressed air in the combustion chamber and burned, making hot gas at a very high temperature. Third, the hot gas expands through the turbine, a set of blades that spins and drives the compressor, and then it rushes out of the back of the engine at high speed. The reaction to that high-speed exhaust pushes the engine forwards, which is thrust, and the engine pushes the aircraft with it. In a turbofan, most of the thrust comes not from the hot core but from the large fan at the front, which moves a huge volume of air backwards at a lower speed, which is more efficient and quieter. The engine's power is enormous: a single large turbofan can produce more than 50,000 pounds of thrust, and it does so while using fuel efficiently enough to fly an airliner across an ocean.

Worked examples: The Heinkel He 178 of 1939 is the founding example: the first aircraft to fly with a jet engine, powered by von Ohain's engine, on a short flight that proved the idea worked. The Gloster E.28/39 of 1941 is the British example: powered by Whittle's engine, it was the first British jet flight, and it led to the Gloster Meteor, the first Allied jet fighter. The de Havilland Comet of 1949 is the airliner example: the first jet airliner, it cut the time of long flights in half, though it suffered accidents that taught the industry about metal fatigue. The Boeing 707 of 1958 is the mass example: it made jet travel common, and it opened the age of mass air travel. The modern turbofan, such as the GE90 that powers the Boeing 777, is the latest example: it is one of the most powerful jet engines ever built, with a fan more than 3 metres wide, and it is quieter and more efficient than the early jets.

Real applications: The jet engine's first application was military: jet fighters and bombers changed warfare, and they are still the main combat aircraft of every major air force. Its second application was air travel: jet airliners made long-distance travel fast and affordable, and they connected the world. Its third application was in cargo: jet freighters carry goods around the world, and they are essential to global trade. Its fourth application was in power generation: gas turbines, derived from jet engines, generate electricity in power stations and drive ships. Finally, the jet engine changed the world: it made tourism global, it let people live and work across continents, and it shrank the planet. Its costs — noise and carbon emissions — are also part of its story, and they drive research into cleaner engines and alternative fuels.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the jet engine? Frank Whittle in Britain and Hans von Ohain in Germany invented it independently in the 1930s; Whittle patented his design first, in 1930, and von Ohain's engine flew first, in 1939. How does a jet engine work? It takes in air, compresses it, burns fuel in it, and expels the hot gas backwards, and the reaction pushes the engine forwards. Why are jet engines so loud? Because the hot gas leaves the engine at very high speed, and the noise comes from the exhaust mixing with the air; modern turbofans are quieter because most of their thrust comes from the fan, not the core. How fast can a jet fly? A passenger jet cruises at about 900 kilometres per hour, and the fastest jet aircraft, the SR-71, flew at more than 3,500 kilometres per hour. Will jets be replaced? Not soon: electric aircraft are being developed for short flights, but for long flights the jet engine remains the only practical power, and research focuses on cleaner fuels.

The Transistor

ID: JAH-IVNT-000021 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The transistor is a small electronic device that controls the flow of electric current, and it is the building block of almost all modern electronics. A transistor can act as a switch, turning current on and off, or as an amplifier, making a weak signal stronger, and billions of them are packed into the chips that run computers, phones, and televisions. The first transistor was built at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the United States in December 1947, by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley, who shared the Nobel Prize in 1956. The transistor replaced the vacuum tube, which was large, hot, and fragile, and it made electronics small, cheap, and reliable, which made the computer, the mobile phone, and the modern world possible.

History: Before the transistor, electronics used vacuum tubes, glass bulbs that controlled current but were large, used a lot of power, and burned out often; a computer with thousands of tubes was enormous and unreliable. In the 1930s and 1940s, scientists at Bell Labs studied semiconductors, materials such as germanium and silicon that conduct electricity partly, and in December 1947 Bardeen and Brattain built the first working transistor, a point-contact device made of germanium, which amplified a signal; Shockley then developed the junction transistor, which was more practical. The first commercial transistor radio, the Regency TR-1, appeared in 1954, and it showed that electronics could be small and portable. In 1958 Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments and Robert Noyce at Fairchild built the first integrated circuits, putting several transistors on a single chip, and in 1971 Intel put a whole computer processor, the 4004, with about 2,300 transistors, on one chip. Since then, the number of transistors on a chip has doubled roughly every two years, a trend called Moore's Law, and a modern chip holds tens of billions of transistors, each only a few nanometres wide.

Key concepts: A semiconductor is a material, usually silicon, that conducts electricity partly, and whose conductivity can be controlled; it is the basis of the transistor. A transistor is a device with three terminals that controls current: a small current at one terminal controls a larger current between the other two. An amplifier is a device that makes a weak signal stronger; a transistor amplifies by using a small input to control a larger output. A switch is a device that turns current on or off; in a computer, transistors act as switches, representing the 1s and 0s of digital data. An integrated circuit, or chip, is a small piece of silicon holding many transistors and their connections; it is the heart of modern electronics. Moore's Law is the observation, made by Gordon Moore in 1965, that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years.

How it works: A transistor works by controlling the flow of current through a semiconductor. In the commonest type, the field-effect transistor, a small voltage applied to the gate, one terminal, creates an electric field that opens or closes a channel between the other two terminals, the source and the drain, letting current flow or stopping it. When the gate voltage is high, the channel is open and current flows, which represents a 1; when it is low, the channel is closed and no current flows, which represents a 0. By combining transistors into circuits, engineers build logic gates, which perform the operations of arithmetic and decision-making, and by combining gates they build processors, memory, and all the parts of a computer. The transistor's power comes from its size and its efficiency: a modern transistor is only a few nanometres wide, it switches billions of times a second, and it uses very little power, so billions of them can work together on a single chip without melting it.

Worked examples: The point-contact transistor of 1947 is the founding example: a small piece of germanium with two gold contacts, built by Bardeen and Brattain at Bell Labs, which amplified a signal for the first time. The Regency TR-1 radio of 1954 is a consumer example: the first transistor radio, it was small enough to carry in a pocket, and it made radio personal. The Intel 4004 of 1971 is a computing example: the first commercial microprocessor, it held about 2,300 transistors on one chip and could do the work of a roomful of earlier electronics. The modern smartphone chip is the latest example: it holds tens of billions of transistors, and it runs a computer, a camera, and a radio in a device that fits in a hand. The hearing aid is a medical example: the transistor made hearing aids small enough to wear behind the ear, and it changed the lives of people with hearing loss.

Real applications: The transistor's first application was in radios and hearing aids: it made electronics small, portable, and reliable, and it replaced the vacuum tube. Its second application was in computers: transistors made computers smaller, faster, and cheaper, and they led to the personal computer, the smartphone, and the internet. Its third application was in communications: transistors are in every phone, radio, and television, and they carry the world's information. Its fourth application was in industry and transport: transistors control machines, cars, and aircraft, and they are in almost every device with a circuit. Finally, the transistor is the basis of the digital age: every digital device, from a watch to a supercomputer, is built from transistors, and the modern world runs on them. The transistor is often called the most important invention of the 20th century.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the transistor? John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley at Bell Labs, who built the first working transistor in December 1947 and shared the Nobel Prize in 1956. What is the difference between a transistor and a vacuum tube? A vacuum tube is a large glass bulb that controls current, while a transistor is a small piece of semiconductor that does the same job with less power, less heat, and greater reliability. How many transistors are in a phone? Tens of billions, on the chips that run the processor, the memory, and the radio. What is Moore's Law? The observation that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years, which held for decades and drove the growth of computing. Will transistors keep getting smaller? They are now only a few nanometres wide, close to the size of atoms, so engineers are looking for new materials and new designs, such as quantum computing, to keep progress going.

The Computer

ID: JAH-IVNT-000022 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The computer is a machine that processes information: it takes in data, follows instructions, and produces results, doing arithmetic, storing information, and running programs at speeds no human can match. The first electronic computers were built in the 1940s, and the question of which was the "first" is disputed: the British Colossus of 1943 broke codes, the American ENIAC of 1945 did general calculations, and the German Z3 of 1941 was an earlier electromechanical machine. The computer changed the world more than any other invention of the 20th century: it is in every office, home, and pocket, it runs the internet, it controls machines, and it has become the main tool of science, business, and art. Today computers range from the tiny chip in a watch to supercomputers that do trillions of calculations a second.

History: Computing began with mechanical aids: the abacus, used for thousands of years, and the mechanical calculators of the 17th century, built by Pascal and Leibniz. In the 1830s, Charles Babbage in England designed the Analytical Engine, a mechanical computer with a processor and memory, but it was never built; Ada Lovelace, who worked with him, wrote the first program for it. In the 1940s, the first electronic computers appeared: the Colossus, built in Britain in 1943 to break German codes, the Z3, built by Konrad Zuse in Germany in 1941, and the ENIAC, built in the United States in 1945, which weighed 30 tonnes and filled a room. In the 1950s and 1960s, computers used transistors instead of vacuum tubes, and they became smaller and more reliable; in the 1970s, the microprocessor put a computer on a chip, and the personal computer appeared. In the 1980s and 1990s, the personal computer spread to homes and offices, and the internet connected computers around the world. In the 2000s, the smartphone put a computer in every pocket, and today computers are everywhere, from cars to watches to the cloud.

Key concepts: A program is a set of instructions that tells a computer what to do; it is written in a programming language and stored in memory. Hardware is the physical parts of a computer: the processor, the memory, the storage, and the input and output devices. Software is the programs and data that run on the hardware. A processor, or CPU, is the chip that carries out instructions, doing arithmetic and making decisions. Memory is where the computer holds data and programs while it works; storage, such as a hard drive, holds them for longer. A bit is the smallest unit of information, a 1 or a 0; a byte is eight bits, enough to represent one character.

How it works: A computer works by following instructions, one at a time, very quickly. The processor fetches an instruction from memory, decodes it, and executes it: it might add two numbers, compare them, or move data from one place to another. It does this billions of times a second, and by combining simple instructions it can do complex tasks, such as displaying a picture, playing a video, or searching the internet. Data is stored as bits, 1s and 0s, and the computer represents everything — numbers, letters, pictures, and sounds — as patterns of bits. Input devices, such as a keyboard, a mouse, or a camera, let the user give the computer data, and output devices, such as a screen or a speaker, show the results. The operating system is a program that manages the computer's resources, and applications are programs that do specific jobs, such as writing, browsing, or playing games. The computer's power comes from its speed and its precision: it can do in a second what would take a human a lifetime.

Worked examples: The ENIAC of 1945 is a founding example: a room-sized machine with 18,000 vacuum tubes, it could do 5,000 additions a second, and it was used to calculate artillery tables. The Colossus of 1943 is a secret example: built to break the German Lorenz code, it was one of the first programmable electronic computers, and its existence was kept secret for decades. The Apple II of 1977 is a personal example: one of the first successful personal computers, it brought computing into homes and schools. The World Wide Web, invented by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989, is a network example: it turned the internet into a system of linked pages that anyone could use. The smartphone is the modern example: a computer, a phone, a camera, and an internet terminal in one device, used by billions of people.

Real applications: The computer's first application was calculation: it did arithmetic for science, engineering, and the military, faster than any human. Its second application was in business: computers keep accounts, manage stock, and run payrolls, and they are essential to every company. Its third application was in communication: computers run the internet, email, and social media, and they connect the world. Its fourth application was in science: computers model the weather, design drugs, and simulate the universe, and they have become the main tool of research. Finally, the computer is a tool of art and culture: it makes music, films, and games, and it has changed how people create and share. The computer is now so common that it is easy to forget how recent it is: the first electronic computers are less than a century old.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the computer? No one person did: Babbage designed the first programmable machine, Zuse built the Z3, the British built Colossus, and the Americans built ENIAC; the "first computer" depends on how the question is defined, and the claim is disputed. What is the difference between hardware and software? Hardware is the physical machine, and software is the programs that run on it; a computer needs both. How fast is a computer? A modern processor does billions of instructions a second, and a supercomputer does trillions of calculations a second. Will computers replace humans? The Laser

ID: JAH-IVNT-000023 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The laser is a device that produces a narrow, intense beam of light, in which all the light waves are aligned and of the same colour. The word "laser" stands for "light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation", and the first laser was built by Theodore Maiman in the United States in 1960, using a ruby crystal. The idea behind it came from Albert Einstein, who in 1917 described stimulated emission, the process by which an atom can be made to emit light in step with other atoms. The laser changed the world: it is used in medicine, in communications, in manufacturing, in entertainment, and in science, and it is one of the most versatile inventions of the 20th century.

History: The laser began with Einstein's theory of 1917, in which he described stimulated emission: an atom in an excited state, when struck by a photon of the right energy, emits a second photon identical to the first. In the 1950s, Charles Townes and Arthur Schawlow in the United States, and Nikolay Basov and Alexander Prokhorov in the Soviet Union, developed the maser, a device that did the same thing with microwaves, and they shared the Nobel Prize in 1964. In 1960, Theodore Maiman at Hughes Research Laboratories built the first laser, using a ruby crystal flashed by a lamp, which produced a beam of red light; within months, other lasers were built, using gases and other materials. In the 1960s and 1970s, lasers were used in science and in the military, and in the 1980s they became common in consumer products: the compact disc player, which reads music with a laser, and the barcode scanner, which reads prices in shops. Today lasers are everywhere: in fibre-optic communications, in surgery, in printers, in pointers, and in the cutting and welding of materials.

Key concepts: Stimulated emission is the process by which an excited atom, struck by a photon, emits a second photon identical to the first, in the same direction and with the same energy. Coherence is the property of laser light in which all the waves are aligned and of the same wavelength, which makes the beam narrow and intense. A photon is a particle of light. A ruby laser uses a crystal of ruby, aluminium oxide with chromium, as its active material, and it produces red light. A fibre-optic cable is a thin strand of glass that carries light, including laser light, over long distances; it is the basis of modern communications. A maser is a device like a laser that works with microwaves instead of visible light.

How it works: A laser works by amplifying light. Inside the laser is an active material, such as a ruby crystal or a gas, whose atoms can be excited, or raised to a higher energy state, by a flash of light or an electric current. When one excited atom emits a photon, that photon strikes another excited atom, which emits a second photon identical to the first, and the two photons strike more atoms, producing more photons, all identical and all moving in the same direction. The photons bounce back and forth between two mirrors at the ends of the laser, one of which is partly transparent, and with each pass more photons are produced, until a narrow, intense beam escapes through the partly transparent mirror. Because all the photons are identical, the beam is coherent: it stays narrow over long distances, and it can be focused to a tiny, intense spot. Different lasers use different materials and produce different colours, from infrared to ultraviolet, and they are chosen for different jobs.

Worked examples: Maiman's ruby laser of 1960 is the founding example: a ruby crystal the size of a fingertip, flashed by a lamp, which produced a beam of red light and proved the laser worked. The helium-neon laser is a common example: a gas laser that produces a red beam, used in supermarket barcode scanners and in school laboratories. The compact disc player is a consumer example: a small semiconductor laser reads the pits on a CD and turns them into music, and it made digital music possible. The fibre-optic laser is a communications example: lasers send pulses of light through glass fibres under the oceans, carrying the internet's data around the world. The surgical laser is a medical example: a laser can cut tissue precisely, seal blood vessels, and correct vision, and it is used in eye surgery, dentistry, and cancer treatment.

Real applications: The laser's first application was in science: it gave researchers a new tool for studying atoms, molecules, and materials, and it led to new discoveries in physics and chemistry. Its second application was in communications: lasers carry data through fibre-optic cables, and they are the basis of the internet's backbone. Its third application was in medicine: lasers are used in eye surgery, in dentistry, and in the treatment of cancer and skin conditions. Its fourth application was in manufacturing: lasers cut, weld, and engrave metal, plastic, and wood with great precision, and they are used in factories around the world. Finally, the laser is in everyday life: in barcode scanners, in printers, in pointers, in DVD players, and in the measurement of distances, from a room to the Moon, where reflectors left by the Apollo astronauts return laser beams to Earth.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the laser? Theodore Maiman built the first working laser in 1960, building on the work of Einstein, Townes, Schawlow, Basov, and Prokhorov; the Nobel Prize for the maser and the laser went to Townes, Basov, and Prokhorov in 1964. What does "laser" stand for? "Light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation", which describes how the device works. Is a laser dangerous? A powerful laser can burn skin and damage eyes, and lasers are classified by their power; the small lasers in pointers and scanners are safe, but industrial and medical lasers must be handled carefully. Why is laser light different from ordinary light? Because it is coherent: all the waves are aligned and of the same colour, so the beam stays narrow and can be focused to a tiny spot. What is the laser used for in space? Lasers measure the distance to the Moon, using reflectors left by the Apollo missions, and they are used in satellites and in the search for planets.

The Internet

ID: JAH-IVNT-000024 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The Internet is a global network of computers that communicate with each other, sharing information, messages, and services. It is the network of networks: millions of smaller networks, in homes, offices, universities, and companies, all connected by common rules, so that a computer in one country can send data to a computer in another in a fraction of a second. The Internet began as ARPANET, a research network built in the United States in 1969, and it grew through the 1970s and 1980s, when the rules for sending data, called TCP/IP, were agreed, and the World Wide Web, invented by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989, made it easy for anyone to use. The Internet has changed the world more than any invention since the printing press: it carries the world's knowledge, commerce, and conversation, and it is used by billions of people every day.

History: The Internet began in the 1960s, when the United States Department of Defense wanted a communications network that could survive an attack. In 1969, ARPANET, the Advanced Research Projects Agency Network, connected four computers, at the University of California, Los Angeles, the Stanford Research Institute, the University of California, Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah; the first message sent was "LO", because the system crashed as the sender typed "LOGIN". In the 1970s, Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn developed TCP/IP, the rules that let different networks talk to each other, and in 1983 ARPANET adopted them, becoming the Internet. In 1989, Tim Berners-Lee, a scientist at CERN in Switzerland, invented the World Wide Web, a system of linked pages that could be viewed with a browser, and in 1991 the Web was made available to the public. In the 1990s, the Web grew rapidly: the first browsers, the first search engines, and the first online shops appeared, and by 2000 hundreds of millions of people were online. In the 2000s, broadband made the Internet fast, Wi-Fi made it wireless, and the smartphone made it portable, and today more than five billion people use it.

Key concepts: A network is a group of computers connected so they can share information; the Internet is a network of networks. TCP/IP is the set of rules, or protocols, that govern how data is sent over the Internet; TCP breaks data into packets, and IP addresses them. A packet is a small block of data, sent across the network and reassembled at the other end. The World Wide Web is a system of linked documents, or web pages, that can be viewed with a browser; it runs on the Internet but is not the same as the Internet. A browser is a program that displays web pages. A server is a computer that stores web pages and sends them to browsers on request. An IP address is a number that identifies a computer on the Internet.

How it works: The Internet works by breaking data into packets and sending them across a network of routers. When a user asks for a web page, the browser sends a request to the server that holds the page, and the server breaks the page into packets, each labelled with the address of the destination and a number showing its place in the sequence. The packets travel across the network, hopping from router to router, and each router forwards them toward their destination, choosing the best path at the time; if one path is busy or broken, the packets take another. At the destination, the packets are reassembled in order, and the browser displays the page. The system is called packet switching, and it is robust: because there is no single path, the network can survive damage, and because the data is broken into packets, many users can share the same wires. The physical network is made of cables under the oceans, fibre-optic lines on land, and radio links, including Wi-Fi and mobile networks, and it is run by thousands of companies and organisations, with no single owner.

Worked examples: ARPANET's first message of 1969 is the founding example: the letters "LO", sent from UCLA to Stanford, because the system crashed as the sender typed "LOGIN". The first email, sent by Ray Tomlinson in 1971, is a communication example: he chose the "@" sign to separate the user's name from the computer's name, a choice still used today. The World Wide Web of 1989 is a usability example: Berners-Lee's system of linked pages made the Internet easy to use, and it turned a research network into a public medium. The first web browser, Mosaic, of 1993, is a popular example: it showed pictures and text together, and it made the Web attractive to ordinary people. The smartphone is the modern example: it put the Internet in billions of pockets, and it made the Internet the main way people communicate, shop, and learn.

Real applications: The Internet's first application was research and the military: it let scientists and soldiers share information across distances. Its second application was communication: email, messaging, and video calls let people talk to each other instantly, anywhere in the world. Its third application was commerce: online shops, banking, and payments moved trade onto the Internet, and new companies, such as Amazon and Alibaba, grew up on it. Its fourth application was knowledge: the Web holds the world's information, from encyclopedias to libraries, and search engines make it findable. Finally, the Internet is a platform for art, entertainment, and politics: it carries music, films, games, and news, and it has changed how people organise, debate, and vote. The Internet's challenges — privacy, security, and misinformation — are also part of its story, and they are the subject of ongoing debate.

Common questions and misconceptions: Who invented the Internet? No one person did: ARPANET was built by many people, Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn developed TCP/IP, and Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web; the Internet is the work of thousands. What is the difference between the Internet and the World Wide Web? The Internet is the network of computers, and the Web is a system of linked pages that runs on it; email, messaging, and other services also run on the Internet but are not the Web. How many people use the Internet? More than five billion, about two-thirds of the world's population, and the number is still growing. Is the Internet owned by anyone? No, it is a network of networks, run by thousands of companies, universities, and governments, with no single owner, though its rules are agreed by international bodies. Will the Internet keep growing? Probably yes: new users come online every year, and new uses, from the Internet of Things to virtual reality, continue to appear.
