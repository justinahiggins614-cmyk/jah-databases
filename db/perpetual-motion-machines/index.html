Overbalanced Wheel JAH-PMMO-000001

The overbalanced wheel is the oldest perpetual-motion design: weights mounted on a wheel are arranged to fall farther on one side than they rise on the other, so the wheel should keep turning from the imbalance. Drawings of it appear from the Middle Ages onward, in many variations, with hinged arms, rolling balls, or sliding weights all meant to keep the descending side heavier.

The design fails for a reason that can be shown on paper: although individual weights may sit farther out on one side, the wheel's center of mass never rises above its axle in a way that produces net work over a full turn. Every gain on the descending side is paid back on the ascending side, and friction takes its cut besides. The wheel may rock or turn briefly from a push, but it always settles, because no arrangement of weights changes the energy balance of a closed cycle.

The demo computes the torque contribution of weights at stated angles and radii, showing that the sum over a full turn is zero before friction. It is a torque-balance illustration with assumed values, not a test of any built wheel.

Bessler Wheel Story JAH-PMMO-000002

Johann Bessler, also known as Orffyreus, exhibited wheels in the early eighteenth century that he claimed turned without external power, and he showed them to witnesses under conditions he controlled. No independent investigator was ever allowed the examination that would have settled the question, Bessler destroyed his devices rather than reveal them, and no wheel of his design has ever been reproduced from his writings. The historical record therefore documents a showman's claim, not a machine.

The Bessler story matters because it set the pattern for later claims: impressive demonstrations, controlled conditions, secrecy about the mechanism, and destruction or disappearance before independent testing. Learning that pattern helps readers evaluate new claims, because genuine machines improve under examination while claimed perpetual machines consistently avoid it.

The demo prints the historical checkpoints of the Bessler episode as a timeline. It is a history summary, not evidence about any mechanism.

Magnetic Motor Claims JAH-PMMO-000003

Magnetic motor claims propose that permanent magnets, arranged in a rotor and stator, can push a wheel around forever, often with shielding that is supposed to block the magnets on the return side. The idea recurs in every generation, with new magnet materials and new geometries, and it fails for the same reason each time: a static magnetic field is conservative, so the work done on a magnet moving around a closed loop sums to zero, just as with gravity.

Shielding does not rescue the design, because any shield that blocks the field on one side also costs energy to move through, and the shield itself is part of the same closed system. Careful measurements of magnetic motors that were actually tested show them running down like everything else, with the run time explained by the initial push, bearing quality, and flywheel inertia rather than by any energy gain.

The demo integrates a simple conservative field around a loop and prints the net work, which is zero. It is a field-arithmetic illustration, not a motor test.

Buoyancy Wheel Claims JAH-PMMO-000004

Buoyancy wheel claims float hollow containers up through water on one side of a wheel, expecting the lift to outweigh the cost of pushing containers down on the other side. The design looks plausible in a diagram, because buoyancy is a real upward force, but the accounting fails at the seals: pushing a container into the water at the bottom, against water pressure, costs exactly the energy the rising side appears to gain, before friction is even counted.

The general lesson is that any cycle operating in a fluid must pay for every part of the cycle, including the parts the diagram draws small. Seals, valves, and entry points are where the missing energy hides in most fluid perpetual-motion designs, and a careful energy account that includes them closes the loop at zero or worse.

The demo computes buoyant force for a stated submerged volume and the work of pushing the same volume down against pressure, printing both. The numbers are a teaching illustration with assumed values.

Capillary Pump Claims JAH-PMMO-000005

Capillary pump claims propose that narrow tubes can lift water continuously and then let it fall through a turbine, generating power forever. Capillary rise is real: water climbs a narrow tube because surface tension pulls it up the walls. But the water does not then fall out and start again for free, because the same surface tension that lifted it holds it at the top, and breaking free costs the energy the rise appeared to give.

The design fails at the exit, which is the pattern for many perpetual-motion proposals: the attractive half of the cycle is drawn in detail while the return half, where the energy is paid back, is drawn vaguely or not at all. A complete cycle account, including detachment at the top, sums to zero before losses.

The demo computes capillary rise height from the standard formula with stated surface tension, tube radius, and contact angle, printing the rise. It is a capillarity calculation, not a power measurement.

Self-Flowing Flask Claims JAH-PMMO-000006

The self-flowing flask, sometimes called a Boyle flask, proposes that water siphoning out of a vessel can refill the vessel from below and run forever. The siphon half works: water flows from a higher level to a lower one through a tube. The refill half does not, because refilling means lifting water back to the higher level, which costs exactly the energy the siphon delivered, and the siphon itself stops when the levels equalize.

The flask is a good teaching device because the error is easy to spot once the cycle is drawn completely: the diagram usually emphasizes the falling stream and minimizes the lift. Drawing the full cycle, with levels marked, shows the loop closing at zero, which is the skill the whole database exists to teach.

The demo tracks two vessel levels through siphon steps, printing the levels as they equalize and the flow stops. It is a level simulation, not a flask experiment.

First Law: Energy Conservation JAH-PMMO-000007

The first law of thermodynamics states that energy is conserved: in a closed system, energy changes form but the total does not change, so a machine cannot output more energy than it takes in. A perpetual-motion machine of the first kind would violate this law by creating energy, and no such machine has ever survived measurement. The law is not a prejudice against invention; it is a summary of every careful energy measurement ever made, and any new machine must be consistent with it or overturn it with evidence of the same quality.

Energy accounting is the practical form of the first law: list every input, every output, and every loss, in the same units, over the same cycle, and check that the books balance. Most perpetual-motion claims fail this check in public, because the account omits an input, double-counts an output, or measures over an incomplete cycle that starts charged and ends discharged.

The demo balances a simple energy account with stated inputs, useful output, and losses, printing the balance. It is an accounting illustration, not a measurement of any machine.

Second Law: Entropy and Waste Heat JAH-PMMO-000008

The second law of thermodynamics states that in a closed system, entropy does not decrease, which in practical terms means every real energy conversion wastes some energy as heat that cannot be fully recovered. A perpetual-motion machine of the second kind would convert waste heat completely back into work, and no such machine exists. The law explains why even excellent machines need a continuous energy supply: losses are not an engineering embarrassment to be eliminated, but a feature of how energy flows.

The second law also explains why friction, air resistance, electrical resistance, and hysteresis all point the same way: each turns organized motion or current into disorganized heat, and heat does not spontaneously reorganize itself into motion. Reversing any one loss in isolation is possible with an energy input, but reversing all of them together, for free, is what the law forbids.

The demo applies a stated efficiency below unity over repeated cycles, showing stored energy decaying toward zero. It is a decay illustration, not a measurement.

Friction and Loss Accounting JAH-PMMO-000009

Friction converts motion into heat at every sliding contact, bearing, and gear mesh, and it is the loss that most often ends a claimed perpetual machine's run. A wheel that seems to run for a long time is usually a good flywheel with good bearings, storing the initial push efficiently, not a machine gaining energy. Measuring run-down time against bearing quality and flywheel inertia explains the long runs without any new physics.

A complete loss account lists friction, air drag, electrical resistance, magnetic hysteresis, and vibration, each estimated or measured separately, because losses hide in the category nobody bothered to list. The discipline of listing losses before claiming gains is the single most useful habit this database teaches.

The demo models a flywheel losing a fixed fraction of its speed per minute to friction and drag, printing speed over ten minutes. It is a decay model with assumed values.

Measurement Traps in Claims JAH-PMMO-000010

Measurement traps are the ways a claimed machine can appear to gain energy when it does not: starting the measurement with a charged battery, flywheel, or spring and ending discharged; measuring power in but not power out, or vice versa; sampling during a transient; and ignoring an input such as a hand, a magnet, or mains power. Each trap is innocent-looking in isolation, which is why independent measurement with a complete account matters more than any demonstration.

The standard safeguards are simple: measure over complete cycles that start and end in the same state, measure all inputs and outputs simultaneously, run long enough for transients to die, and let an independent party choose the instruments. A claim that resists those safeguards has a problem, and a claim that welcomes them is behaving like real engineering.

The demo shows how a partial measurement flatters a device: counting only output during a discharge while ignoring the charging input. It is an arithmetic illustration of the trap.

Conservation in Practice: Pendulum Clock JAH-PMMO-000011

A pendulum clock is sometimes mistaken for a perpetual machine because it runs for a long time, but it runs on a weight or spring that must be rewound, and its long run comes from efficient storage and low losses, not from energy creation. The pendulum itself only times the release of stored energy; it does not supply it. That distinction, between timing and supplying, clarifies many claims at once.

The clock's energy account is small and complete: gravitational potential in the raised weight, or elastic potential in the wound spring, flows through the escapement to the pendulum and the hands, with friction taking its share at every step. When the weight reaches the bottom or the spring unwinds, the clock stops, exactly as conservation requires.

Why Claims Persist: Sociology JAH-PMMO-000012

Perpetual-motion claims persist for understandable reasons: the designs are mechanically clever, the inventors are often sincere, and the dream of free energy is attractive. Patent offices and physics institutions do not reject the machines from prejudice; they reject them because every tested machine has run down, and because the underlying laws have survived every careful test. The persistence of claims despite that record is a sociological fact about hope, craftsmanship, and distrust of authority, not a scientific controversy.

The constructive response to a new claim is not ridicule but measurement: invite a complete energy account, offer independent testing, and publish the result either way. That response respects the inventor's work while protecting the public from investing in a machine that cannot deliver, and it is the response this database recommends for every new claim it catalogs.

Energy Accounting Template JAH-PMMO-000013

An energy accounting template is a table with rows for every input, output, and loss, columns for energy, power, and time, and a balance row that must sum to zero over a complete cycle. The template forces the accountant to name each term, state its measurement method, and give its uncertainty, so that gaps appear as blank rows rather than as hidden assumptions. Any machine, claimed or conventional, can be described in the template, which is what makes the template fair.

The template's rules are few and strict: same units throughout, same cycle boundaries for every term, uncertainties stated, and no term labeled negligible without a measurement or a bound. A template filled under those rules either balances, which means the machine is conventional, or it does not, which means a term is missing or mismeasured. Either outcome is progress.

Flywheel Storage vs Generation JAH-PMMO-000014

A flywheel stores energy as rotation and returns most of it on demand, which makes it an excellent buffer and a frequent source of confusion: a heavy, well-balanced flywheel spins for a long time after a push, and that long spin looks like generation to an observer who missed the push. The distinction is storage versus generation, and it is settled by the energy account: a flywheel's output never exceeds its input plus what was stored, minus losses.

Flywheels are genuinely useful in vehicles, grid stabilization, and machinery, precisely because they store and return energy efficiently over short times. Their usefulness does not need any perpetual-motion claim, and attaching such a claim to a flywheel only obscures a real technology with an impossible one.

Regenerative Braking: Real Recovery JAH-PMMO-000015

Regenerative braking is real energy recovery, and it is instructive precisely because it is not perpetual motion: a moving vehicle already carries kinetic energy, and regenerative braking converts part of that energy back into stored electrical energy instead of wasting it all as heat in friction brakes. The recovered energy came from fuel or from a charged battery earlier, and the round trip loses energy at every conversion, so the vehicle still needs an external supply.

The recovery fraction is limited by motor efficiency, battery charge acceptance, and the fact that hard braking still needs friction brakes for safety. Typical recovery recovers a fraction of braking energy, not all of it, and no fraction of a fraction, compounded over cycles, can exceed the original input. That arithmetic is the whole difference between recovery and creation.

Evaluating a New Claim: Protocol JAH-PMMO-000016

Evaluating a new perpetual-motion claim well means following a protocol: request the complete energy account, require complete cycles starting and ending in the same state, measure all inputs and outputs simultaneously with independent instruments, run long enough for transients to die, and publish the method and the result. A claim that passes the protocol is a discovery; a claim that fails it is, at minimum, unproven, and usually explained by a missing term the protocol finds.

The protocol protects both sides: the inventor gets a fair, public test instead of dismissal, and the public gets a result it can trust instead of a demonstration it cannot check. The protocol's final step is archiving the data, so that a later investigator need not repeat the whole exercise to know what was tried.
