What Topology Is

ID: JAH-TOPO-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-topology#JAH-TOPO-000001 k=c325e577

Definition: Topology is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties of shapes that survive stretching, bending, and twisting, but not tearing or gluing. A coffee cup and a doughnut are the same in topology, because each has one hole; a sphere and a cube are the same, because neither has a hole. Topology asks what is essential about a shape when size, distance, and angles are ignored.

History: Topology began with Euler's solution to the Königsberg bridge problem in 1736, which asked whether a walk could cross each of seven bridges exactly once. Euler showed the answer depends only on the connections, not the distances — the first topological argument. In the 19th century, Listing coined the word topology in 1847, and Riemann's work on surfaces laid the foundations. Poincaré's Analysis Situs of 1895 founded algebraic topology, introducing the fundamental group and homology. In the 20th century, topology became central to mathematics: the Poincaré conjecture, stated in 1904 and proved by Perelman in 2003, classified three-dimensional spaces, and topology now appears in physics, data analysis, and robotics.

Key concepts: The key ideas are topology, homeomorphism, hole, continuous, topological space. Topology is the study of properties preserved under continuous deformations: stretching and bending are allowed, tearing and gluing are not. A homeomorphism is a continuous deformation with a continuous inverse. Two shapes related by one are topologically the same. A hole is a topological feature: a doughnut has one hole, a sphere has none, and a figure-eight has two. Holes are counted by homology. A continuous map sends nearby points to nearby points, with no jumps or tears. Continuity is the only structure topology uses. A topological space is a set with a collection of open sets satisfying the axioms: the whole set and the empty set are open, and unions and finite intersections of opens are open. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, describe the shape and what is allowed: stretching and bending yes, tearing and gluing no. Second, look for the features that survive deformation: holes, connectedness, compactness. Third, count the holes, or compute an invariant like the Euler characteristic. Fourth, compare two shapes by their invariants: different invariants mean different shapes. Fifth, check the answer with a concrete deformation or a cut-and-paste argument. Worked examples: A coffee cup and a doughnut are the same topologically: push the cup's handle out and flatten the cup, and the handle becomes the doughnut's hole, with one hole throughout. A sphere and a cube are the same: inflate the cube's faces and the corners smooth out, with no hole at any stage. But a sphere and a doughnut are different: no amount of stretching turns a sphere into a doughnut, because the hole cannot be created without tearing. The Euler characteristic tells them apart: a sphere has 2, a torus has 0.

Real applications: Data analysis uses topology: persistent homology finds the shape of data, like loops and voids in a point cloud, which ordinary statistics misses. Physics uses topology: topological insulators conduct on their surfaces but not inside, and the property is protected by topology. Robotics uses topology: the space of a robot arm's configurations is a topological space, and planning a motion is finding a path in it. Biology uses topology: DNA knots and links are classified topologically, and enzymes cut and rejoin DNA by changing its topology.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is a coffee cup really the same as a doughnut? Topologically, yes: each has exactly one hole, and one can be deformed into the other without tearing. They differ in geometry, not in topology. What does topology ignore? Distance, angle, size, and straightness. It keeps only what survives continuous deformation: connectedness, holes, and compactness. Is topology useful or just abstract? Both: it is abstract, and it is used in data analysis, physics, robotics, and biology, wherever shape matters more than size. What is the hardest problem in topology? The Poincaré conjecture was, for a century. It was proved by Perelman in 2003, and the four-dimensional smooth Poincaré conjecture remains open.

Open Sets and Continuity

ID: JAH-TOPO-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-topology#JAH-TOPO-000002 k=777cd5f3

Definition: An open set is the basic building block of topology: a set in which every point has a little neighbourhood entirely inside the set. In the real line, an open interval (0, 1) is open, because around any point inside it there is a smaller interval still inside; the closed interval [0, 1] is not, because 0 and 1 have no neighbourhood inside. A function is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open.

History: Open sets were implicit in the 19th-century work on continuity: Cauchy and Weierstrass defined continuity with epsilons and deltas, which is continuity in terms of open intervals. Hausdorff's Grundzüge der Mengenlehre of 1914 gave the first axiomatic definition of a topological space in terms of neighbourhoods, and the open-set axioms became standard in the 1920s and 1930s. The definition of continuity through open sets, due to the same period, replaced epsilons and deltas with a single clean condition. Today open sets are the foundation of topology, analysis, and much of modern mathematics: every topological argument starts with them.

Key concepts: The key ideas are open set, closed set, neighbourhood, continuous function, topology. An open set is a set where every point has a neighbourhood inside the set. (0, 1) is open; [0, 1] is not, because of its endpoints. A closed set is one whose complement is open. [0, 1] is closed; (0, 1) is not. A neighbourhood of a point is a set containing an open set around the point. It is the precise form of nearby. A function is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open. Intuitively, nearby points go to nearby points. A topology is a collection of open sets satisfying three axioms: the empty set and the whole space are open, arbitrary unions are open, and finite intersections are open. Worked examples: The interval (0, 1) is open: take any point x inside, and the interval (x - d, x + d) with d the smaller of x and 1 - x lies inside (0, 1). The interval [0, 1] is not open: around 0, every interval (-d, d) contains negative numbers outside [0, 1]. The set (0, 1) union (2, 3) is open, because it is a union of opens. Real applications: Analysis uses open sets to define continuity, convergence, and compactness without mentioning distance: the same definitions work in any topological space. Differential geometry builds manifolds from open sets: a manifold is covered by open patches, each looking like ordinary space. Computer science uses topology in domain theory: open sets model observable properties of programs.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is an open set the same as an open interval? No: an open interval is one example. An open set can be a union of intervals, a disc, or something stranger, as long as every point has a neighbourhood inside. Can a set be both open and closed? Yes: the empty set and the whole space are both, in any topology. In the real line they are the only ones. Why define continuity with open sets? Because it works everywhere, not just on the real line: the same definition applies to surfaces, function spaces, and abstract spaces. What is a closed set, intuitively? A set that contains all its limit points: if a sequence in the set converges, its limit is in the set too.

Homeomorphisms

ID: JAH-TOPO-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-topology#JAH-TOPO-000003 k=63288d09

Definition: A homeomorphism is a continuous deformation with a continuous inverse: a map between two spaces that stretches and bends one into the other without tearing or gluing, and can be undone the same way. Two spaces related by a homeomorphism are topologically the same, and every topological property is shared. A sphere and a cube are homeomorphic; a sphere and a torus are not.

History: Homeomorphisms were implicit in Riemann's work on surfaces in the 1850s and in Poincaré's Analysis Situs of 1895, which founded algebraic topology by studying properties invariant under homeomorphism. The word comes from the Greek for same shape. In the 20th century, the classification of surfaces showed that homeomorphism classes of surfaces are completely understood: they are determined by the number of holes and the orientability. The homeomorphism problem — deciding whether two given spaces are homeomorphic — is hard in general: for three-dimensional manifolds it was solved only after Perelman's proof of the Poincaré conjecture in 2003.

Key concepts: The key ideas are homeomorphism, topological invariant, embedding, deformation, isomorphism. A homeomorphism is a bijection that is continuous both ways. It is the isomorphism of topology: the spaces it relates are the same topologically. A topological invariant is a property unchanged by homeomorphism: the number of holes, connectedness, and compactness are invariants. An embedding is a homeomorphism onto its image: a space placed inside another without self-intersection, like a knotted circle in space. A deformation stretches and bends continuously. A homeomorphism is the end result of a deformation that can be undone. An isomorphism is a structure-preserving bijection. In topology the structure is the open sets, and the isomorphism is a homeomorphism. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

Third, check the map is a bijection: one-to-one and onto. Fourth, if any step fails, look for an invariant that differs, to prove no homeomorphism exists. Fifth, confirm the spaces share all topological properties. Worked examples: A sphere and a cube are homeomorphic: inflate the cube by pushing each point out along a ray from the centre until it meets the sphere, and the map is continuous with a continuous inverse. A circle and a square are homeomorphic the same way. But a circle and a figure-eight are not: removing one point from a circle leaves it connected, while removing the crossing point from a figure-eight disconnects it into four pieces, so no homeomorphism can exist — connectedness after removing a point is an invariant. A line and a circle are not homeomorphic: removing a point disconnects the line into two pieces but leaves the circle connected.

Real applications: Computer graphics deforms meshes with homeomorphisms: a character's skin is deformed without tearing, which is a homeomorphism of the surface. Robotics plans motions as homeomorphisms of configuration space: a path that avoids obstacles is a continuous deformation. Data analysis compares shapes by homeomorphism: two point clouds with the same persistent homology are candidates for being the same shape. Physics classifies phases of matter by topology: two materials are in the same phase when their ground states are homeomorphic in the relevant sense.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is a homeomorphism the same as a deformation? Almost: a homeomorphism is a map, and a deformation is a process. Every deformation produces a homeomorphism, but some homeomorphisms cannot be reached by a deformation without passing through the space. Can a homeomorphism change the size? Yes: size is not topological. A tiny sphere and a huge sphere are homeomorphic, and the map is just scaling. Why is the inverse important? Because a continuous bijection need not have a continuous inverse: the map from [0, 1) to the circle that wraps the interval around is continuous and bijective, but its inverse tears at the join. Are homeomorphic spaces identical? Topologically, yes: they share every topological property. Geometrically they can be very different, like a cube and a sphere.

Connectedness

ID: JAH-TOPO-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-topology#JAH-TOPO-000004 k=a1f62f19

Definition: A space is connected when it is all in one piece: it cannot be split into two non-empty open sets that do not touch. An interval is connected; two separate intervals are not. Connectedness is the simplest topological invariant, and it is preserved by continuous maps: the continuous image of a connected space is connected. It is the topological version of being in one piece, and it is the first thing checked about any space.

History: Connectedness was made precise in the 19th century, as topology separated from geometry: Jordan and Cantor studied connected sets, and Hausdorff's axioms of 1914 gave the modern definition. The intermediate value theorem — that a continuous function on an interval takes every value between its endpoints — is really a statement about connectedness: the image of a connected set is connected, and the connected subsets of the real line are exactly the intervals. In the 20th century, connectedness became the first invariant computed for any space, and its refinements, like path-connectedness and simple connectedness, became central to algebraic topology.

Key concepts: The key ideas are connected, path-connected, component, disconnected, intermediate value theorem. A space is connected when it cannot be split into two disjoint non-empty open sets. An interval is connected; two disjoint intervals are not. A space is path-connected when any two points can be joined by a continuous path. Path-connected implies connected, but not conversely. A connected component is a maximal connected piece. A space with two components is two separate pieces. A space is disconnected when it splits into two disjoint non-empty open sets. The rational numbers are totally disconnected in a strong sense. The intermediate value theorem says a continuous function on an interval takes every intermediate value. It follows from connectedness. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

Fifth, check path-connectedness separately: a path is stronger than a connection. Worked examples: The interval [0, 1] is connected: it cannot be split into two open pieces, because any split would need a gap, and the real line has no gaps. The set (0, 1) union (2, 3) is disconnected: it splits into (0, 1) and (2, 3), two non-empty open sets. A circle is connected and path-connected: any two points can be joined by an arc.

Real applications: Analysis uses connectedness for the intermediate value theorem: it guarantees that equations have solutions when a continuous function changes sign. Complex analysis uses connectedness to define domains: a domain is an open connected set, and holomorphic functions behave well on domains. Robotics uses connectedness of configuration space: a robot can reach a position only if it lies in the same component as the start. Data analysis uses connected components to cluster data: points in the same component belong together.

The topologist's sine curve is connected but not path-connected, because no path can reach the limit segment. Are the rational numbers connected? No: between any two rationals there is an irrational, so the rationals split into pieces, and they are totally disconnected. Does a continuous image preserve connectedness? Yes: the continuous image of a connected space is connected. That is why the intermediate value theorem works. Can a connected space be split by removing a point? Yes: removing a point from a line disconnects it, and removing the crossing from a figure-eight splits it into four pieces.

Compactness

ID: JAH-TOPO-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-topology#JAH-TOPO-000005 k=2542d876

Definition: A space is compact when every open cover has a finite subcover: however you cover it with open sets, a few of them already cover it. In the real line, a set is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded: [0, 1] is compact, (0, 1) is not, and the whole line is not. Compactness is the topological form of finiteness, and it is what makes continuous functions on a compact space attain their maxima and minima.

History: Compactness grew out of the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem of the 19th century: every bounded sequence in the real line has a convergent subsequence. Borel and Lebesgue, around 1900, proved the covering property for intervals, and Fréchet coined the word compact in 1906. The Heine-Borel theorem says that in Euclidean space, compact means closed and bounded. In the 20th century, compactness became the most used property in topology and analysis: it guarantees that maxima exist, that sequences converge, and that infinite processes can be reduced to finite ones.

Key concepts: The key ideas are compact, open cover, Heine-Borel theorem, sequentially compact, extreme value theorem. A space is compact when every open cover has a finite subcover. [0, 1] is compact; (0, 1) and the whole line are not. An open cover is a collection of open sets whose union is the whole space. Compactness says a finite few always suffice. The Heine-Borel theorem says a subset of Euclidean space is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded. A space is sequentially compact when every sequence has a convergent subsequence. In metric spaces this is the same as compact. The extreme value theorem says a continuous function on a compact space attains its maximum and minimum. Worked examples: The interval [0, 1] is compact: it is closed and bounded, so Heine-Borel applies. The function f(x) = x^2 on [0, 1] attains its maximum 1 at x = 1 and its minimum 0 at x = 0, as the extreme value theorem promises. Real applications: Optimisation uses compactness: a continuous function on a compact set attains its maximum, so an optimum exists. Yes: any open cover of a finite set has a finite subcover — just pick one set for each point. Does compactness survive continuous maps? Yes: the continuous image of a compact space is compact, which is why maxima are preserved.

The Hausdorff Condition

ID: JAH-TOPO-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-topology#JAH-TOPO-000006 k=cd422157

Definition: A space is Hausdorff when any two distinct points can be separated by disjoint open neighbourhoods: around each point there is an open set, and the two open sets do not meet. The condition is named after Felix Hausdorff, who introduced it in 1914. The real line, the plane, and every metric space are Hausdorff. The condition rules out pathological spaces where points cannot be told apart, and it is what makes limits unique.

History: Hausdorff introduced his separation axiom in his Grundzüge der Mengenlehre of 1914, as part of his axiomatic definition of topological spaces. He distinguished several separation axioms, and the Hausdorff condition — now called T2 — became the standard assumption: most spaces in mathematics are Hausdorff, and most theorems assume it. The condition matters because without it, a sequence can converge to two different limits at once, which breaks analysis. In the 20th century, non-Hausdorff spaces still appeared, notably in algebraic geometry, where the Zariski topology is not Hausdorff, and in the study of foliations and orbifolds.

Key concepts: The key ideas are Hausdorff, separation axiom, unique limits, metric space, Zariski topology. A space is Hausdorff when distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods. Metric spaces are Hausdorff. A separation axiom states how well points can be separated by open sets. Hausdorff, or T2, is the most used. In a Hausdorff space, a sequence has at most one limit. This is what makes analysis work. A metric space has a distance function. Every metric space is Hausdorff, because balls of half the distance apart do not meet. The Zariski topology on algebraic varieties is not Hausdorff: its open sets are so large that distinct points cannot be separated. It is named after Oscar Zariski, and its closed sets are the zero sets of polynomials. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

How it works: The method is straightforward and checkable at every step. First, take two distinct points in the space. Second, find an open neighbourhood of each. Third, check the neighbourhoods are disjoint. Fourth, if this works for every pair, the space is Hausdorff. Fifth, in a metric space, use balls of radius half the distance between the points. Sixth, test the condition on a sequence: in a Hausdorff space a sequence can have at most one limit.
