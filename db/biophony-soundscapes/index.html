What Is a Soundscape Rank 1 · simple

A soundscape is the acoustic environment of a place, heard as a whole: every sound present at a location over time, whether made by animals, by the earth, or by people. Soundscape ecology, following Bernie Krause's vocabulary, divides that whole into three layers that must be kept distinct. Biophony is the sound of living organisms, from bird song to insect hum to whale calls. Geophony is the sound of the earth itself, including wind in trees, running water, rain, thunder, and ice. Anthrophony is human-made sound, from conversation and music to traffic, aircraft, and industry. A single place can hold all three at once, a forest with birdsong over a stream near a road, and the art of listening to a soundscape is hearing the layers separately inside the whole. This record covers all three layers and is the companion pair's authority for keeping them distinct.

Science proof

Science proof: soundscape ecology is an established field that studies acoustic environments as ecological data, using recordings, spectrograms, and indices to compare places and track change over time. Its three-layer vocabulary is published and widely used, and its methods, long recordings, band analysis, and index computation, are documented in the peer-reviewed literature. A soundscape is therefore not a poetic metaphor but a measurable object: a recording with a time, a place, and a spectrum.

Math proof

Math proof: if a soundscape recording holds biophony at 55 dB, geophony at 50 dB, and anthrophony at 60 dB, the combined level is 10 x log10(10^5.5 + 10^5 + 10^6) = 61.51 dB. The demo prints the three layer levels and the combined level 61.51 dB, plus the share of total power from anthrophony, 10^6 / (10^5.5 + 10^5 + 10^6) = 70.6 percent.

Forest Soundscape Rank 2 · simple

A temperate forest soundscape is layered by height and by hour. At dawn, birds sing from canopy and understorey in the middle bands, while insects fill the high bands later in the day and frogs call from wet ground at night. Wind through leaves adds a broad, rustling geophony that rises and falls with gusts, and rain on canopy adds a steady patter that can mask quieter voices. The forest floor itself contributes: falling leaves, snapping twigs, and the movement of animals through litter. In autumn the biophony thins as migrants leave and insects quiet, while the geophony of wind and rain grows more prominent, so the same forest sounds like a different place in October than in May. Listening across seasons is therefore the honest way to know a forest soundscape, not a single visit.

Science proof

Science proof: forest soundscape studies use dawn-chorus recordings and acoustic indices to compare forest types, ages, and management histories, and they consistently find that structurally complex, older forests support richer, more spectrally spread soundscapes than simplified plantations. Seasonal recordings document the spring peak and autumn thinning described here, and wind and rain effects on recordings are measured and corrected for in published analyses.

Math proof

Math proof: if dawn bird song occupies 6 of 10 frequency bands, insect sound occupies 3 bands, and 1 band is shared, the total occupied bands are 6 + 3 - 1 = 8, an occupancy of 8 / 10 = 80.0 percent. The demo prints bird bands 6, insect bands 3, shared bands 1, total occupied 8, and occupancy 80.0 percent.

Ocean Soundscape Rank 3 · simple

The ocean soundscape is dominated by water itself and by the animals that live in it. Waves breaking on shores, wind on the surface, and rain on the sea make a broad geophony, while snapping shrimp crackle over reefs, fish grunt and drum, and whales and dolphins call, click, and sing across great distances. Sound travels about four and a half times faster in seawater than in air, and low frequencies travel furthest, so the ocean is a long-distance acoustic world in a way the land is not. Depth matters: the deep sound channel, a layer where sound speed is at a minimum, can trap low-frequency sound and carry it across ocean basins. Human shipping adds a low-frequency anthrophony layer to many ocean soundscapes, and this record notes it as a separate layer rather than folding it into the natural ocean sound.

Science proof

Science proof: hydrophone recordings document the ocean soundscape's components directly: wave and wind noise spectra, snapping shrimp crackle, fish choruses at dawn and dusk, and whale calls localised by arrays. The deep sound channel, or SOFAR channel, is a measured physical feature of ocean sound-speed profiles, confirmed by long-range transmission experiments. Ocean soundscape monitoring is now used to track both marine life and human noise.

Math proof

Math proof: sound in seawater travels at roughly 1500 metres per second, about 1500 / 343 = 4.37 times the speed in air. A sound crossing 150 kilometres of ocean takes 150000 / 1500 = 100.0 seconds, while the same distance in air would take 150000 / 343 = 437.3 seconds. The demo prints the speed ratio 4.37, ocean travel time 100.0 seconds, and air travel time 437.3 seconds.

Desert Soundscape Rank 4 · simple

A desert soundscape is defined as much by silence as by sound. With little vegetation to rustle and few water sources, the geophony of wind over sand and rock dominates: wind hisses across dunes, hums through canyons, and, in rare singing dunes, produces a low booming tone as sand avalanches down a slip face. Biophony is sparse and scheduled: birds sing briefly at dawn and dusk, insects buzz near the few water sources, and nocturnal animals, from kangaroo rats to owls, own the night. Sound carries far in dry, still desert air, especially at night when temperature inversions trap sound near the ground, so a distant coyote chorus can seem close. The desert teaches the listener that quiet is not empty: a quiet soundscape is one in which every remaining voice stands out.

Science proof

Science proof: desert acoustic studies document the dominance of wind geophony and the dawn, dusk, and night scheduling of animal sound, driven by heat avoidance. Singing dunes are a documented physical phenomenon: sand avalanches on certain dunes produce sustained low-frequency tones, studied with seismic and acoustic instruments. Night-time temperature inversions and their effect on sound propagation are standard atmospheric acoustics.

Math proof

Math proof: if a desert night recording holds 20 minutes of coyote calls, 15 minutes of insect sound, and 10 minutes of wind-only sound in a 120-minute window, the total biophony time is 35 minutes, a share of 35 / 120 = 29.2 percent, and wind-only time is 10 / 120 = 8.3 percent. The demo prints biophony 35 minutes, biophony share 29.2 percent, and wind-only share 8.3 percent.

Wetland Soundscape Rank 5 · simple

A wetland soundscape is wet, dense, and loud at night. Frogs and toads chorus from ponds and marshes, bitterns boom from reed beds, rails call from dense cover, and insects fill the high bands through summer evenings. Water itself contributes geophony: rain pattering on open water, wind rippling reeds, and the trickle of inflows and outflows. Wetlands are among the most productive soundscapes per unit area because water supports dense animal communities and because sound carries well over open water at night. They are also among the most threatened: drained, polluted, or noise-encroached wetlands lose their choruses first, and acoustic monitoring is widely used to track wetland restoration, since a returning frog chorus is audible evidence that a restored wetland is working.

Science proof

Science proof: wetland acoustic surveys identify frog, bird, and insect species from recordings and track calling activity across breeding seasons. Restoration studies compare restored and reference wetlands acoustically, finding that acoustic richness recovers as vegetation and hydrology recover. The link between wetland health and chorus richness is published across multiple regions and taxa.

Math proof

Math proof: if a wetland chorus holds 80 calling frogs and each frog calls for 0.6 seconds every 2.4 seconds, each frog's duty cycle is 0.6 / 2.4 = 25.0 percent and the chorus produces 80 / 2.4 = 33.33 calls per second, shown as 33.3. The demo prints duty cycle 25.0 percent, calls per second 33.3, and calls per minute 2000.0.

Urban Soundscape Rank 6 · simple

An urban soundscape is an anthrophony-dominated soundscape with biophony surviving in its gaps. Traffic hums at low frequencies through the day, aircraft pass overhead, construction adds impulsive bangs, and human voices, music, and sirens fill the middle bands, while birds sing from parks, rooftops, and street trees, often louder, higher, or earlier than their rural counterparts. Urban parks and gardens act as acoustic refuges where biophony can still be heard, and the contrast between a busy street and a quiet park, sometimes only a block apart, is one of the sharpest soundscape gradients in the world. Urban soundscape research studies both problems, noise exposure and masking, and pleasures, the restorative value of birdsong and water sounds in city parks, and it treats the city as a soundscape to be designed, not merely endured.

Science proof

Science proof: urban soundscape surveys measure traffic-dominated low-frequency spectra on streets and richer biophony spectra in parks, and studies of urban birds document louder, higher-pitched, or earlier singing compared with rural populations of the same species. Soundscape-perception research finds that natural sounds in urban parks are rated as more pleasant and restorative than traffic noise at the same level, showing that soundscape quality depends on content, not level alone.

Math proof

Math proof: if street traffic measures 70 dB and a park measures 50 dB, the difference is 20 dB, which corresponds to an intensity ratio of 10^(20/10) = 100.0 times. The demo prints the level difference 20 dB, the intensity ratio 100.0, and the combined level of two 70 dB streets, 73.01 dB.

Rainforest Dawn Soundscape Rank 7 · compounded

A tropical rainforest at dawn is the richest terrestrial soundscape on earth. Howler monkeys roar from the canopy, toucans and parrots call in loud bursts, tinamous whistle from the forest floor, frogs finish their night choruses, and insects, especially cicadas, rise as the sun warms the canopy. Rain adds a sudden, drowning geophony when a shower passes, and the chorus rebuilds within minutes after it stops. The acoustic niche organisation is most visible here: dozens of species sing at once, yet each occupies its own band and time pattern, so a trained listener can pick out individual voices from the wall of sound. Rainforest soundscapes are also the most threatened by logging and fragmentation, and long-term recordings from the same forest plots document the thinning of the dawn chorus as species disappear.

Science proof

Science proof: rainforest soundscape recordings analysed by band show the densest spectral occupancy of any terrestrial habitat, with species partitioned by frequency and time as the acoustic niche hypothesis predicts. Long-term recording programmes in tropical forests document declines in acoustic richness following logging and fragmentation, and playback and survey studies confirm that many rainforest species are detectable by sound long before they are seen.

Math proof

Math proof: if a rainforest dawn recording holds 40 vocal species and each occupies an average band of 400 hertz, the total occupied bandwidth is 40 x 400 = 16000 hertz, which exceeds the 10000-hertz audible band used in this example, so overlap is unavoidable and time-sharing must help. The demo prints total occupied 16000 Hz, band width 10000 Hz, and the excess, 16000 - 10000 = 6000 Hz.

Arctic Soundscape Rank 8 · compounded

An Arctic soundscape is a soundscape of ice, wind, and brief, intense life. Sea ice groans, cracks, and booms as it shifts, a geophony of enormous low-frequency events that can travel far through ice and water. Wind over tundra and snow adds a steady hiss, and meltwater streams add summer trickles. Biophony is seasonal: migratory birds arrive in a rush of song in the short summer, seals call under the ice, and whales, including bowheads and belugas, sing and call in ice-covered waters where their low and mid-frequency voices carry for kilometres. In winter, the soundscape thins to wind and ice alone, and the silence between events is profound. Arctic soundscapes are changing measurably as sea ice retreats: open water brings more wave noise and more shipping anthrophony into waters that were once ice-quiet.

Science proof

Science proof: Arctic acoustic monitoring documents sea-ice sounds, seasonal bird and marine-mammal vocal activity, and the increase in shipping noise as ice-free seasons lengthen. Under-ice seal and whale calls are recorded by hydrophones deployed through the ice, and their seasonal patterns track migration and breeding. The link between ice loss and soundscape change is published across Arctic monitoring programmes.

Math proof

Math proof: if sea-ice cover falls from 100 percent to 60 percent of a study area, the open-water share rises from 0 percent to 40 percent. If wave noise level rises by 3 dB for each 20 percent of open water, the rise for 40 percent open water is (40 / 20) x 3 = 6.0 dB. The demo prints open water 40 percent and wave-noise rise 6.0 dB, plus the intensity ratio for 6 dB, 10^(6/10) = 3.98 times, shown as 4.0.

Grassland and Savanna Soundscape Rank 9 · compounded

A grassland or savanna soundscape is open, windy, and wide. Wind through grass makes a continuous rustling geophony that sets the background level, while birds sing from scattered trees and shrubs, insects buzz in the heat of the day, and large mammals add low rumbles, roars, and alarm calls that carry across open ground. At night, crickets and frogs near waterholes take over, and predators and prey exchange alarm calls that ripple across the plain. Because there is little to block sound, voices carry far, and the soundscape's information travels with it: an alarm call from one herd can warn animals hundreds of metres away. Fire is part of the cycle too: after a burn, the soundscape thins until grass and insects return, and the recovery of sound tracks the recovery of the community.

Science proof

Science proof: savanna acoustic studies document wind-dominated background spectra, long-distance mammal vocal communication, and the role of alarm calls in multi-species predator warning networks. Post-fire acoustic monitoring shows reduced richness immediately after burning followed by recovery as vegetation and insect communities return, matching visual survey findings.

Math proof

Math proof: if an alarm call is audible to 500 metres in still air but wind raises background noise by 6 dB, and each 6 dB of added noise roughly halves the audible range, the new range is 500 / 2 = 250.0 metres. The demo prints original range 500 metres, reduced range 250.0 metres, and the area covered before, 3.14159 x 500^2 = 785398.2 square metres, shown as 785398 square metres, and after, 196349 square metres.

River and Stream Soundscape Rank 10 · compounded

A river soundscape is built on moving water. Rapids and riffles produce broad, steady, white-noise-like geophony that fills the low and middle bands, waterfalls add a roaring low-frequency component, and quiet pools let biophony through: dippers call from mid-stream rocks, kingfishers rattle along the bank, frogs call from backwaters, and insects hum over the surface. The balance between water noise and animal sound shifts with flow: in spate, after rain, the river drowns nearly everything, while in low summer flow, the biophony emerges clearly. River soundscapes matter to the animals themselves: species that live beside noisy water, such as torrent ducks and some frogs, have evolved calls pitched above the water's dominant band or timed into the gaps between surges, a neat example of acoustic niche adaptation to geophony.

Science proof

Science proof: river soundscape studies measure water-noise spectra at different flows and show that animal call detection falls as flow rises, confirming masking by geophony. Species beside fast water are documented calling at higher frequencies or with altered timing compared with relatives beside quiet water, consistent with adaptation to the water-noise band. River acoustic monitoring is used to estimate discharge and to track riparian species.

Math proof

Math proof: if river noise measures 65 dB and a bird call measures 60 dB in the same band, the call is 5 dB below the noise, and the signal-to-noise ratio is 60 - 65 = -5 dB, meaning the call is masked. The demo prints signal-to-noise -5 dB, and for a louder 70 dB call, signal-to-noise 5 dB, plus the combined level of river and 70 dB call, 71.19 dB.

Night Soundscape Rank 11 · compounded

The night soundscape is a different world from the day's. Birds largely fall silent, and the biophony passes to owls, nightjars, frogs, crickets, and katydids, with mammals, from bats to foxes to deer, adding rustles, barks, and screams. Geophony changes too: wind often drops after sunset, rain sounds sharper against quiet, and distant sounds carry further in the cool, still night air. Anthrophony thins in rural places but persists in cities, where traffic and nightlife hold the night soundscape at a higher level. Night listening rewards patience: a listener who sits still for an hour hears the soundscape's structure, the owl's territory, the frog chorus's pulse, the insect band's steady hum, in a way no daytime visit reveals. Night soundscapes are also the most vulnerable to light and noise pollution, both of which shift animal activity away from the dark hours.

Science proof

Science proof: night acoustic monitoring documents the distinct nocturnal community, including owl territories, frog choruses, and insect bands, and shows that night recordings detect species missed by day surveys. Studies of light and noise pollution show shifted calling times and reduced nocturnal activity in affected areas. The physics of night sound propagation, cooler, calmer air carrying sound further, is standard atmospheric acoustics.

Math proof

Math proof: if a night recording holds 45 minutes of cricket sound, 20 minutes of frog calls, and 15 minutes of owl calls in a 120-minute window, with 10 minutes of overlap between crickets and frogs, the total biophony time is 45 + 20 + 15 - 10 = 70 minutes, a share of 70 / 120 = 58.3 percent. The demo prints total 70 minutes and share 58.3 percent, plus the silent remainder, 120 - 70 = 50 minutes.

Seasonal Soundscape Change Rank 12 · complex

A soundscape is not a fixed object: it changes with the seasons, and listening across a year is the only way to know a place's full acoustic life. Spring brings migrant songbirds and breeding choruses, summer brings insects and fledgling begging calls, autumn brings departing migrants and rutting deer, and winter brings wind, ice, and the calls of resident species that stay. Geophony follows its own seasons: spring meltwater, summer thunderstorms, autumn gales, and winter ice. Anthrophony has seasons too: harvest machinery in autumn, snowploughs in winter, tourism in summer. Long-term recordings capture all of this, and ecologists use seasonal soundscape patterns, phenology, to track climate change, since earlier springs shift the arrival of song and chorus by measurable days. This record covers all three layers, because seasonal change touches each of them.

Science proof

Science proof: long-term acoustic monitoring programmes document seasonal phenology in bird song, frog choruses, and insect activity, and show shifts in timing correlated with temperature trends. Seasonal soundscape indices computed month by month reveal the annual cycle and its anomalies, and those anomalies are published alongside temperature and migration records for cross-checking.

Math proof

Math proof: if spring chorus onset advances by 2 days per decade, then over 5 decades it advances by 2 x 5 = 10 days. The demo prints advance per decade 2 days, decades 5, total advance 10 days, and the new onset day if the old onset was day 100 of the year, day 90.

Storm Soundscape Rank 13 · complex

A storm soundscape is geophony at full power. Wind rises from a whisper to a roar that fills every band, rain hammers roofs, leaves, and water, thunder rolls across the sky with low-frequency energy that can be felt in the chest, and lightning's crack arrives as a sharp transient. Biophony responds in stages: birds fall silent before the storm, insects quiet as rain begins, frogs may call louder in the rain's lulls, and after the storm passes, the chorus rebuilds, sometimes with a burst of activity as animals resume feeding and calling. Storm sound is also a measurement tool: thunder delay gives lightning distance, rain noise on the sea surface is used to estimate rainfall over oceans, and the sound of wind through vegetation is studied as a proxy for storm intensity. A storm recording, heard in full, is a lesson in how completely geophony can dominate a soundscape, and how quickly biophony returns.

Science proof

Science proof: thunder is produced by the explosive heating and expansion of air along a lightning channel, and its low-frequency roll and delay are measured with microphones and lightning-location networks. Rain noise on water surfaces is a documented acoustic signal used in ocean rainfall estimation. Animal responses to storms, including pre-storm silence and post-storm chorus recovery, are documented in field recordings.

Math proof

Math proof: thunder distance equals the speed of sound times the delay between lightning and thunder. For a delay of 15 seconds, distance = 343 x 15 = 5145 metres, about 5.1 kilometres. The demo prints 5145 metres for 15 seconds, 3430 metres for 10 seconds, and 10290 metres for 30 seconds, each computed as 343 times the delay.

Coastal Soundscape Rank 14 · complex

A coastal soundscape is where land, sea, and human activity meet in sound. Waves break with a rhythmic surge and crash, the period set by swell and shore shape, while wind adds a steady hiss and gulls, terns, and waders call over the water. Below the surface, the coastal ocean holds snapping shrimp, fish choruses, and, in season, whale calls, so the coast has two soundscapes, one above water and one below, linked by the shore itself. Anthrophony is rarely far away: harbours, boats, beach visitors, and coastal roads add engine and voice noise, and in busy coasts the boat layer can dominate the underwater soundscape. Tides shape the whole pattern: a high tide brings waves closer and louder, a low tide exposes mudflats where waders feed and call, and the twice-daily cycle gives coastal soundscapes a rhythm no inland place matches.

Science proof

Science proof: coastal soundscape studies measure wave-noise spectra against tide and swell, document seabird colonies acoustically, and use hydrophones to track fish choruses, shrimp snaps, and boat noise in the same waters. Tidal modulation of coastal sound levels is a measured effect, and boat-noise masking of fish communication is documented in harbour and reef studies.

Math proof

Math proof: if waves break every 8 seconds, the wave rate is 60 / 8 = 7.5 waves per minute, or 450 waves per hour. The demo prints 7.5 waves per minute and 450 waves per hour, plus the waves in a 6-hour tidal half-cycle, 450 x 6 = 2700 waves.

Soundscape Restoration and Quiet Places Rank 15 · complex

Soundscape restoration asks a simple question: what does a place sound like when it is allowed to heal? Protected areas, rewilded land, and restored wetlands show the answer acoustically: as vegetation returns and disturbance falls, bird song, frog choruses, and insect bands return, and the soundscape's richness rises measurably. The quiet-places movement takes the same idea further, seeking to protect naturally quiet places from noise intrusion. Gordon Hempton, an acoustic ecologist, identified One Square Inch of Silence in Olympic National Park in Washington State as a place to protect from noise, arguing that a single quiet square inch, kept free of human noise, symbolises the protection of natural quiet everywhere. Quiet is not the goal for its own sake: the goal is a soundscape in which biophony and geophony can be heard clearly, with anthrophony present but not dominant. This record covers all three layers, because restoration is judged by their balance.

Science proof

Science proof: restoration acoustics studies compare restored and reference sites and find that acoustic richness and biophony levels recover alongside vegetation and animal communities, making sound a rapid restoration indicator. The One Square Inch of Silence project is a documented conservation effort with published reasoning about natural quiet. Soundscape-perception research supports the restorative value of natural sound for human listeners as well as its ecological value.

Math proof

Math proof: if a restored site's biophony power rises from 0.4 to 0.8 while anthrophony power stays at 0.2, NDSI rises from (0.4 - 0.2) / (0.4 + 0.2) = 0.33 to (0.8 - 0.2) / (0.8 + 0.2) = 0.60. The demo prints NDSI 0.33 before restoration and 0.60 after, plus the improvement, 0.60 - 0.33 = 0.27.
