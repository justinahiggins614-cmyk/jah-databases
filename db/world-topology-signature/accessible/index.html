What Topology Is

Topology is the branch of mathematics that studies the properties of shapes that survive stretching, bending, and twisting, but not tearing or gluing. A coffee cup and a doughnut are the same in topology, because each has one hole; a sphere and a cube are the same, because neither has a hole. Topology asks what is essential about a shape when size, distance, and angles are ignored.

A coffee cup and a doughnut are the same topologically: push the cup's handle out and flatten the cup, and the handle becomes the doughnut's hole, with one hole throughout. A sphere and a cube are the same: inflate the cube's faces and the corners smooth out, with no hole at any stage. But a sphere and a doughnut are different: no amount of stretching turns a sphere into a doughnut, because the hole cannot be created without tearing. The Euler characteristic tells them apart: a sphere has 2, a torus has 0.

Open Sets and Continuity

An open set is the basic building block of topology: a set in which every point has a little neighbourhood entirely inside the set. In the real line, an open interval (0, 1) is open, because around any point inside it there is a smaller interval still inside; the closed interval [0, 1] is not, because 0 and 1 have no neighbourhood inside. A function is continuous when the preimage of every open set is open.

The interval (0, 1) is open: take any point x inside, and the interval (x - d, x + d) with d the smaller of x and 1 - x lies inside (0, 1). The interval [0, 1] is not open: around 0, every interval (-d, d) contains negative numbers outside [0, 1]. The set (0, 1) union (2, 3) is open, because it is a union of opens. The function f(x) = x^2 is continuous: the preimage of the open interval (1, 4) is (-2, -1) union (1, 2), which is open.

Homeomorphisms

A homeomorphism is a continuous deformation with a continuous inverse: a map between two spaces that stretches and bends one into the other without tearing or gluing, and can be undone the same way. Two spaces related by a homeomorphism are topologically the same, and every topological property is shared. A sphere and a cube are homeomorphic; a sphere and a torus are not.

A sphere and a cube are homeomorphic: inflate the cube by pushing each point out along a ray from the centre until it meets the sphere, and the map is continuous with a continuous inverse. A circle and a square are homeomorphic the same way. But a circle and a figure-eight are not: removing one point from a circle leaves it connected, while removing the crossing point from a figure-eight disconnects it into four pieces, so no homeomorphism can exist — connectedness after removing a point is an invariant. A line and a circle are not homeomorphic: removing a point disconnects the line into two pieces but leaves the circle connected.

Connectedness

A space is connected when it is all in one piece: it cannot be split into two non-empty open sets that do not touch. An interval is connected; two separate intervals are not. Connectedness is the simplest topological invariant, and it is preserved by continuous maps: the continuous image of a connected space is connected. It is the topological version of being in one piece, and it is the first thing checked about any space.

The interval [0, 1] is connected: it cannot be split into two open pieces, because any split would need a gap, and the real line has no gaps. The set (0, 1) union (2, 3) is disconnected: it splits into (0, 1) and (2, 3), two non-empty open sets. The topologist's sine curve is connected but not path-connected: it is the graph of sin(1/x) for x > 0 together with the segment at x = 0, and no path can reach the segment. A circle is connected and path-connected: any two points can be joined by an arc.

Compactness

A space is compact when every open cover has a finite subcover: however you cover it with open sets, a few of them already cover it. In the real line, a set is compact exactly when it is closed and bounded: [0, 1] is compact, (0, 1) is not, and the whole line is not. Compactness is the topological form of finiteness, and it is what makes continuous functions on a compact space attain their maxima and minima.

The interval [0, 1] is compact: it is closed and bounded, so Heine-Borel applies. The function f(x) = x^2 on [0, 1] attains its maximum 1 at x = 1 and its minimum 0 at x = 0, as the extreme value theorem promises. The interval (0, 1) is not compact: the cover by (1/n, 1) for n = 2, 3, ... has no finite subcover, because any finite few leave out points near 0. The whole real line is not compact: it is not bounded. A circle is compact: it is closed and bounded in the plane.

The Hausdorff Condition

A space is Hausdorff when any two distinct points can be separated by disjoint open neighbourhoods: around each point there is an open set, and the two open sets do not meet. The condition is named after Felix Hausdorff, who introduced it in 1914. The real line, the plane, and every metric space are Hausdorff. The condition rules out pathological spaces where points cannot be told apart, and it is what makes limits unique.

In the real line, take the points 0 and 1: the neighbourhoods (-0.25, 0.25) and (0.75, 1.25) are disjoint, so the points are separated. In general, for points a and b at distance d, the balls of radius d/2 around each are disjoint. A sequence in a Hausdorff space has at most one limit: if a sequence converged to both 0 and 1, every neighbourhood of 0 and every neighbourhood of 1 would contain the tail of the sequence, but disjoint neighbourhoods cannot both contain it. The line with two origins — two copies of the real line glued everywhere except at 0 — is not Hausdorff, because the two origins cannot be separated.

Manifolds

A manifold is a space that looks locally like ordinary Euclidean space: every point has a neighbourhood that is homeomorphic to an open set in R^n, for a fixed n called the dimension. A circle is a 1-manifold, a sphere is a 2-manifold, and spacetime in general relativity is a 4-manifold. Manifolds are where topology meets geometry: they have a shape globally and a familiar structure locally.

A circle is a 1-manifold: around any point, a small arc looks like an open interval, and two overlapping arcs cover the whole circle. A sphere is a 2-manifold: stereographic projection maps the sphere minus a point to the whole plane, and two such projections, from the north and south poles, form an atlas. A torus is a 2-manifold: it is the product of two circles, and locally it looks like a plane. The surface of a doughnut, the surface of a ball, and the plane are all 2-manifolds, but they differ globally: the plane is not compact, the sphere has Euler characteristic 2, and the torus has 0.

Surfaces and the Classification Theorem

A surface is a 2-dimensional manifold: a space that locally looks like a plane. The classification theorem says every compact connected surface is determined by two numbers: whether it is orientable, and how many holes it has. The orientable surfaces are the sphere with g handles: g = 0 is the sphere, g = 1 is the torus, g = 2 is the double torus. The non-orientable surfaces are the sphere with k cross-caps: k = 1 is the projective plane, k = 2 is the Klein bottle.

A sphere has Euler characteristic 2, so its genus is 0. A torus has characteristic 0, so 2 - 2g = 0 gives g = 1: one handle. A double torus has characteristic -2, so 2 - 2g = -2 gives g = 2. The Klein bottle has characteristic 0 and is non-orientable, so it is the sphere with 2 cross-caps. The projective plane has characteristic 1 and is non-orientable, so it is the sphere with 1 cross-cap. The Euler characteristic of a surface with g handles and b boundary circles is 2 - 2g - b.

The Euler Characteristic

The Euler characteristic is the number V - E + F, where V, E, and F are the numbers of vertices, edges, and faces in a triangulation of a surface. It is a topological invariant: it does not change when the surface is deformed, and it depends only on the topology. For a sphere it is 2, for a torus 0, and for a surface of genus g it is 2 - 2g. It is the oldest topological invariant.

A cube has 8 vertices, 12 edges, and 6 faces, so chi = 8 - 12 + 6 = 2, the same as a sphere, as it must be, since a cube is homeomorphic to a sphere. A tetrahedron has 4 vertices, 6 edges, and 4 faces, so chi = 4 - 6 + 4 = 2. A torus triangulated with 9 vertices, 27 edges, and 18 faces has chi = 9 - 27 + 18 = 0. The Euler characteristic of a surface of genus g is 2 - 2g, so a genus-3 surface has chi = 2 - 6 = -4.

The Möbius Strip and Klein Bottle

The Möbius strip is a surface with one side: take a strip of paper, give it a half-twist, and join the ends, and the result has a single edge and a single side. The Klein bottle is a closed surface with one side and no inside or outside: it is a bottle whose neck passes through its side and joins its base, and it cannot be built in three dimensions without self-intersection. Both are non-orientable surfaces.

A Möbius strip made from a strip 30 cm long and 3 cm wide has one edge of length 60 cm, because the edge goes around twice before closing. Cutting it down the middle gives a single longer strip with two full twists, not two strips. Cutting it a third of the way from the edge gives two linked strips: one Möbius strip and one longer strip with two twists. The Klein bottle has Euler characteristic 0, like the torus, but it is non-orientable, so it is the sphere with 2 cross-caps, while the torus is the sphere with 1 handle.

Knots and Links

A knot is a closed loop embedded in three-dimensional space: a circle that is tangled but not cut. Two knots are the same when one can be deformed into the other without cutting, which is ambient isotopy. Knot theory classifies knots by invariants: the crossing number, the Jones polynomial, and the knot group. The unknot, the trefoil, and the figure-eight are the simplest knots, with 0, 3, and 4 crossings.

The trefoil knot has 3 crossings and Jones polynomial q^(-1) + q^(-3) - q^(-4) in one convention. The figure-eight knot has 4 crossings and is amphichiral: it is the same as its mirror image, unlike the trefoil, which is chiral. The unknot has crossing number 0 and Jones polynomial 1. The Hopf link has linking number 1: the two circles wind around each other once. Tait's tables list 1 knot with 3 crossings, 1 with 4, 2 with 5, 3 with 6, and 7 with 7 — the numbers grow fast, and there are 1,701,936 prime knots with up to 16 crossings.

The Fundamental Group

The fundamental group is the group of loops in a space, up to homotopy: two loops are the same when one can be deformed into the other. It measures the holes a loop can wind around. The fundamental group of a circle is the integers, because a loop can wind around any number of times, in either direction. The fundamental group of a sphere is trivial, because every loop can be shrunk to a point.

The fundamental group of the circle is Z: a loop that winds around once is 1, twice is 2, once backwards is -1, and the constant loop is 0. The fundamental group of the torus is Z times Z: a loop is identified by how many times it winds around each of the two circles. The fundamental group of a sphere is trivial: every loop on a sphere can be shrunk to a point, because there is no hole for it to wind around. The fundamental group of a figure-eight is the free group on two generators: loops around each lobe, with no relation between them.

Homotopy

Homotopy is continuous deformation: two maps are homotopic when one can be deformed into the other through a family of continuous maps. Two spaces are homotopy equivalent when there are maps back and forth whose compositions are homotopic to the identities. Homotopy is coarser than homeomorphism: a disc and a point are homotopy equivalent, but not homeomorphic, because the disc can be shrunk to a point.

A disc is homotopy equivalent to a point: shrink the disc radially to its centre, and the shrinking is a homotopy from the identity map to the constant map. A circle is not contractible: no homotopy can shrink it to a point, because the winding number is a homotopy invariant and the identity map has winding number 1 while the constant map has 0. The cylinder and the circle are homotopy equivalent: the cylinder deformation-retracts onto its central circle. The sphere and the point are not homotopy equivalent: the sphere's second homotopy group is Z, while the point's is trivial.

Homology

Homology counts holes algebraically: it assigns to each space a sequence of groups, the homology groups, whose ranks — the Betti numbers — count the holes of each dimension. The 0th Betti number counts components, the 1st counts loops, and the 2nd counts voids. A torus has Betti numbers 1, 2, 1: one component, two independent loops, one void. Homology is computable, unlike the fundamental group, and it is the workhorse of algebraic topology.

The torus has Betti numbers 1, 2, 1: one component, two independent loops (around the tube and around the hole), and one void (the inside). The sphere has Betti numbers 1, 0, 1: one component, no loops, one void. The circle has 1, 1, 0: one component, one loop, no void. The figure-eight has 1, 2, 0: one component and two loops. The Euler characteristic is the alternating sum of the Betti numbers: for the torus, 1 - 2 + 1 = 0, which matches V - E + F.

The Brouwer Fixed-Point Theorem

The Brouwer fixed-point theorem says that any continuous map from a disc to itself has a fixed point: a point that the map leaves in place. Stir a cup of coffee, and at least one point of the coffee ends up exactly where it started — though it may have moved in between. The theorem is true for a disc, a ball, and any space homeomorphic to them, and it is false for a circle, where a rotation has no fixed point.

Stir a cup of coffee: the coffee moves continuously, and the map from the disc of coffee to itself must have a fixed point — some particle ends where it started. Crumple a map of a country and drop it on the ground inside the country's borders: some point of the crumpled map lies exactly over the point it represents, because the crumpling is a continuous map from the disc to itself. A rotation of a circle has no fixed point, because the circle is not a disc: the theorem needs the disc, with its interior, not just its boundary.

The Hairy Ball Theorem

The hairy ball theorem says you cannot comb a hairy ball flat: any continuous tangent vector field on a sphere must vanish somewhere — there is always at least one cowlick. More precisely, the sphere has no continuous nowhere-zero tangent vector field. The torus, by contrast, can be combed: it has a nowhere-zero tangent vector field, because it is flat in the relevant sense.

Comb a tennis ball: wherever you start, the hair must swirl into at least one cowlick, because the indices of the zeros sum to 2 and a single zero has index 1 or 2. A doughnut, by contrast, can be combed: its Euler characteristic is 0, so a field with no zeros is possible, and indeed the field that points around the tube is continuous and nowhere zero. A sphere with a vector field that points outward from the north pole and inward at the south pole has two zeros, each of index 1, summing to 2, as the theorem requires.

The Poincaré Conjecture

The Poincaré conjecture says that every simply connected, closed three-dimensional manifold is homeomorphic to the three-sphere. In plain words: if a three-dimensional space has no holes that a loop can wind around, and it is finite and without boundary, then it is a sphere. It was stated by Henri Poincaré in 1904, and it was the most famous open problem in topology for a century, until Grigori Perelman proved it in 2003.

The 2-sphere is the analogue in two dimensions: a simply connected closed surface is a sphere, and that was known in the 19th century. In three dimensions, the 3-torus is not simply connected: a loop around its hole cannot be shrunk, so the conjecture does not apply to it. The Poincaré homology sphere is a 3-manifold with the same homology as the 3-sphere but a non-trivial fundamental group, so it is not a counterexample — the conjecture requires simple connectedness, not just homology. Perelman's proof took three papers, 2002 to 2003, and the verification took until 2006.

Dimension

Dimension is the number of independent directions in a space: a line is 1-dimensional, a plane is 2-dimensional, and ordinary space is 3-dimensional. In topology, dimension is defined carefully, because there are spaces whose dimension is not obvious: a fractal can have a non-integer dimension, and a space can be topologically 1-dimensional but look complicated. The dimension of a manifold is the number of coordinates needed locally.

A line has dimension 1: one coordinate suffices. A plane has dimension 2: two coordinates. A cube's surface has dimension 2, though it sits in 3-dimensional space. The Koch curve has Hausdorff dimension log(4)/log(3), about 1.26: each iteration replaces a segment by 4 segments of a third the length, so the dimension is log 4 / log 3. A coastline measured with a 1 km ruler is shorter than the same coastline measured with a 1 m ruler, because the finer ruler follows more detail — that is what a dimension between 1 and 2 means.

Fractals and Topological Dimension

A fractal is a shape with detail at every scale: zoom in and the pattern repeats, roughly or exactly. Its Hausdorff dimension is usually fractional, larger than its topological dimension: the Koch curve is topologically 1-dimensional but has Hausdorff dimension about 1.26. Fractals model coastlines, clouds, mountains, and blood vessels — shapes that ordinary geometry cannot describe. They appear throughout nature, from the branching of trees to the shapes of clouds and the structure of lungs.

The Koch curve starts as a segment, replaces the middle third by two sides of a triangle, and repeats. Each step multiplies the number of segments by 4 and divides their length by 3, so the dimension is log(4)/log(3) = 1.262. After 4 steps there are 4^4 = 256 segments, each of length (1/3)^4 = 1/81, so the total length is 256/81 = 3.16 times the original. A coastline of Britain measured with a 200 km ruler is about 2,400 km long, and with a 50 km ruler about 3,400 km: the length grows as the ruler shrinks, which is what a dimension between 1 and 2 means.

Topology in Data Analysis

Topological data analysis finds the shape of data: given a cloud of points, it builds a simplicial complex at many scales and watches which topological features — components, loops, voids — persist. A loop that persists across many scales is real signal; one that appears and vanishes is noise. The output is a persistence diagram, and the method is called persistent homology.

Take points sampled from a circle with noise: at a small threshold the complex is many separate components; as the threshold grows, the components join into one, and a loop appears — the circle itself. The loop persists over a wide range of thresholds, while small spurious loops appear and vanish quickly. In the persistence diagram the circle's loop is a point far from the diagonal, and the noise is a cluster near it. For points sampled from a sphere, the persistent feature is a void, with Betti number b_2 = 1, which persists while the noise does not.

Topology in Physics

Physics uses topology wherever shape matters more than size: the topology of a material determines whether it conducts, the topology of spacetime determines what can happen, and the topology of a field determines its defects. Topological insulators conduct on their surfaces but not inside, and the property is protected: small changes cannot destroy it, because topology is robust. The quantum Hall effect, discovered in 1980, is topological: the Hall conductance is an integer, a topological invariant.

The quantum Hall effect: in a two-dimensional electron gas at low temperature and high magnetic field, the Hall conductance is quantised in units of e^2/h, about 3.87 x 10^-5 siemens, times an integer, the Chern number. The integer is exact, to better than one part in a billion, because it is topological. A topological insulator like bismuth selenide conducts on its surface with a single Dirac cone, and the conduction survives impurities that would destroy ordinary conduction. A vortex in a superfluid is labelled by an integer winding number, and it cannot be removed without cutting the fluid.

Metric Spaces

A metric space is a set with a distance function: a rule that assigns a non-negative number d(x, y) to each pair of points, with d(x, y) = 0 exactly when x = y, d(x, y) = d(y, x), and the triangle inequality d(x, z) <= d(x, y) + d(y, z). The real line with d(x, y) = |x - y|, the plane with Euclidean distance, and the set of functions with the supremum distance are all metric spaces. Every metric space is a topological space, with open sets given by balls.

On the real line, d(0, 3) = 3 and d(3, 7) = 4, and the triangle inequality says d(0, 7) = 7 <= 3 + 4 = 7, with equality because 3 lies between 0 and 7. The sequence 1, 1.4, 1.41, 1.414, ... is Cauchy and converges to the square root of 2 in the reals, but in the rationals it has no limit, so the rationals are incomplete. The contraction f(x) = (x + 1)/2 on [0, 1] has Lipschitz constant 1/2, so iterating it from any start converges to the fixed point 1: after 10 steps the error is at most (1/2)^10, about 0.001.

A History of Topology

The history of topology runs from Euler's bridge problem in 1736 to the proof of the Poincaré conjecture in 2003. It began as analysis situs, the analysis of position, became a subject in its own right with Poincaré in 1895, and grew into the central organising discipline of 20th-century mathematics, with applications in physics, data, and computing that its founders could not have imagined.

The timeline is long: Euler in 1736, Listing's word topology in 1847, the Möbius strip in 1858, Riemann's lecture in 1854, Poincaré in 1895, Hausdorff in 1914, the surface classification in 1907, and Perelman in 2003. From Euler's bridges to Perelman's proof is 267 years. The word topology comes from the Greek topos, place, and logos, study — the study of place. The subject's first problem, the bridges, was about a walk; its most famous problem, the Poincaré conjecture, was about the shape of space itself.

Topology in the Modern World

Topology is everywhere in the modern world: it shapes data analysis, protects quantum computers, classifies materials, and plans robot motions. The mathematics that began with a puzzle about bridges now reads the shape of data, designs materials that conduct only on their surfaces, and stores information in the braiding of particles. Topology is the study of shape, and shape is everywhere.

The quantum Hall conductance is exact to better than one part in a billion, because it is a topological invariant. A topological insulator's surface conducts even when the material is disordered, because the conduction is protected. A persistence diagram of a brain scan can distinguish a healthy brain from a diseased one by the shape of its activity. A robot arm with six joints has a configuration space that is a six-dimensional torus, and planning its motion means finding a path in that torus. The observable universe is at least 93 billion light-years across, and its global topology is still unknown.
