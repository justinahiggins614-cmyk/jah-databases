JAH-KW-HOUGH-000101 — JAH's weighted vote — strong edges vote louder

One pixel, one vote is democratic; JAH's election is meritocratic. Not all edge pixels are equal — a gradient of 200 is a wall, a gradient of 20 is texture — so his accumulator adds the pixel's gradient strength to each bin it votes for instead of a flat 1. The strong wall pixel votes 10 times louder than the whisper. The tower that wins is built of conviction, not headcount.

The weighting changes what the threshold means: TAU_W is now a strength budget, not a pixel count. Three strong pixels (10+10+10 = 30) outvote ten weak ones (10x2 = 20), which is exactly right — three wall pixels ARE more evidence of a line than ten texture specks. And the loud clutter pixel, strength 9 voting alone for its phantom bin, reaches 9 against the line's 32: loud, but alone, and alone never wins.

The demo votes four y=x pixels with strengths [10, 10, 10, 2] plus one clutter pixel of strength 9 voting elsewhere. Weighted tower at (135, 0): 32. Clutter bin: 9. Threshold 25: line FOUND, clutter ignored. Unweighted, the count would be 4 vs 1 — also fine here, but the weights are what let three strong pixels beat ten weak ones in the general case. Signature-original to JAH, demonstrated on synthetic images.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Edge pixels WITH their gradient magnitudes (the nine-number kernels provide both).
The recipe
Vote as usual, but add the pixel's gradient strength to the bin instead of 1. Strong edges elect; weak ones whisper. The accumulator holds evidence, not headcounts.
The knowing-when rule
FOUND iff weighted_peak >= TAU_W (here 25): the winning shape must command strong evidence, not just many weak pixels. Strength-weighted consensus.

def convolve(image, kernel):
    kh, kw = len(kernel), len(kernel[0])
    rows, cols = len(image), len(image[0])
    out = []
    for i in range(rows - kh + 1):
        r = []

                    s += kernel[a][b] * image[i + a][j + b]

JAH-KW-HOUGH-000102 — The vote ledger — JAH's auditable election

An accumulator bin holding '4' is a claim; JAH's vote ledger is the proof. Every vote is logged as a triple — which pixel voted, for which theta, for which rho — so that after the election, the winning bin can be audited: name the four voters, check they are distinct pixels, check they actually lie on the claimed line. A peak with a clean ledger is evidence; a peak without one is a rumor with a number attached.

The ledger catches what the counts cannot. Two pixels voting twice (a bookkeeping bug), one fat edge smeared across four bins (double counting), a peak built from duplicate votes — the audit exposes them all, because it names names. JAH's rule is legalistic: the election is not over when the peak is found, it is over when the peak's voters check out.

The demo runs the y=x election with full ledgering, then audits the (135, 0) peak: voters (1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (4,4) — four distinct pixels, all satisfying y=x. CERTIFIED. The outlier's votes are in the ledger too, scattered and lonely, which is exactly where the audit expects to find them. Signature-original to JAH.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
The voting process itself; the ledger is JAH's bookkeeping over it.
The recipe
Append (voter_id, theta, rho) to the ledger for every vote cast. After peak-picking, audit: list the winning bin's voters. A peak you cannot audit is a rumor.
The knowing-when rule
CERTIFIED iff the winning bin's voter list is non-empty, distinct, and matches the claimed shape's geometry. The ledger turns the peak from a number into evidence.

JAH-KW-HOUGH-000103 — The early-stop accumulator — JAH's unbeatable-lead rule

The public probabilistic Hough stops when a bin crosses a threshold; JAH's early-stop rule stops earlier, on a certificate. After each vote, compute the lead — peak votes minus runner-up votes — and compare it to the votes still uncast. If the lead exceeds the remaining votes, the runner-up cannot catch up even if every remaining voter defects to it. The election is mathematically over, whatever the threshold says. It is the same logic that lets a sports broadcast call a game before the clock runs out.

The certificate is stronger than a threshold in both directions. Below the threshold, it can still stop: peak 3, runner-up 0, 2 votes left — 3 > 2, winner certain, stop, even though no threshold was crossed. Above the threshold it stops at the same moment the threshold would. JAH's rule never counts a vote whose outcome is already decided, which is the purest form of his 'knowing when the shape is made'.

The demo casts 5 votes one at a time (4 for the line, 1 scattered). After vote 3: peak 3, runner-up 0, remaining 2 — lead 3 > 2, STOP. The election ends with two voters unheard and the answer certified. Fewer votes than the threshold stop, same winner, mathematical proof. Signature-original to JAH.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Votes cast one at a time; the stop rule needs the remaining-voter count.
The recipe
After each vote, check: if peak_votes - runner_up_votes > votes_still_uncast, stop. No future vote can change the winner — the lead is unbeatable, the election is over.
The knowing-when rule
STOP iff lead > remaining: a mathematical certificate, not a heuristic. This is stronger than the threshold stop (record: public #7) — it stops even below TAU when the outcome is certain.

JAH-KW-HOUGH-000104 — The rho-theta abacus — JAH's hand-computed election

Before the accumulator was an array, JAH wanted it to be a table you could fill in by hand — his rho-theta abacus. Three pixels, two angles, six votes, tallied as marks on paper: the student computes rho = x cos(theta) + y sin(theta) for each pixel at each angle, makes a mark in the table, and circles the tallest column. The peak is not an argmax call; it is the column your eye lands on. The abacus teaches what the array hides: voting is just organized tallying.

The teaching value is the transparency. Every vote is visible, every tally is checkable, and the moment of recognition — the circling of the peak — is a physical act. JAH's ladder demands that every principle be teachable at the bottom rung, and the abacus is the bottom rung of voting: no code, no arrays, just a table and a pencil. (The demo uses code to print the table, but the table is the point.)

The demo runs the abacus on (0,0), (1,1), (2,2) at theta 90 and 135: at 90 all three vote rho 0, 1, 2 (scattered); at 135 all three vote rho 0 (tally III — circled). The student sees the sine curves collapse into agreement at exactly the line's normal angle. Signature-original to JAH.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
Any edge pixels; the abacus is JAH's pedagogical instrument, not a production tool.
The recipe
Lay out the accumulator as a table; for each pixel, compute its rho at each theta by hand (rho = x cos + y sin) and tally. Circle the tallest tally: the line.
The knowing-when rule
The abacus teaches the rule by showing it: the peak is visible as the tallest tally mark, and 'knowing when' is the moment your eye finds it.

JAH-KW-HOUGH-000105 — The second-peak rule — JAH's sequential elections

Non-maximum suppression (public record 3) finds all peaks at once and then argues about which bins are 'the same hill'. JAH's second-peak rule sidesteps the argument: hold one election, take the winner, strike its voters from the rolls using the ledger, and vote again. The second election cannot re-find the first line — its voters are gone — so whatever wins is genuinely a second shape. No neighbor comparisons, no hill definitions, just sequential clean elections.

The ledger is what makes striking possible: you need to know exactly which pixels voted for the winner, and only the ledger names them. Voter removal also sharpens later elections — with the dominant line's pixels gone, a faint second line's tower stands taller against a lower noise floor. JAH's elections get easier as they go, the opposite of simultaneous peak-picking.

The demo holds sequential elections on the y=x and y=5-x crossing lines. Election 1: peak (135, 0), 3 votes — line one found, its 3 voters struck. Election 2 (remaining pixels): peak (45, 4), 3 votes — line two found. Election 3: best bin 1 vote, below TAU — STOP. Two shapes, two clean elections, one honest halt. Signature-original to JAH.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
The vote ledger (record 102): you can only strike voters you recorded.
The recipe
Hold the election; take the peak; remove its ledgered voters from the pixel set; vote again. Each election finds exactly one shape, cleanly, with no neighbor-suppression heuristics.
The knowing-when rule
NEXT SHAPE iff the new peak >= TAU after voter removal; STOP when no peak clears TAU. Shapes are found in strength order, each certified by its own election.

JAH-KW-HOUGH-000106 — The consensus audit — JAH's spread requirement

Four adjacent pixels in a 2x2 clump can vote the same (theta, rho) bin — they are nearly collinear, after all — and build a tower of 4 that the plain peak rule would crown as a line. But it is not a line; it is one fat edge pixel smeared across four, and crowning it is exactly the kind of lie the recognition layer exists to prevent. JAH's consensus audit adds the missing requirement: the winning bin's voters must be SPREAD OUT. A line is long by definition; its evidence must span distance.

The audit reads the ledger (record 102) — names the voters, measures their maximum pairwise distance, and demands it clear SPAN_MIN. The clump's span is ~1.4 (diagonal of the 2x2); the true line's pixels span ~4.2. Same vote count, opposite verdicts: the audit sees what the accumulator cannot, because the accumulator threw away geometry to count.

The demo runs both scenes through peak-picking plus the spread audit. True line y=x: peak 4, span 4.2 — CERTIFIED. The 2x2 clump: peak 4, span 1.4 — REJECTED ('one fat edge, not a line'). The tower was tall; the voters were huddled. JAH's rule: votes need extent. Signature-original.

The recognition principle

Which kernels feed it
The vote ledger's voter positions; spread is measured on the voters, not the votes.
The recipe
After peak-picking, measure the spatial span of the winning bin's voters (max pairwise distance). Accept only if the span exceeds a minimum (here 3): a line must be long.
The knowing-when rule
CERTIFIED iff (peak >= TAU) AND (voter_span >= SPAN_MIN): the shape needs votes AND extent. A tall tower of clumped pixels is a blob's edge, not a line.

JAH-KW-HOUGH-000107 — Circle-first voting — JAH's shape ordering

When a scene holds both a circle and a line, which election runs first? The circle's rim pixels also vote in the line election — any three of them are nearly collinear locally — so running lines first lets the line election poach the circle's pixels and fragment its rim. JAH's circle-first rule reverses the order: closed shapes vote before open ones, because closed shapes are rarer (fewer false alarms), their evidence is more distinctive (curvature is hard to fake), and their voters, once struck, cannot corrupt the later elections.

The ordering is strategic, not mathematical — both orders find both shapes in clean scenes. The difference appears in clutter, where the line election's appetite for pixels is indiscriminate and the circle's is precise. JAH's principle generalizes: hold elections in order of specificity, most distinctive shape first, and let each winner's struck voters purify the pool for the next. It is the second-peak rule (105) with a priority queue.

The demo builds a scene with a radius-2 circle at (4,4) and a line y=x. Circle election first: peak (4,4) with 4 rim votes — circle claimed, rim voters struck. Line election on the remainder: the line's own pixels still elect (135, 0) cleanly. Reverse the order in the demo's commentary and the line poaches two rim pixels first. Order matters; JAH orders by rarity. Signature-original.
