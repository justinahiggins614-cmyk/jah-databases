  1. What Business Valuation Is

    Record 1 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Business valuation is the process of working out what a business is worth in money, using its earnings, its revenue, its assets, and its cash flow. An example business earns $120,000 a year and sells for 3 times earnings, so its value is $360,000.

    Math proof: Value is earnings times a multiple: value equals earnings multiplied by the multiple. With earnings of $120,000 and a multiple of 3, value is 120,000 times 3, which is $360,000. The multiple is a plain number, so the multiplication is the whole proof: 120,000 times 3 equals 360,000.

    Science proof: Valuation works because buyers pay for future benefit, and a business that earns money every year is worth more than one that earns nothing. Earnings, revenue, assets, and cash flow are all measurable facts about the business, so a valuation built from them can be checked line by line. That is why valuation starts from the accounts and not from a guess.

  2. Discounted Cash Flow

    Record 2 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Discounted cash flow values a business by adding up the cash it will earn in future years, with each future year counted for less than a dollar today. An example business earns $100,000, then $110,000, then $121,000 over three years, and at a discount rate of 10 percent the three years are worth $272,727.27 today.

    Math proof: Each future cash flow is divided by one plus the discount rate, raised to the year number. Year 1: 100,000 divided by 1.10 is $90,909.09. Year 2: 110,000 divided by 1.21 is $90,909.09. Year 3: 121,000 divided by 1.331 is $90,909.09. Adding the three present values, 90,909.09 plus 90,909.09 plus 90,909.09, gives $272,727.27.

    Science proof: Discounting works because money today can be used today, while money next year cannot, so a future dollar is worth less than a present dollar. The discount rate measures that waiting cost plus the risk that the cash never arrives. That is why the same cash flow is worth more when the discount rate is low and less when it is high.

  3. Earnings Multiples

    Record 3 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. An earnings multiple values a business by multiplying its yearly earnings by a number that reflects how much buyers pay for each dollar of earnings. An example business earns $200,000 a year and the multiple is 4, so its value is $800,000.

    Math proof: Value equals earnings times the multiple. With earnings of $200,000 and a multiple of 4, value is 200,000 times 4, which is $800,000. The arithmetic is one multiplication, and changing either input changes the answer in the same proportion: double the earnings and the value doubles.

    Science proof: Multiples work because buyers compare businesses by what they earn, and similar businesses in the same trade tend to sell for similar multiples of earnings. A multiple is a market summary, not a law of nature, so it must come from comparable sales and be checked against other methods. That is why this record always pairs the multiple with the earnings figure it multiplies.

  4. Revenue Multiples

    Record 4 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. A revenue multiple values a business by multiplying its yearly revenue by a number, which is useful when earnings are small or uneven. An example business takes in $1,000,000 of revenue a year and the multiple is 1.5, so its value is $1,500,000.

    Math proof: Value equals revenue times the multiple. With revenue of $1,000,000 and a multiple of 1.5, value is 1,000,000 times 1.5, which is $1,500,000. Revenue is a larger number than earnings, so revenue multiples are usually smaller than earnings multiples for the same business.

    Science proof: Revenue multiples work because revenue is harder to adjust than earnings and is visible even when a business makes little profit. But revenue says nothing about costs, so a high-revenue business with high costs can be worth far less than its revenue multiple suggests. That is why a revenue multiple is always checked against an earnings method.

  5. Asset-Based Valuation

    Record 5 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Asset-based valuation values a business by what it owns minus what it owes. An example business owns assets worth $900,000 and owes $400,000, so its asset-based value is $500,000.

    Math proof: Value equals assets minus liabilities. With assets of $900,000 and liabilities of $400,000, value is 900,000 minus 400,000, which is $500,000. Every asset must be counted at its fair value and every liability subtracted, or the subtraction gives the wrong answer.

    Science proof: Asset-based valuation works because a business that owns valuable things and owes little is worth at least the net worth of those things, even if it earns little. It is the floor for many valuations: a buyer would rarely pay less than the net assets, since the assets could be sold. That is why this method matters most for businesses rich in property, equipment, or stock.

  6. Book Value

    Record 6 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Book value is the value of a business shown in its accounts: total assets minus total liabilities, as recorded on the books. An example business has $750,000 of assets and $250,000 of liabilities on its books, so its book value is $500,000, or $50.00 per share over 10,000 shares.

    Math proof: Book value equals assets minus liabilities. With assets of $750,000 and liabilities of $250,000, book value is 750,000 minus 250,000, which is $500,000. Book value per share is book value divided by the number of shares: 500,000 divided by 10,000 is $50.00 per share.

    Science proof: Book value works as an accounting anchor because it is built from recorded figures that can be audited, so two people reading the same books get the same number. But book figures can be old: land bought long ago may be worth far more than its book figure, and worn equipment far less. That is why book value is a starting point, not the final word.

  7. Liquidation Value

    Record 7 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Liquidation value is what a business is worth if its assets are sold off, its debts are paid, and the selling costs are taken out. An example business sells its assets for $600,000, pays $350,000 of debts, and pays $50,000 in selling costs, leaving a liquidation value of $200,000.

    Math proof: Liquidation value equals sale proceeds minus debts minus selling costs. With proceeds of $600,000, debts of $350,000, and selling costs of $50,000, value is 600,000 minus 350,000 minus 50,000, which is $200,000. Selling in a hurry usually brings lower prices, so liquidation value is normally the lowest of the valuation methods.

    Science proof: Liquidation value works because it answers the plain question of what is left when everything is sold and everyone is paid. It is the value that matters when a business closes, since a closed business earns nothing in the future. That is why it sets the bottom of the valuation range.

  8. Comparable Sales

    Record 8 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Comparable sales value a business by looking at what similar businesses actually sold for, and applying the same multiple. Three similar businesses sold for multiples of 3.0, 3.5, and 4.0 times earnings, an average of 3.5, and an example business earning $180,000 is therefore worth $630,000.

    Math proof: The average multiple is the sum of the three multiples divided by 3: 3.0 plus 3.5 plus 4.0 is 10.5, and 10.5 divided by 3 is 3.5. Value is earnings times the average multiple: 180,000 times 3.5 is $630,000. If the comparable businesses are not truly similar, the average multiple is misleading.

    Science proof: Comparable sales work because real sale prices are facts about what buyers actually paid, not opinions about what a business should be worth. The method fails only when the comparisons are poor: different size, different trade, or different growth. That is why good comparables must match the business in trade, size, and earnings pattern.

  9. Seller's Discretionary Earnings

    Record 9 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Seller's discretionary earnings, called SDE, is the profit left for one owner-operator after adding back the owner's salary and personal expenses. An example business has net income of $80,000, an owner salary of $60,000, and personal expenses of $10,000, so its SDE is $150,000, and at a multiple of 2.5 it is worth $375,000.

    Math proof: SDE equals net income plus owner salary plus personal expenses: 80,000 plus 60,000 plus 10,000 is $150,000. Value is SDE times the multiple: 150,000 times 2.5 is $375,000. The add-backs must be real owner benefits, not ordinary business costs, or the SDE is overstated.

    Science proof: SDE works because a small business buyer will be the owner-operator, so the salary the old owner paid themselves is really profit the buyer can keep. Adding it back shows the true cash the business throws off for its owner. That is why SDE is the standard measure for valuing small owner-operated businesses.

  10. Capitalization of Earnings

    Record 10 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Capitalization of earnings values a business by dividing its yearly earnings by a capitalization rate. An example business earns $150,000 a year and the capitalization rate is 20 percent, so its value is $750,000.

    Math proof: Value equals earnings divided by the capitalization rate. With earnings of $150,000 and a rate of 0.20, value is 150,000 divided by 0.20, which is $750,000. Dividing by a smaller rate gives a larger value, which is why the rate must reflect the real risk: a risky business needs a high rate and gets a low value.

    Science proof: Capitalization works because dividing by a rate is the same as asking what lump sum would earn the same yearly amount forever at that rate. A business earning $150,000 a year is like a fund that pays $150,000 a year, and at 20 percent that fund must hold $750,000. That is why the method suits steady businesses with stable earnings.

  11. Goodwill

    Record 11 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Goodwill is the part of a purchase price above the value of the net assets bought: it pays for reputation, customers, and the name. An example business is bought for $800,000 and its net assets are worth $500,000, so its goodwill is $300,000.

    Math proof: Goodwill equals purchase price minus net asset value. With a price of $800,000 and net assets of $500,000, goodwill is 800,000 minus 500,000, which is $300,000. If the price were below the net asset value, there would be no goodwill at all.

    Science proof: Goodwill works as a measure of what cannot be touched: loyal customers, a trusted name, skilled staff, and habits of trade that bring buyers back. Those things earn money, so they are worth paying for, but they cannot be sold separately from the business. That is why goodwill appears only when a business is bought as a going concern.

  12. Working Capital Adjustment

    Record 12 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Working capital is current assets minus current liabilities, and a sale price is adjusted when the working capital handed over differs from the agreed target. An example business has $300,000 of current assets and $180,000 of current liabilities, so its working capital is $120,000, which is $20,000 above a $100,000 target, so the price rises by $20,000.

    Math proof: Working capital equals current assets minus current liabilities: 300,000 minus 180,000 is $120,000. The adjustment is working capital minus the target: 120,000 minus 100,000 is $20,000, added to the price. If working capital were below target, the same amount would be subtracted instead.

    Science proof: The adjustment works because a buyer needs enough working capital to run the business from day one: stock to sell, cash to pay bills, and room to wait for customers to pay. Without the adjustment, a seller could drain the cash before handing the business over. That is why sale agreements fix a target and adjust for the difference.

  13. Discount Rate

    Record 13 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. The discount rate is the yearly rate used to shrink future cash to present value, built from a safe rate plus a risk premium. An example uses a risk-free rate of 3 percent plus a risk premium of 7 percent, giving a discount rate of 10 percent.

    Math proof: The discount rate is the risk-free rate plus the risk premium: 3 percent plus 7 percent is 10 percent. As a decimal, 0.03 plus 0.07 is 0.10. A higher risk premium means a higher discount rate and a lower present value for the same future cash.

    Science proof: The discount rate works because it prices two separate facts: waiting, which the risk-free rate measures, and the chance the cash never comes, which the premium measures. A safe government return sets the floor, and the extra risk of one business sets the premium above it. That is why riskier businesses are discounted harder.

  14. Growth Rate and Terminal Value

    Record 14 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Terminal value is the value of all cash flows beyond the forecast years, grown at a steady rate forever. An example business has a next-year cash flow of $110,000 growing at 3 percent a year, discounted at 10 percent, giving a terminal value of $1,618,571.43.

    Math proof: Terminal value equals cash flow times one plus the growth rate, divided by the discount rate minus the growth rate. The numerator is 110,000 times 1.03, which is $113,300. The denominator is 0.10 minus 0.03, which is 0.07. Dividing 113,300 by 0.07 gives $1,618,571.43. The growth rate must stay below the discount rate, or the division fails.

    Science proof: Terminal value works because most of a growing business's value lies in the years beyond any short forecast, and a steady growth rate is the simplest honest way to count them. Small changes in the growth rate move the answer a lot, so the rate must be modest and defensible. That is why terminal value is always shown with its growth and discount rates beside it.

  15. Cost of Capital

    Record 15 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. The cost of capital is the blended yearly cost of funding a business from equity and debt, weighted by how much of each is used. An example business is funded 60 percent by equity costing 12 percent and 40 percent by debt costing 6 percent before tax, or 4.5 percent after a 25 percent tax rate, giving a weighted cost of 9.0 percent.

    Math proof: Debt after tax is 6 percent times one minus 0.25, which is 4.5 percent. The weighted cost is 0.60 times 12 percent plus 0.40 times 4.5 percent, which is 7.2 percent plus 1.8 percent, giving 9.0 percent. The weights must add to 100 percent, or the blend is wrong.

    Science proof: The cost of capital works because every dollar funding a business has an owner who expects a return: shareholders expect more for taking more risk, and lenders expect less because they are paid first. Weighting each cost by its share of the funding gives the average price of the money the business uses. That is why it is the standard discount rate for valuing a whole business.

  16. Enterprise Value and Equity Value

    Record 16 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Enterprise value is the value of the whole business, while equity value is what is left for shareholders after debt is taken out and cash is added back. An example business has an enterprise value of $1,200,000, debt of $300,000, and cash of $100,000, so its equity value is $1,000,000.

    Math proof: Equity value equals enterprise value minus debt plus cash: 1,200,000 minus 300,000 plus 100,000 is $1,000,000. The order matters only in that debt is subtracted and cash is added; swapping them would give the wrong answer.

    Science proof: The two values differ because a buyer of the whole business takes on its debts and receives its cash, while a buyer of the shares buys only what is left after those debts. Enterprise value lets buyers compare businesses regardless of how they are funded. That is why sale prices are usually quoted as enterprise value first, then bridged to equity value.

  17. EBITDA Multiple

    Record 17 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. An EBITDA multiple values a business by multiplying its earnings before interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization by a number. An example business has EBITDA of $250,000 and the multiple is 5, so its value is $1,250,000.

    Math proof: Value equals EBITDA times the multiple. With EBITDA of $250,000 and a multiple of 5, value is 250,000 times 5, which is $1,250,000. EBITDA is larger than net income because interest, tax, depreciation, and amortization have not yet been taken out, so its multiples are usually smaller than net-income multiples.

    Science proof: EBITDA multiples work because EBITDA is close to the cash a business generates from its operations before funding and accounting choices, so businesses with different debts and tax positions can be compared on equal footing. The method still ignores real costs, since equipment wears out and must be replaced. That is why an EBITDA value is always checked against cash flow.

  18. Net Income and Profit Margin

    Record 18 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. Profit margin is net income divided by revenue, and it shows how much of each dollar taken in is kept as profit. An example business takes in $800,000 of revenue and keeps $120,000 of net income, so its profit margin is 15 percent.

    Math proof: Profit margin equals net income divided by revenue: 120,000 divided by 800,000 is 0.15, or 15 percent. A margin of 15 percent means 15 cents of profit for every dollar of revenue, and 85 cents of cost for the same dollar.

    Science proof: Margins work in valuation because two businesses with the same revenue can be worth very different amounts when one keeps far more of each dollar. A higher margin means the same revenue produces more earnings, and earnings are what buyers pay for. That is why a revenue multiple must always be read together with the margin.

  19. Cash Flow and Free Cash Flow

    Record 19 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. Free cash flow is the cash a business generates from operations minus the money it must spend on equipment to keep running. An example business generates $200,000 of operating cash and spends $50,000 on equipment, so its free cash flow is $150,000.

    Math proof: Free cash flow equals operating cash flow minus capital spending: 200,000 minus 50,000 is $150,000. The subtraction is the whole proof, but both inputs must be measured over the same year, or the result mixes different periods.

    Science proof: Free cash flow works because it is the cash that can actually be taken out of the business or used to pay its owners, after the business has paid to stay in operation. Profit on paper can hide a business that spends all its cash on new equipment, while free cash flow cannot. That is why discounted cash flow values free cash flow, not reported profit.

  20. Risk Adjustment and Marketability Discount

    Record 20 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. A discount for lack of marketability lowers the value of a business interest that cannot be sold quickly, because a buyer cannot turn it back into cash at will. An example interest is worth $500,000 before the discount, and a 20 percent discount for lack of marketability leaves it worth $400,000.

    Math proof: Discounted value equals value times one minus the discount: 500,000 times 0.80 is $400,000. The discount taken off is 500,000 times 0.20, which is $100,000, and 500,000 minus 100,000 is $400,000. The discount must be a fraction between zero and one, or the arithmetic gives nonsense.

    Science proof: The discount works because a share that can be sold in a day is worth more than the same share that may take a year to sell, even when the business behind both is identical. Waiting costs money, and a buyer who cannot exit quickly takes a real risk. That is why private business interests are valued below otherwise identical public ones.

  21. Control Premium and Minority Discount

    Record 21 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. A control premium raises the value of an interest that can control the business, because control lets the owner set policy and decide the future. An example minority interest is worth $400,000, and a control premium of 25 percent raises it to $500,000.

    Math proof: Value with a control premium equals the minority value times one plus the premium: 400,000 times 1.25 is $500,000. The premium added is 400,000 times 0.25, which is $100,000, and 400,000 plus 100,000 is $500,000. The same arithmetic in reverse gives a minority discount.

    Science proof: A control premium works because control is itself valuable: the controlling owner can appoint managers, set pay, declare dividends, and sell the business. A minority owner can do none of those things and must live with the controller's choices. That is why a controlling interest in the same business is worth more than a minority interest.

  22. Valuation Range and Reconciliation

    Record 22 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. Reconciliation combines several valuation methods into one range and one average, rather than trusting a single number. Three methods give $750,000, $800,000, and $650,000, so the valuation range is $650,000 to $800,000 and the average is $733,333.33.

    Math proof: The average is the sum of the three values divided by 3: 750,000 plus 800,000 plus 650,000 is 2,200,000, and 2,200,000 divided by 3 is $733,333.33. The range is the lowest value, $650,000, to the highest value, $800,000, a spread of $150,000.

    Science proof: Reconciliation works because every method rests on different assumptions, so agreement between methods is evidence and disagreement is a warning. A wide range means the inputs need checking before any price is trusted. That is why a serious valuation always shows its range and explains which method it trusts most and why.

  23. Valuing a Small Business: A Full Worked Example

    Record 23 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. A full worked example values one small example business by two methods and averages them. Its seller's discretionary earnings are $150,000, which at a multiple of 3 gives $450,000, while its asset-based value is $400,000, so the average of the two methods is $425,000.

    Math proof: The earnings method gives 150,000 times 3, which is $450,000. The asset method gives $400,000. The average is 450,000 plus 400,000, which is 850,000, divided by 2, which is $425,000. Both methods use the same example business, so the average compares like with like.

    Science proof: A full example works because it shows the whole path from the business's own figures to a final number, with every step visible and checkable. Averaging two methods guards against the weakness of either one alone: the earnings method can overstate a business with few assets, and the asset method can understate a business with strong earnings. That is why the example ends with an average and a range, not a single unexplained price.
