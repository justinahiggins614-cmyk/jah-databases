What a Haplogroup Is Rank 1 · simple

A haplogroup is a branch of a family tree of DNA lineages: mitochondrial haplogroups trace the maternal line, and Y-chromosome haplogroups trace the paternal line, because those two kinds of DNA pass down one line without the mixing that autosomal DNA undergoes. A haplogroup is defined by shared inherited variants, and its members share a common ancestor at the branch's root, thousands of years ago. Haplogroups are population history, not personal identity: knowing a haplogroup places a lineage in a broad ancient population, and it says nothing about any individual's family, appearance, or life. This pair reports haplogroups only as aggregate webs, never as individual results.

Mitochondrial Haplogroup Tree Rank 2 · simple

The mitochondrial haplogroup tree begins with the deepest African branches, then divides into the great branches M and N, which carried the out-of-Africa dispersals, and their many descendants across Eurasia, Oceania, and the Americas. Mitochondrial DNA passes from mother to child, so a mitochondrial haplogroup traces one line among thousands in any person's ancestry, and it is a mistake to treat it as the whole ancestry. The tree is built from published sequences, and its branches are named by letters, with sub-branches numbered and lettered beneath them. A tree read correctly shows deep time: the oldest branches are African, and the youngest are regional, formed as populations settled and grew.

Y-Chromosome Haplogroup Tree Rank 3 · simple

The Y-chromosome haplogroup tree begins with the deepest branches A and B in Africa, then divides into branches that spread across Eurasia, including C, D, E, F, and the great branch F's descendants G through T. Y-chromosome DNA passes from father to son, so a Y haplogroup traces one paternal line among thousands, and it too is a mistake to treat as whole ancestry. The tree's shape reflects ancient population structure: some branches are old and widespread, others are young and regional, formed when a growing population carried one lineage to dominance in a new area. Y and mitochondrial trees tell different stories because men and women moved differently in the past, and both stories are needed.

Haplogroup H in Europe Rank 4 · simple

Haplogroup H in Europe is the commonest mitochondrial haplogroup in much of Europe in aggregate frequency tables, frequent across the continent with regional variation. H expanded with farming and later populations, and its many sub-branches are regional: some are common in the west, others in the east, and their distributions map the movements of the populations that carried them. Frequency here is reported qualitatively, as common and widespread, because precise shares vary by study, sample, and region, and a single percentage would mislead. H is a European population pattern, not a marker of any individual, and its sub-branches are the interesting level for migration history.

Haplogroup U: Ancient European Lineages Rank 5 · simple

Haplogroup U is a group of ancient European mitochondrial lineages, including U5, which is common among Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, and U4 and U2, which appear in ancient steppe and eastern populations. U lineages predate farming in Europe, and their survival into later populations shows that hunter-gatherer ancestry persisted after farming arrived, rather than being replaced. Ancient genomes carry U lineages in Mesolithic burials across Europe, and modern aggregate frequencies show U at lower shares than H, with regional pockets where it remains frequent. U is therefore a continuity signal: it connects Mesolithic populations to later ones in aggregate terms, without naming any individual link.

Haplogroup L: African Diversity Rank 6 · simple

Haplogroup L is not one lineage but the deepest set of mitochondrial branches, all African, from which M and N, and therefore all non-African mitochondrial haplogroups, descend. L's diversity is the greatest of any region, because humans lived longest in Africa, and its branches include L0 through L6 and their many sub-branches, each with its own geography within Africa. Reporting L as a single frequency would hide that diversity, so this record reports it as a tree: deep, branching, and regionally structured. L is the root of the mitochondrial story, and every non-African haplogroup is a descendant of one L branch that left Africa.

Reading a Frequency Map Rank 13 · complex

Reading a frequency map means reading its date, its sample, and its scale before reading its colors: a map from one study year, with one sampling scheme, shows that study's aggregate, not a timeless truth. Frequencies vary sharply by region, so a haplogroup common in one valley may be rare in the next, and a map that averages a country hides that variation. Sample size matters: a map built from a few dozen samples per region is a sketch, while one built from thousands is a portrait, and the two must not be quoted alike. A frequency map is aggregate evidence, and it proves a population pattern, never an individual's ancestry.

Haplogroups and Migration: What They Can and Cannot Show Rank 14 · complex

Haplogroups and migration can show that a population moved, that two populations share a deep ancestor, and that a lineage expanded at a certain time, but they cannot show why people moved, what language they spoke, or who any individual ancestor was. A haplogroup is one line among thousands, so a person with a European haplogroup may have most of their ancestry from elsewhere, and a population's haplogroup profile is not a portrait of its whole ancestry. Migration claims from haplogroups need a second line of evidence, such as archaeology, dating, or autosomal ancestry in aggregate, before they are more than a suggestion. This record states those limits so that no web in this pair is read as a pedigree.

Phylogeny Updates and Renaming Rank 15 · complex

Phylogeny updates and renaming are a constant in haplogroup research: new sequences split old branches, old names change, and a haplogroup called one thing in an older paper may be called another in a newer one. The mitochondrial tree is maintained through published updates, and the Y-chromosome tree through the work of researchers who revise branch names as new variants are found. A reader comparing an old study with a new one must check whether the names match, or the comparison will be false. This record gives the rule: cite the tree version with the haplogroup name, and never quote a haplogroup without its sub-branch where the sub-branch is what carries the history.
