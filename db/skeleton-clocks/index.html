JAH-SKEL-000001 — What a Skeleton Clock Is: The Open Movement (simple, Principles)

Record

Specifications. A skeleton clock is a clock whose movement is deliberately left open to view: instead of a solid dial and a closed case hiding the works, the plates are pierced or reduced to an open framework, the wheels are exposed, and the escapement works in plain sight. A typical table skeleton clock stands 25 to 40 centimetres tall, often under a glass dome, with a chapter ring carried on slim supports rather than a full dial plate, so the eye passes straight through to the gear train.

Mechanism. The open framework changes nothing about how time is kept, and that is the point. A mainspring or a weight drives a going barrel, the barrel turns a centre wheel, and a train of wheels steps the motion down to the escape wheel. The escapement meters that motion out in small, regular steps, and the pendulum hanging below regulates the rate. Because the plates are open, a viewer can watch every stage of that chain at once: barrel turning slowly, centre wheel turning once an hour, escape wheel stepping tooth by tooth.

Maintenance. Keep a skeleton clock under its dome and out of direct sunlight, since the open movement collects dust far faster than a closed clock. Wind it on a regular schedule and never force the key past firm resistance. Dust the exposed wheels only with a soft brush and no liquids, and have the movement cleaned by a clockmaker every five to eight years, because dried oil on an exposed train shows up quickly as uneven running.

Build and restoration guidance. When restoring a skeleton clock, resist the temptation to polish the movement bright: the original finish, gilding or lacquer is part of the clock, and over-polishing erases it and can thin pierced plates. Clean gently, re-oil sparingly at the pivots only, and replace a broken glass dome with one that fits the base, so the open movement stays protected without being sealed away from view.

Tools. Winding key, soft brush, spirit level, glass dome, service logbook.

JAH-SKEL-000002 — Open Plates and Pillars: The See-Through Frame (simple, Frames)

Record

Specifications. The frame of a skeleton clock is its skeleton in the literal sense: two shaped plates, usually brass, held apart by turned pillars, with as much metal cut away as the design allows while keeping the pivots rigid. Some frames are architectural, with arches and scrolls; others are plain, with slim bars that barely interrupt the view. Plate spacing is typically 25 to 40 millimetres, enough for the wheels and the pendulum suspension to work freely between them.

Mechanism. The pillars carry the whole structure, so their length sets the depth of the movement and their rigidity sets its accuracy. Each wheel pivot runs in a hole in the front and back plates, and because the plates are open, a clockmaker can see at a glance whether a pivot is upright, whether a wheel is true, and whether any wheel rubs its neighbour. That visibility is why skeleton clocks are prized as teaching pieces as well as display pieces.

Maintenance. Check the pillars and plates for looseness once a year: a frame that flexes lets wheels go out of depth and causes stoppages. Never lift a skeleton clock by its open frame bars or by the pendulum; lift it by the base. Keep the clock level so the pendulum hangs centrally between the plates.

Build and restoration guidance. Rebuilding a loose frame means re-pinning or re-bushing pillars, never glueing them. If a pierced plate is cracked, a specialist repair preserves the piercing pattern; a replacement plate should copy the original outline exactly, since the frame is the clock's visible identity.

Tools. Screwdrivers, pivot polish, spirit level, soft brush, loupe.

JAH-SKEL-000003 — The Visible Gear Train: Centre, Third, Escape (simple, Mechanisms)

Record

Specifications. A skeleton clock's gear train is usually a going train of three or four wheels: the great wheel on the barrel, the centre wheel turning once an hour, a third wheel, and the escape wheel. Wheel teeth counts vary by maker, but the pattern is constant: each stage multiplies the speed, so the escape wheel turns many times faster than the barrel while delivering far less torque. Typical overall ratios from barrel to escape wheel run into the hundreds.

Mechanism. Watching the train teaches the whole clock at a glance. The barrel creeps, the centre wheel carries the minute hand, and the escape wheel flickers forward in small jumps as the pallets release it. If the train hesitates or a wheel wobbles, the fault is visible before it is audible, which is why open movements make diagnosis easier than closed ones.

Maintenance. Listen and look together: a steady tick with even wheel motion means a healthy train. Oil only the pivot points, one tiny drop each, and never oil the wheel teeth themselves, since oil on teeth attracts dust and changes the effective tooth shape. A train that runs unevenly usually needs cleaning, not more oil.

Build and restoration guidance. When rebuilding a train, check each wheel for bent teeth and each pinion for worn leaves before reassembly, and verify the depthing of every mesh by eye through the open plates. Replace a damaged wheel with one of matching tooth count and module, or the ratio and the rate will both change.

Tools. Loupe, oiler, pegwood, wheel puller, depthing tool.

JAH-SKEL-000004 — The Anchor Recoil Escapement in the Open (simple, Escapements)

Record

Specifications. The anchor recoil escapement is the classic escapement of the longcase and skeleton clock: an anchor-shaped pallet arm rocks above the escape wheel, and its two pallets alternately catch and release the wheel's teeth. The pendulum hangs from the anchor's arbor, so each swing of the pendulum rocks the anchor and lets one tooth escape. Recoil means the escape wheel is pushed slightly backwards at part of each swing, a motion plainly visible in an open movement.

Mechanism. The viewer sees the whole action: the pendulum swings, the anchor rocks, a pallet lifts, a tooth slips past, and the wheel jumps forward before the opposite pallet catches the next tooth. That jump is the tick. Because the pallets are exposed, the drop of each tooth and the lock of each pallet can be inspected without dismantling anything.

Maintenance. A recoil escapement needs the clock level and in beat, meaning the ticks sound evenly spaced. If the ticks are uneven, adjust the crutch or the pendulum suspension until they even out, and check that the anchor is not rubbing the plates. Oil the pallet faces very sparingly; excess oil here flings onto the escape wheel.

Build and restoration guidance. Restoring an anchor escapement means checking pallet faces for wear grooves and the escape wheel teeth for hooked tips. Polishing a worn pallet changes its geometry, so worn pallets are usually refaced by a specialist or replaced as a matched pair with the wheel.

Tools. Beat amplifier or ear, spirit level, oiler, loupe, crutch-bending tool.

JAH-SKEL-000005 — The Deadbeat Escapement, Visible at Work (simple, Escapements)

Record

Specifications. The deadbeat escapement, refined in the eighteenth century, improves on the recoil anchor by giving the escape wheel no backward motion: while a pallet is locked, the wheel stands still, dead, until the pendulum releases it. In a skeleton clock the difference is visible side by side with a recoil clock: the deadbeat wheel advances in clean steps with no backward shudder between them.

Mechanism. The deadbeat anchor's pallets have locking faces curved around the anchor's axis, so the wheel rests without recoil while the pendulum swings through its extra arc, and impulse faces that give the pendulum its small push. The open plates let a viewer watch the lock, the impulse and the drop as three distinct moments in each beat.

Maintenance. Deadbeat escapements are fussier about beat and about level than recoil ones, and they reward careful setting with steadier timekeeping. Keep the pendulum arc modest: a wide arc on a deadbeat wastes energy and can cause the pallets to misbehave. Oil the impulse faces only, in the smallest quantity that leaves a sheen.

Build and restoration guidance. When servicing a deadbeat, never stone the locking faces freehand; their concentric curve is what removes the recoil, and altering it turns the escapement back into a recoil one. Wear is judged by watching the drop through the open frame and measuring it, not by guessing.

Tools. Loupe, oiler, beat meter, spirit level, depthing tool.

JAH-SKEL-000006 — Fusee and Going Barrel: Power You Can Watch (compounded, Power)

Record

Specifications. Skeleton clocks are driven either by a going barrel, where the mainspring sits in a drum that turns as it unwinds, or by a fusee, a cone-shaped pulley with a chain that equalises the spring's falling pull. In an open movement both are on show: the barrel's slow rotation, or the chain winding up and down the fusee cone, is part of the display. Fusee clocks typically run eight days on a winding; going-barrel skeletons may run eight days or thirty hours depending on the train.

Mechanism. The fusee's trick is geometry: as the mainspring weakens, the chain pulls on a larger radius of the cone, so the torque delivered to the great wheel stays nearly constant. The viewer can watch the chain climb the cone over the week and see the equalisation happening. A going barrel is simpler and shows the spring drum itself turning, with the ratchet click holding it between windings.

Maintenance. Wind a fusee clock gently and evenly, and stop at firm resistance; a broken fusee chain is a specialist repair. Wind a going-barrel clock on the same day each week so the habit holds. Never let a mainspring run fully down with the pendulum swinging, since the train then coasts without power and can overbank.

Build and restoration guidance. Replacing a fusee chain means matching its length and link size exactly and re-seating it on the cone grooves; a chain one link too long or short changes the run time. Barrel work, including mainspring replacement, is done with a winder and clamps, never by hand against the spring's force.

Tools. Winding key, fusee-chain tool, mainspring winder, loupe, service logbook.

JAH-SKEL-000007 — Striking Skeleton Clocks: Bells in the Open (compounded, Striking)

Record

Specifications. Many skeleton clocks strike the hours on a bell or gongs, and the striking work is as exposed as the going train: the striking barrel, the count wheel or rack, the gathering pallet, and the hammer are all visible. A rack-and-snail strike counts the hour from a stepped snail cam, while a count-wheel strike steps through a fixed sequence of blows. Bells sit above or beside the movement, struck by a hammer the viewer can watch lift and fall.

Mechanism. When the hour comes, the going train releases the striking train, the rack drops onto the snail, and the gathering pallet collects one tooth per blow while the hammer strikes. In an open movement the whole conversation between the going and striking trains is visible: the warning, the release, the count, and the lock-up at the end of the strike.

Maintenance. Keep striking skeletons wound on both trains: a going train without a wound striking train will still release the strike and jam the warning. If the strike counts wrong, do not force the hands; let the strike finish, then check the rack and snail alignment through the open plates before correcting.

Build and restoration guidance. Striking repairs start with watching a full strike cycle through the frame and noting where it hesitates: a bent gathering pallet, a worn rack tooth, or a hammer spring that has lost its temper. Hammer leather or felt is replaced when it hardens, so the bell keeps its tone.

Tools. Screwdrivers, loupe, hammer-leather, oiler, strike-test logbook.

JAH-SKEL-000008 — Weight-Driven Skeleton Regulators (compounded, Regulators)

Record

Specifications. A weight-driven skeleton regulator hangs its power in the open too: one or two cylindrical weights on lines or gut, dropping slowly inside or beside the frame, driving the going train through the great wheel. Regulators of this kind are built for accuracy rather than decoration, with a long seconds pendulum and a deadbeat escapement, and the open frame lets the owner verify the drop, the beat and the rate at a glance.

Mechanism. The weight's steady pull gives the train constant torque, which is why regulators keep better time than spring clocks. The line winds onto the barrel drum as the weight falls, and the drum's rotation is geared down through the train to the escape wheel. A seconds pendulum, about 994 millimetres long, beats once per second and makes rating the clock against a reference straightforward.

Maintenance. Weight clocks must hang truly vertical and firmly fixed, since any lean changes the pendulum's arc and the line's run. Wind by raising the weights evenly, never letting a weight drop freely, and keep the lines clear of the frame and the pendulum rod. Record the drop weekly so a shortening run warns of increased friction before the clock stops.

Build and restoration guidance. Replacing a weight line means matching its diameter and material and re-seating it in the drum grooves without crossing turns. A regulator that loses rate usually needs cleaning and pivot polishing, not a heavier weight, and adding weight to force a dirty train only wears it faster.

Tools. Pulley tool, gut or line, spirit level, rating logbook, loupe.

JAH-SKEL-000009 — Spring-Driven Skeleton Timepieces (compounded, Power)

Record

Specifications. Spring-driven skeleton timepieces trade the weight's drop for a mainspring's coil, which makes them portable and dome-friendly: no wall fixing, no hanging weights, just a base, an open frame and a key. The spring lives in a going barrel or, in finer pieces, behind a fusee, and the train and escapement are the same pattern as in weight clocks, adjusted for the spring's varying torque.

Mechanism. The viewer watches the barrel turn a fraction of a revolution per day while the escape wheel steps steadily on. Because the spring's pull is strongest just after winding and weakest before the next winding, the rate of a spring skeleton varies slightly across the week, and the best pieces minimise that variation with a fusee or a well-matched train.

Maintenance. Wind spring skeletons on a fixed day and to the same point each time, so the rate pattern stays comparable week to week. Never overwind: the click will protest, and forcing it breaks the spring or the winding square. If the clock stops a day early, suspect dirt or a tired spring before suspecting the escapement.

Build and restoration guidance. Mainspring replacement is the most common major repair on spring skeletons and is done with a proper winder: the old spring is measured for height, thickness and length, and the replacement matched, since a stronger spring overdrives the escapement and a weaker one starves it.

Tools. Mainspring winder, winding key, barrel clamp, loupe, oiler.

JAH-SKEL-000010 — Glass, Dust and Display: Protecting an Open Movement (compounded, Care)

Record

Specifications. A skeleton clock's worst enemy is the air in the room: dust settles on every exposed wheel, pivot and pallet, and because the movement is open there is no case to slow it down. The standard defence is a glass dome on a wooden or marble base, which keeps dust off while keeping the movement visible, and many skeleton clocks were sold with the dome as part of the piece.

Mechanism. Display placement matters as much as the dome. Put the clock where it is seen but not handled, away from fireplaces, kitchens and open windows, since soot, grease and draughts all reach an open movement faster than a closed one. A level, vibration-free surface keeps the pendulum in beat, and steady room temperature keeps the rate steady.

Maintenance. Lift the dome by its base, never by the glass knob alone if the dome is loose, and dust the inside of the dome with a dry cloth before replacing it, so trapped dust does not fall onto the wheels. Clean the movement itself only with a soft brush; compressed air drives dust into the pivots.

Build and restoration guidance. When a dome is chipped or its base warped, replace or repair it promptly: an open movement left uncovered in a lived-in room can need cleaning within months. A replacement dome should match the original height and diameter so the clock's proportions survive.

Tools. Glass dome, soft brush, dry cloth, spirit level, display stand.

JAH-SKEL-000011 — Oiling an Open Movement: Less Is More (compounded, Maintenance)

Record

Specifications. Oiling a skeleton clock is visible work, which makes over-oiling visible too: excess oil creeps across open plates, collects dust, and turns into a grinding paste at the pivots. The rule is one tiny drop of clock oil at each pivot and at the escapement's impulse faces, and nowhere else: never on wheel teeth, never on pinion leaves, never on the pendulum suspension.

Mechanism. The open frame helps here, because every pivot can be reached and inspected without removing the movement from its case. A clockmaker works through the train from barrel to escape wheel, checking each pivot for side-shake and end-shake as the oil goes on, and wipes away any drop that spreads beyond its pivot.

Maintenance. Between professional services, owners should not add oil at all: topping up between cleanings mixes fresh oil with old, gummed oil and shortens the interval to the next full clean. If a skeleton clock runs unevenly, the answer is a clean, not a drop from a household oil can, which would ruin the movement.

Build and restoration guidance. A full service strips the movement, cleans each wheel and pivot in solution, pegs the pivot holes clean, re-bushes worn holes, and re-oils from dry. The open plates make bushing wear easy to spot: an oval hole or a pivot sitting off-centre shows plainly against the light.

Tools. Clock oil, fine oiler, pegwood, loupe, cleaning solution.

JAH-SKEL-000012 — Beat Setting on an Open Frame (complex, Regulation)

Record

Specifications. Beat setting is the adjustment that makes a clock's ticks evenly spaced, and on a skeleton clock the whole procedure is visible: the pendulum swings, the anchor rocks, and the ear judges whether the tick and the tock are equally spaced. A clock out of beat leans to one side of its swing, loses power, and may stop, even though the movement itself is sound.

Mechanism. The crutch, the arm that carries the impulse from the anchor to the pendulum, is bent slightly to shift the anchor's rest position until the beats even out. Because the frame is open, the clockmaker can watch the pallets' lock and drop on both sides while adjusting, and confirm that both sides release the same amount of tooth.

Maintenance. To set the beat at home, level the clock first, then listen: if the ticks gallop, the clock is out of beat. Small adjustments to the crutch, made gently and checked by ear after each touch, are enough; never bend the pendulum rod or the suspension spring to chase the beat.

Build and restoration guidance. A clock that will not hold its beat usually has a bent crutch, a loose pendulum suspension, or a frame that flexes. Each fault is visible through the open plates, and each is repaired before the beat is set again, since setting the beat on a loose movement only masks the fault.

Tools. Beat meter or ear, spirit level, crutch tool, loupe, rating logbook.

JAH-SKEL-000013 — Designing a Skeleton Dial: Chapter Ring Without a Face (complex, Design)

Record

Specifications. A skeleton clock has no dial plate in the usual sense: the hours are carried on a chapter ring, a slim annulus of metal, enamel or glass, held on slender supports in front of the movement so the works show through its centre. Roman or Arabic numerals mark the hours, with minute divisions on the ring's edge, and the hands sweep across open space rather than a painted face.

Mechanism. The chapter ring's supports are part of the design: four slim feet, a pair of scrolls, or a full subsidiary ring that echoes the frame's architecture. The hands must be long enough to reach the ring and light enough not to load the motion work, and they are often elaborately pierced or blued to stay legible against the moving wheels behind them.

Maintenance. Clean a chapter ring with a dry soft cloth only; enamel rings chip if knocked, and gilt rings lose their finish if polished. If the ring loosens on its supports, have it re-pinned promptly, since a ring that sags lets the hands catch on it and stop the clock.

Build and restoration guidance. Making a replacement chapter ring means matching the original's diameter, numeral style and foot positions exactly, because the ring is the clock's face and its most-read feature. Modern makers engrave or enamel the ring to the same pattern rather than substituting a solid dial, which would turn the skeleton clock into an ordinary one.

Tools. Soft cloth, loupe, hand puller, pinning tool, chapter-ring gauge.

JAH-SKEL-000014 — Complications in the Open: Calendar and Moon (complex, Complications)

Record

Specifications. Complicated skeleton clocks add calendar work, moonphase discs, or subsidiary seconds to the open movement, and every added wheel is visible: the date wheel stepping once a day, the moon disc creeping through its aperture, the extra train wheels carrying motion to the complication. The complication must be driven from the going train without stealing enough power to disturb the escapement, which is the maker's central problem.

Mechanism. A moonphase complication uses a disc with two moons that advances one tooth per day, completing its cycle in about 59 days, so each moon appears for roughly 29.5 days. A calendar complication steps a date ring daily and must handle months of different lengths, usually with the owner's hand to correct the short months. Watching these steps through the open frame shows the slow calendar of the clock against the fast ticking of the escapement.

Maintenance. Set complications in the morning, never near midnight when the calendar work is engaged, since forcing a date change then can bend the stepping levers. Advance a moonphase by its corrector only, and record the moon's age when setting it, so the display stays honest rather than decorative.

Build and restoration guidance. Repairing a complication starts with watching a full daily step through the frame and checking that each lever returns to rest. Worn date-jumper springs and bent moon-disc teeth are the common faults, and both are visible before the movement is opened.

Tools. Corrector tool, loupe, moon-age table, date logbook, oiler.

JAH-SKEL-000015 — Restoring a Skeleton Clock: Cleaning Without Erasing Patina (complex, Restoration)

Record

Specifications. Restoring a skeleton clock is a balance between clean and original: the movement must run well, but its patina, gilding and hand-finished surfaces are the evidence of its age and maker. A good restoration cleans the train, re-bushes worn pivots, repairs damage, and re-oils, while leaving original finishes, engraving and case wear intact wherever they do not affect running.

Mechanism. The open movement makes this judgement public: every cleaned surface and every replaced part is on view, so a restoration that over-polishes or substitutes modern wheels is visible to any knowledgeable eye. The restorer documents each decision, photographs the movement before and after, and keeps removed original parts with the clock rather than discarding them.

Maintenance. Owners commissioning a restoration should ask what will be cleaned, what will be replaced, and what will be left alone, and should expect the clock to come back running but not looking new. A skeleton clock that returns gleaming and scratch-free has usually lost more than it gained.

Build and restoration guidance. Structural repairs follow the same rule: a cracked pierced plate is conserved and stabilised, a missing pillar is remade to match its fellows, and a replaced wheel is marked as a replacement in the service record. Reversibility is the test: a future restorer should be able to undo today's work without damage.

Tools. Ultrasonic cleaner, pegwood, bushing tool, loupe, photo logbook.

JAH-SKEL-000016 — Collecting and Judging Skeleton Clocks: What the Open Movement Reveals (complex, Collecting)

Record

Specifications. For collectors, a skeleton clock's open movement is its own certificate: originality, condition and quality are all visible without opening a case. Matching wheel finishes, undisturbed screws, consistent gilding and honest wear all speak for an untouched movement, while replaced wheels, re-pierced plates and modern screws stand out against the original work.

Mechanism. Judging a skeleton clock means reading the movement like a document. Check that the escapement matches the period, that the frame's piercing is crisp rather than recut, that the chapter ring and hands belong to the piece, and that the strike, if present, counts correctly through a full cycle. Then check the running: a skeleton clock should tick evenly, strike cleanly, and hold its rate over a week.

Maintenance. Buy on condition and originality, not on shine: a brightly polished movement with replaced parts is worth less than a dulled but original one. Ask for the service history, check the dome and base belong to the clock, and run the clock for a week before judging its rate.

Build and restoration guidance. For the collector-restorer, the rule is minimal intervention: clean, repair what stops the clock, and preserve the rest. Every replaced part is recorded, and the clock's story, including its faults, stays with it, since the open movement will tell that story to the next owner anyway.

Tools. Loupe, rating logbook, strike-test notes, reference books, camera.
