Indo-European Language Family

ID: JAH-LANHS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a side-by-side branch ladder: the same ten everyday words traced through Germanic, Romance, Slavic and Indo-Iranian, so a learner can see the family tree in a single table.

Definition: A language family is a group of languages that all descend from one common ancestor language. The Indo-European family is the largest such family by number of speakers: about 445 living languages spoken by roughly 3.4 billion people, close to half of humanity. Its members include English, Spanish, French, German, Russian, Hindi, Persian, Greek and Latin. All of them descend from a single prehistoric language called Proto-Indo-European, which was spoken thousands of years ago and left no written records.

History: The family was recognised in the late eighteenth century. In 1786 the British scholar William Jones noted that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin shared striking similarities in words and grammar, and suggested they had "sprung from some common source". In the nineteenth century, scholars such as Franz Bopp, Jacob Grimm and August Schleicher turned that hint into a science: Grimm described regular sound changes, Bopp compared grammars, and Schleicher drew the first family tree. Where Proto-Indo-European was spoken is still debated. The most widely accepted view, the steppe hypothesis, places it on the Pontic-Caspian steppe, north of the Black and Caspian seas, roughly 4500 to 2500 BCE, and links its spread to horse domestication and wheeled vehicles. A rival view places the homeland in Anatolia, in present-day Turkey, several thousand years earlier, tied to the spread of farming. From the homeland the language spread in branches: Indo-Iranian eastward to India and Persia, Balto-Slavic and Germanic across Europe, Italic giving Latin, and others. Each branch split again and again into the languages of today. The twentieth century added new branches to the tree. Hittite, deciphered from clay tablets found in Turkey, proved to be an Indo-European language, the oldest attested branch, and Tocharian, found in manuscripts from western China, showed the family reached far into Central Asia. DNA studies in the twenty-first century have added a new line of evidence, tracing the movement of steppe peoples into Europe around 3000 BCE, which matches the linguistic evidence for the spread of the family.

Key concepts: A language family is a set of languages with a shared ancestor. Other key ideas include the sound law, a regular change that applies to every word meeting its conditions, and the distinction between inherited words and borrowings: English "beef" is borrowed from French, while "cow" is inherited from Proto-Indo-European, so the two words have different histories even though they refer to the same animal. The comparative method is the technique that ties it all together, comparing daughter languages to rebuild the parent. Finally, the family tree is a model, not a photograph: real languages also exchange words and features with neighbours, so the tree must be read alongside the history of contact. A proto-language is the reconstructed ancestor itself, never directly recorded. A cognate is a word inherited from that ancestor in two or more daughter languages, such as English "mother", German "Mutter" and Latin "mater". A sound correspondence is a regular pattern linking sounds between related languages, for example English "t" matching German "z" in words like "ten" and "zehn". A branch is one of the family's main divisions, such as Indo-Iranian or Germanic. The family tree model pictures languages splitting like branches, though real languages also borrow words from neighbours, which the tree alone does not show.

How it works: Linguists prove relatedness with the comparative method. First they collect words with the same meaning across languages and look for regular sound correspondences, not random resemblances. If English "father", Latin "pater", Greek "pater" and Sanskrit "pitar" line up sound by sound, chance is unlikely. Next they reconstruct the ancestor's sounds and words by working backwards through the regular changes. Grammar helps too: shared irregularities, such as the verb "to be" behaving oddly in many Indo-European languages, are strong evidence, because unrelated languages rarely share the same quirks. Once the correspondences are regular and the reconstructions consistent, the languages are placed on a family tree with dated splits. The method is slow and careful: a handful of look-alike words proves nothing, but hundreds of regular correspondences prove a great deal.

Worked examples: The word for "mother" shows the pattern clearly: English "mother", German "Mutter", Dutch "moeder", Latin "mater", Greek "meter", Russian "mat", Persian "madar" and Sanskrit "matar" all descend from one reconstructed Proto-Indo-European word, "meh2ter". Numbers work the same way: "three" in English, "tres" in Spanish, "trois" in French, "drei" in German, "tri" in Russian and "trayas" in Sanskrit. The verb "to be" is irregular in nearly every branch, which is itself evidence of shared inheritance. Even pronouns match: English "me", Latin "me", Greek "eme" and Sanskrit "mam". These are not borrowings, because the words are among the most basic and most resistant to replacement in any language.

Real applications: Knowing the family helps historians trace migrations and contacts long before writing existed. Archaeologists test the steppe hypothesis against evidence of horses, wheels and burial mounds. Language teachers use cognates as shortcuts: a Spanish speaker learning Italian already knows thousands of shared words. Dictionary makers and translators rely on family knowledge to explain why words look alike. In computing, language models group related languages to share training data, which improves translation for smaller languages such as Welsh or Nepali. Forensic linguists and historians of religion also use the family tree to date texts and trace how ideas travelled.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is English related to Hindi? Yes. Both are Indo-European, English through the Germanic branch and Hindi through the Indo-Iranian branch, so they share a distant ancestor. Is Indo-European the largest family? By speakers, yes, with about 3.4 billion; by number of languages, no — Niger-Congo has far more languages. Does the family include Chinese or Arabic? No. Chinese belongs to Sino-Tibetan and Arabic to Afro-Asiatic. A common misconception is that the family means the languages are mutually understandable; they are not. A Hindi speaker cannot understand German. The relationship is historical, like cousins sharing a great-great-grandparent, not a shared present-day language. Another misconception is that Sanskrit is the mother of all the languages; it is not. Sanskrit is a cousin, an early-recorded daughter, and Proto-Indo-European itself is reconstructed, not recorded.

Sino-Tibetan Language Family

ID: JAH-LANHS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a tone ladder: the same syllable carried through Mandarin's four tones, then Cantonese's six, with a simple pitch diagram described in words, so a beginner can hear the system before memorising it.

Definition: The Sino-Tibetan family is the second-largest language family by number of speakers, with about 500 languages spoken by roughly 1.4 billion people, mostly in China and across South and Southeast Asia. Its best-known member is Mandarin Chinese, the most spoken native language in the world, with about a billion speakers. The family divides into two great groups: the Sinitic languages, which include Mandarin, Cantonese and other varieties of Chinese, and the Tibeto-Burman languages, which include Tibetan, Burmese, Nepali and hundreds of smaller languages of the Himalayas and Southeast Asia.

History: The family's history begins in northern China. Most scholars place its homeland in the Yellow River valley or nearby regions of north-central China, perhaps 4,000 to 6,000 years ago, though the exact place and date are debated. From there, one line of descent became the Sinitic languages, tied to the rise of Chinese civilisation. The earliest Chinese writing, on oracle bones used for divination, dates to about 1250 BCE, during the Shang dynasty. Another line moved south and west into the mountains, becoming the Tibeto-Burman languages. Tibetan developed a written form in the seventh century CE, and Burmese writing appeared around the eleventh century. Over the centuries, Chinese expanded through empire, migration and schooling, so that Sinitic speakers came to dominate the family numerically. The study of the family's relationships is younger than Indo-European studies: serious comparison of Tibeto-Burman languages began in the nineteenth century, and the internal grouping of the family is still being refined today. In the twentieth century, the study of the family was transformed by fieldwork among its smaller languages. Linguists recorded languages of the Himalayas and Southeast Asia that had never been written down, and the reconstruction of Proto-Sino-Tibetan advanced, with scholars proposing shared words for body parts, animals and tools. The recognition of the family's age and diversity changed the picture of Asian history: the spread of Sino-Tibetan languages is now linked to the spread of farming, especially rice cultivation, from northern China southward, and the family's many small languages preserve evidence of migrations that written history never recorded.

Key concepts: Tone is a feature in which the pitch of a syllable changes its meaning, as in Mandarin, where "ma" can mean mother, hemp, horse or scold depending on pitch. Other concepts include the Sino-Tibetan homeland debate: whether the family began in northern China, as most scholars hold, or further south, a question still argued today. A dialect continuum is a chain of local varieties in which neighbours understand each other but distant speakers do not, a pattern common across China. A writing system is separate from the language it writes: Chinese characters can be used for different spoken varieties, which is why the same text can be read aloud in Mandarin or Cantonese. Finally, language classification is a hypothesis, not a fact: the grouping of Sino-Tibetan languages is still being revised as new evidence appears. An isolating language builds words mainly from single syllables with little inflection, so meaning depends heavily on word order and context. A logographic writing system uses characters that stand for words or morphemes rather than sounds, as Chinese characters do. The Sinitic branch is the Chinese side of the family. The Tibeto-Burman branch covers the non-Chinese languages, from Tibetan to Burmese. A language variety is a local form of speech; whether two Chinese varieties count as dialects or separate languages is a famous borderline case.

How it works: Tones work by adding pitch as a layer of meaning on top of consonants and vowels. Mandarin has four main tones — high and level, rising, falling then rising, and sharply falling — plus a neutral tone, and changing the tone changes the word entirely. Grammar works differently from English: verbs do not change form for tense or person, and nouns do not change for number. Instead, separate small words mark time, such as a particle after the verb meaning "already happened", and number is shown by context or by a counting word. Word order is usually subject, verb, object, the same as English. In the Tibeto-Burman languages the pattern varies more: some put the verb last, some use elaborate verb endings, and many are tonal too. Writing in Chinese uses characters, each generally standing for one syllable and one meaning, which lets speakers of different varieties read the same text even when they pronounce it differently.

Worked examples: Mandarin tones give the classic example: "ma" with a high level tone means mother, with a rising tone means hemp, with a dipping tone means horse, and with a falling tone means to scold. Cantonese has six or seven tones, so the same syllable can carry even more meanings. Burmese is also tonal and writes with its own script, derived from Indian writing systems. Tibetan, written with a script invented in the seventh century, preserves older sounds that Chinese has lost, which helps linguists reconstruct the family. Nepali, a Tibeto-Burman language written in the Devanagari script, is the official language of Nepal. In southern China, varieties such as Cantonese and Hokkien are so different from Mandarin in speech that linguists often treat them as separate languages, even though they share the written characters.

Real applications: Sino-Tibetan knowledge matters for education, since hundreds of millions of children learn to read and write in Chinese characters, one of the world's oldest writing systems still in daily use. For technology, Mandarin is one of the most important languages for speech recognition and machine translation, and tonal languages push engineers to model pitch carefully. Historians use the family's spread to trace migrations from northern China into the Himalayas and Southeast Asia. Conservationists and linguists document endangered Tibeto-Burman languages, many spoken by small communities, before they disappear. For learners, understanding tones and character structure is the key that turns Chinese from a wall of unfamiliar symbols into a learnable system.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is Chinese a single language? That is debated. The spoken varieties of Chinese, such as Mandarin, Cantonese and Wu, are often mutually unintelligible, so linguists frequently call them separate languages, while the shared writing system and cultural tradition lead many to call them dialects. Are Tibetan and Chinese related? Yes, both are Sino-Tibetan, though distantly; they split thousands of years ago. A common misconception is that Chinese characters are pictures of things; most characters are built from sound and meaning components, not drawings. Another is that tones are musical decoration; in fact a wrong tone can change a word's meaning entirely, so tones are as much a part of the word as its consonants. It is also wrong to think all Sino-Tibetan languages use Chinese characters; Tibetan, Burmese and Nepali each have their own scripts.

Afro-Asiatic Language Family

ID: JAH-LANHS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a root ladder: one Semitic root traced through Arabic, Hebrew and Amharic, from the bare three consonants up to a full sentence, showing the root-and-pattern system step by step.

Definition: The Afro-Asiatic family is one of the oldest recognised language families, with about 300 languages spoken by roughly 495 million people across North Africa, the Horn of Africa, the Arabian Peninsula and the Middle East. Its members include Arabic, Hebrew, Amharic, Hausa and the ancient languages Egyptian and Akkadian. The family has six branches: Semitic, Berber, Cushitic, Chadic, Omotic and Egyptian. Arabic is by far its largest language, with about 380 million speakers, while Hausa and Amharic are the largest in the Chadic and Semitic branches respectively outside Arabic.

History: The family's homeland is debated, with scholars arguing for the Horn of Africa, the Levant, or North Africa, and its age is estimated at more than 10,000 years, making it older than Indo-European. Its oldest written records are among the oldest writing anywhere: Egyptian hieroglyphs appear around 3200 BCE, and Akkadian, a Semitic language of Mesopotamia, was written in cuneiform from about 2500 BCE. Hebrew's oldest texts date to around 1000 BCE, and the classical Arabic of the Quran was fixed in the seventh century CE. Arabic spread far beyond Arabia with the expansion of Islam from the seventh century onward, becoming the language of religion, scholarship and administration from Spain to Central Asia. Meanwhile Berber languages persisted across North Africa, Cushitic languages such as Oromo and Somali spread across the Horn, and Hausa became the great trade language of West Africa. The family was recognised as a unit in the nineteenth century, when scholars saw that Semitic languages shared structure with Egyptian, Berber and Cushitic. The twentieth century brought new evidence for the family's history. The decipherment of ancient scripts and the discovery of new inscriptions pushed the written record further back, and the study of Cushitic and Omotic languages, long neglected, showed that the family is more diverse in Africa than was once thought. Genetic studies have linked the spread of Afro-Asiatic languages to the spread of farming and herding in North Africa and the Horn, though the exact route remains debated. The family's languages continue to grow: Arabic is now one of the fastest-growing languages on the internet, and Hausa is expanding as a language of media and trade across West Africa.

Key concepts: A triconsonantal root is a set of three consonants carrying a core meaning, the signature of Semitic languages. Other key ideas include the Afro-Asiatic homeland debate: scholars disagree about whether the family began in the Levant, North Africa or the Horn of Africa, and the evidence is still being weighed. A liturgical language is one used for worship after it has ceased to be spoken daily, as Ge'ez is for Ethiopian Christians and Classical Arabic is for Muslims. Language contact has shaped the family deeply: Berber languages borrowed from Arabic, Arabic borrowed from Persian and Turkish, and Swahili borrowed from Arabic, so the family's history is a web as well as a tree. Finally, a language's prestige matters: Arabic spread because it was the language of a great civilisation, not only because its speakers moved. A root-and-pattern system builds words by fitting vowels and affixes around that root, so one root can produce a whole family of related words. The Semitic branch includes Arabic, Hebrew, Aramaic, Amharic and the ancient Akkadian and Phoenician. The Cushitic branch includes Oromo, Somali and Afar. The Chadic branch includes Hausa. The Berber branch includes Tamazight, spoken across North Africa. The Egyptian branch is a single line, the language of the pharaohs, whose last stage, Coptic, survived as a church language.

How it works: Semitic word-building works like a skeleton and flesh. Take the Arabic root k-t-b, meaning writing. Slot the vowels a-a-a into it and you get "kataba", he wrote; change the pattern to "kitab" and you get book; "katib" is writer; "maktab" is office or desk. The three consonants stay in order while the vowels and affixes do the grammatical work. Other branches work differently: Hausa uses tone and word order, Berber uses consonant roots of its own, and Cushitic languages often mark gender and case with suffixes. What ties the family together is shared vocabulary and grammar from the common ancestor: words for body parts, kinship and basic actions, similar pronoun systems, and in several branches a feminine marker built from the sound "t". Linguists reconstruct these shared features to show the branches are related even though their speakers have lived far apart for thousands of years.

Worked examples: From the root k-t-b, Arabic makes "kataba" (he wrote), "kitab" (book), "kutub" (books), "katib" (writer) and "maktaba" (library). Hebrew uses the same root, spelled k-t-v, for "katav" (he wrote) and "ktav" (writing). The word for peace shows the kinship: Arabic "salaam" and Hebrew "shalom" come from the shared root s-l-m. Amharic, the official language of Ethiopia, writes with the Ge'ez script, an ancient system with roots over two thousand years old. Hausa, spoken by about 80 million people in Nigeria and Niger, became a trade language written first in Arabic script and now mainly in Latin letters. Ancient Egyptian's word for water, "mw", survives in Coptic, the last stage of Egyptian, still used in Coptic Christian worship.

Real applications: Afro-Asiatic knowledge underpins the study of the world's oldest civilisations: Egyptian hieroglyphs, Mesopotamian cuneiform and the Hebrew Bible all belong to this family. It matters for religion, since Arabic is the language of the Quran and Hebrew of the Jewish scriptures. In modern life, Arabic is an official language of the United Nations and the working language of more than twenty countries, while Amharic, Somali, Oromo and Hausa are languages of government and media for tens of millions. Archaeologists and geneticists compare the family's spread with evidence of farming and migration in Africa and the Near East. For language learners, the root system is a powerful shortcut: learning one root unlocks a dozen words at once.

Common questions and misconceptions: Are Arabic and Hebrew related? Yes, both are Semitic, so they share an ancestor and a root system; "salaam" and "shalom" are cousins, not borrowings. Is Ancient Egyptian related to Arabic? Yes, distantly: Egyptian is its own branch of Afro-Asiatic, while Arabic is Semitic, so they share a much older ancestor. A common misconception is that Afro-Asiatic means "African plus Asian languages in general"; it does not. The name describes where the family is spoken, and the family includes only its six branches, not every language of Africa or Asia. Another misconception is that all Semitic languages use the Arabic script; Hebrew uses its own alphabet, Amharic uses Ge'ez, and ancient Akkadian used cuneiform. It is also wrong to say Arabic has one uniform form: spoken dialects from Morocco to Iraq differ greatly, while Modern Standard Arabic is the shared written form.

Niger-Congo Language Family

ID: JAH-LANHS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a noun-class ladder: the same five objects named in Swahili, Zulu and Yoruba, singular and plural, so the class system can be compared across three languages at a glance.

Definition: Niger-Congo is the largest language family in the world by number of languages, with about 1,500 languages spoken by roughly 700 million people, mainly in sub-Saharan Africa. Its members include Swahili, Yoruba, Igbo, Zulu, Xhosa, Shona, Akan, Wolof and Lingala. The family takes its name from the Niger and Congo rivers, between which many of its languages are spoken. Its most widespread subgroup is Bantu, a cluster of several hundred closely related languages that spread across central, eastern and southern Africa over the last few thousand years.

History: The family is thought to have originated in West Africa, in the region of present-day Nigeria and Cameroon, perhaps 6,000 or more years ago. Its early history is reconstructed from language comparison rather than writing, because most Niger-Congo languages were unwritten until recent centuries. The great event in its history is the Bantu expansion, which began around 3000 BCE: farming communities speaking early Bantu languages moved south and east from the Nigeria-Cameroon borderlands, carrying iron-working and agriculture, and over two or three thousand years their languages spread across half the continent, replacing or absorbing many earlier languages. Meanwhile other branches stayed in West Africa and diversified into the dense patchwork of languages found in Nigeria today, the most linguistically diverse country in Africa, with more than 500 languages. Swahili grew later as a trade language of the East African coast, borrowing heavily from Arabic and becoming a lingua franca. The family was recognised in the nineteenth century and named by the linguist Joseph Greenberg in the twentieth; its internal grouping is still debated, especially the place of the Mande and Dogon languages. The twentieth century changed the study of the family. Joseph Greenberg's classification of African languages, published in 1963, gave Niger-Congo its modern shape, grouping hundreds of languages into one family on the basis of shared vocabulary and grammar. Field linguists then documented languages that had never been written, and the Bantu expansion was dated more precisely with the help of archaeology and, later, genetics. In the twenty-first century, the family's languages have grown in new directions: Swahili is now used in African Union meetings, Nigerian Pidgin is a language of film and music, and African languages are appearing on the internet and in mobile phone apps in numbers never seen before.

Key concepts: A noun class is a category of nouns marked by a prefix, the Niger-Congo equivalent of grammatical gender, but with many more classes, sometimes more than twenty. Other key ideas include the noun class agreement system, in which adjectives and verbs change to match the class of the noun, a feature that makes Niger-Congo grammar feel musical to outsiders. A language continuum is a chain of dialects shading into one another, common in West Africa, where the boundary between one language and the next can be hard to draw. Tone is not only a feature of words but of grammar: in many Niger-Congo languages, changing the pitch of a verb changes its tense. Finally, the Bantu languages form a subgroup within the family, not the whole family: the non-Bantu languages of West Africa, such as Yoruba, Igbo and Akan, are equally part of Niger-Congo, and some are older than the Bantu expansion itself. The Bantu expansion is the long migration of Bantu-speaking farmers across central, eastern and southern Africa. A lingua franca is a language used for communication between people whose first languages differ, such as Swahili in East Africa. Tone is widespread in the family: pitch can distinguish words, as in Yoruba and Igbo. An agglutinative language builds words by stringing meaningful pieces together, each piece adding one bit of meaning.

How it works: Noun classes are the family's signature system. In Swahili, the prefix ki- marks singular nouns of one class and vi- marks their plurals: "kitabu" is a book and "vitabu" are books, while "mtu" is a person and "watu" are people, using a different class pair. Adjectives and verbs agree with the noun's class, so the class echoes through the whole sentence, like a chord. Verbs are built by adding pieces: a root for the action, markers for tense, and endings for who is acting, so one long word can say what English needs a whole sentence for. Tone adds another layer: in Yoruba, the same syllables with different pitches mean different things, so pitch is part of the word, not decoration. Because so many languages share this machinery, a speaker of one Bantu language can often recognise the structure of another, even without understanding the words.

Worked examples: Swahili shows noun classes in action: "mtoto" is a child, "watoto" are children, "mti" is a tree, "miti" are trees, and "kiti" is a chair, "viti" are chairs. Yoruba tone gives a famous example: the syllable "ogun" can mean war, medicine, inheritance or a god's name depending on pitch. Zulu uses click consonants borrowed from the Khoisan languages of southern Africa, so a Zulu word can begin with a sound made by clicking the tongue. Igbo, spoken in southeastern Nigeria, has a rich system of verb tones that change meaning with context. Lingala, spoken in the Congo, grew as a trade language along the Congo River and is now a language of music, with Congolese rumba sung in Lingala across Africa.

Real applications: Niger-Congo languages are the working languages of hundreds of millions of people, used in government, radio, schooling and music across sub-Saharan Africa. Swahili is an official language of the African Union and is taught as a foreign language worldwide. Understanding noun classes and tone is essential for building speech technology, dictionaries and school materials for these languages. Historians use the Bantu expansion to trace the spread of farming and iron across Africa, complementing archaeology. History: The Austronesian peoples began in Taiwan, where the greatest diversity of Austronesian languages is still found today, a sign that the family started there. Around 3000 BCE, or perhaps earlier, seafaring farmers began moving south from Taiwan into the Philippines, carrying rice, pigs, dogs and the outrigger canoe. The family also shares vocabulary from its seafaring past: words for eye, water, fish, canoe and star are recognisably similar from Madagascar to Hawaii. Definition: The Romance languages are the modern descendants of Latin: French, Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Romanian, Catalan and several smaller languages, spoken by more than 800 million people across Europe, the Americas and Africa. They all developed from the everyday Latin spoken by soldiers, settlers and traders of the Roman Empire, known as Vulgar Latin, rather than from the formal Latin of Cicero's speeches. Over centuries, the Latin of each region changed in its own direction, until speakers of the different regions could no longer understand one another, and new languages had been born.

History: Latin began as the language of a small city-state, Rome, founded traditionally in 753 BCE. As Rome conquered Italy and then a vast empire, Latin spread with its armies and administrators. By the first century CE it was spoken from Britain to North Africa to the Danube. After the western Roman Empire fell in the fifth century CE, central authority vanished, and local forms of Latin drifted apart. Reconstructing Proto-Indo-European

Definition: Proto-Indo-European is the reconstructed ancestor of the Indo-European language family, the language from which English, Hindi, Greek, Latin, Russian and Persian all descend. Nobody ever wrote it down, and no recording of it exists. Instead, linguists rebuilt it piece by piece in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by comparing its daughter languages and working backwards through regular sound changes. The reconstruction is a scientific hypothesis: a set of sounds, words and grammar rules that best explains the evidence, marked with an asterisk to show it is reconstructed, not attested. It is our only window onto a language spoken some six thousand years ago.

History: The reconstruction began with William Jones's observation in 1786 that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin were too similar to be coincidence. In the early nineteenth century, Franz Bopp compared the grammar of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian and Germanic and showed the resemblances were systematic. Jacob Grimm, in 1822, described the regular sound shifts that separate Germanic from the other branches, the first great sound law. August Schleicher then drew the first family tree and began reconstructing the parent language itself, coining the term Indo-European. Later scholars refined the sound system: the discovery of Hittite in the twentieth century, deciphered from clay tablets in Anatolia, added a whole branch and forced revisions, including the laryngeal theory, which proposed sounds that had vanished everywhere except in their effects on neighbouring vowels. By the late twentieth century, the reconstruction was detailed enough to describe the speakers' world: they had words for horses, wheels, wool, snow and the sky father, which archaeologists matched to the Pontic-Caspian steppe around 4500 to 2500 BCE. The homeland and date remain debated, but the reconstruction itself is one of the great achievements of historical science. In the twentieth century, the reconstruction was tested against new evidence. The decipherment of Hittite in 1915 confirmed the laryngeal theory: Hittite tablets showed the lost consonants in writing, a prediction made decades before the evidence appeared. The discovery of Tocharian manuscripts in western China added a branch that had been entirely unknown, and Mycenaean Greek tablets, deciphered in 1952, pushed written Greek back to 1450 BCE. In the twenty-first century, ancient DNA has supported the steppe hypothesis, showing a large migration from the steppe into Europe around 3000 BCE, though the Anatolian hypothesis still has defenders, and the debate continues.

Key concepts: A reconstructed form is a word or sound marked with an asterisk, meaning it is inferred, not recorded. Other key ideas include the asterisk convention: every reconstructed form is written with a star to show it is inferred, not recorded, a mark of honesty that distinguishes science from speculation. The regularity hypothesis states that sound change applies without exception to all words meeting its conditions, and apparent exceptions must be explained by borrowing or analogy. A shared innovation is a change found in several languages but not in others, used to group them into branches: the satem languages, for example, share a change in the pronunciation of certain consonants. Finally, reconstruction is limited: words for things the speakers did not know, such as the sea, cannot be reconstructed, and the absence of a word is itself evidence about their world. A sound law is a regular, exceptionless change in pronunciation, such as Grimm's law. The laryngeal theory proposes three lost consonants, written h1, h2 and h3, whose traces survive as vowel colours in daughter languages. A cognate set is a group of related words across languages used as evidence for a reconstruction. Internal reconstruction rebuilds earlier forms from irregularities inside one language alone. The comparative method is the overall technique of comparing daughter languages to recover the parent.

How it works: Reconstruction starts with cognate sets: words with the same meaning in several daughter languages. The linguist aligns their sounds and asks what single ancestor sound could produce all of them through known, regular changes. For example, English "foot", Latin "ped-", Greek "pod-" and Sanskrit "pad-" all point to a reconstructed "pod-", with the initial sound shifting to "f" in Germanic by Grimm's law. The process repeats for hundreds of words, and the reconstructed sounds must be consistent across all of them: a proposed ancestor sound cannot explain one word and contradict another. Grammar is reconstructed the same way, from shared endings and irregular verbs. Where the daughters disagree, the linguist weighs which form is most likely original, using clues such as Hittite evidence or the principle that irregular forms are usually older. The result is a system, not a single word: sounds, words, and rules that fit together.

Worked examples: The reconstructed word for father is "ph2ter": English "father", Latin "pater", Greek "pater" and Sanskrit "pitar" all follow from it by regular changes. The word for mother is "meh2ter", giving English "mother" and Latin "mater". The number three reconstructs as "treyes", visible in English "three", Latin "tres" and Sanskrit "trayas". The laryngeal theory explains a puzzle: Greek "pater" has a long "a" where Sanskrit "pitar" has a short "i", and the lost sound h2 accounts for the difference. Hittite supplied the proof: its word for water, "watar", matches English "water" and shows the family reached Anatolia too. The reconstructed vocabulary even tells us about the speakers' lives: they had words for horse, wheel, axle and wool, but apparently none for the sea, which suggests an inland homeland.

Real applications: Reconstruction gives historians a language for a people who left no writing, and archaeologists use it to test theories about the steppe homeland and the spread of horses and wheels. It provides the model for reconstructing other families, from Sino-Tibetan to Austronesian. It matters for understanding English itself: many of its strangest features, such as the irregular verb "to be" or the "f" in "foot" where Latin has "p", are explained by descent from Proto-Indo-European. In education, it shows how science works on evidence that cannot be observed directly, by building the hypothesis that best fits the data. In computing, the same comparative logic now helps trace the history of manuscript traditions and even computer viruses.

Common questions and misconceptions: Can we know exactly how Proto-Indo-European sounded? No. The reconstruction is a best-supported hypothesis, and details such as the exact pronunciation of laryngeals are still debated; the asterisk marks this uncertainty honestly. Was it one uniform language? Probably not: like any real language, it had dialects, and the reconstruction is a kind of average. A common misconception is that reconstruction is guesswork; it is not. Every reconstructed form must follow regular sound laws across hundreds of words, and a wrong guess is quickly exposed. Another misconception is that Proto-Indo-European was a primitive tongue; its grammar was rich and complex, with three genders, eight cases and elaborate verb forms. It is also wrong to say Sanskrit or Latin is Proto-Indo-European itself; both are daughters, and the parent is older than either.

How Languages Change

Definition: Languages are never still. Every living language changes over time, in sounds, words, grammar and meaning, and the study of that change is called language change or historical linguistics. Change is not decay and it is not progress: it is the normal result of people learning, speaking and adapting their language across generations. Old English of a thousand years ago is unintelligible to a modern English speaker, yet each generation in between understood the one before it. The same slow drift has turned Latin into French, Spanish and Italian, and is turning the English of today into the English of the future.

History: The study of change began in earnest in the nineteenth century, when scholars such as Jacob Grimm and the Neogrammarians showed that sound change follows regular rules. Before that, change was often seen as corruption: Latin was pure, its descendants were decayed. The nineteenth century reversed that view, treating all stages of a language as equally systematic. In the twentieth century, the sociolinguist William Labov showed that change spreads through social groups: new pronunciations often start with one group, such as young women in a city, and spread outward. The study of English gives a clear timeline. Old English, spoken from about 450 CE, had cases and grammatical gender. After the Norman Conquest of 1066, French words flooded in and the case system collapsed. Between about 1400 and 1700, the Great Vowel Shift raised the long vowels. In the eighteenth century, dictionaries and schools began to standardise spelling and grammar, slowing some changes while others, such as the spread of "you" for singular and plural, continued regardless. Change has never stopped: new words, new pronunciations and new constructions appear every year. The twentieth century added a social dimension to the study of change. William Labov, working in New York and on the island of Martha's Vineyard, showed that sound change spreads through social networks and that young people, especially young women in cities, often lead it. The study of language in use, called sociolinguistics, showed that change is not only a matter of sounds and grammar but of identity: people adopt new forms to sound like the group they belong to, or to distance themselves from a group they do not. In the twenty-first century, the internet has become a new engine of change, spreading slang and new words around the world in days, and creating new forms of writing that mix speech and text.

Key concepts: Sound change is a shift in pronunciation that spreads through a language's words. Other key ideas include the wave model, which pictures change spreading outward from a centre like ripples in a pond, in contrast to the family tree model, which pictures languages splitting apart. A standard language is the form promoted by schools and dictionaries, which changes more slowly than everyday speech. A register is a style of language suited to a situation, such as formal or informal speech, and change often begins in one register before spreading to others. Finally, language change is value-neutral: a new form is not better or worse than the old one, though speakers often feel strongly about it, and the study of change separates the facts of how language works from the opinions people hold about it. Analogy is the remodelling of an irregular form to match a regular pattern, as when children say "goed" instead of "went". Borrowing is taking words from another language, such as "sushi" from Japanese. Semantic change is a shift in a word's meaning, as when "nice" moved from meaning foolish to meaning pleasant. Grammaticalisation is the process by which an ordinary word becomes a grammatical marker, such as "going to" becoming a future marker. Language contact is the meeting of two languages, which often speeds change in both.

How it works: Change happens through small steps repeated millions of times. A speaker pronounces a sound slightly differently, listeners copy the new pronunciation, and over generations the difference becomes the standard. Sound change usually follows regular rules: in the Great Vowel Shift, every long vowel moved up one step, so "house" went from sounding like "hoose" to its modern sound. Analogy tidies up irregularities: English speakers turned the old plural "kine" into "cows" to match the regular pattern. Borrowing fills gaps: English took "algebra" from Arabic, "piano" from Italian and "kindergarten" from German. Meaning shifts by association: "broadcast" once meant scattering seed, then radio transmission, and now any wide distribution. Social forces steer all of this: prestigious forms spread, stigmatised forms retreat, and contact between communities mixes their vocabularies.

Worked examples: English "foot" and "feet" preserve an ancient vowel change that once applied to many words but now survives only in a few pairs, such as "man" and "men" and "goose" and "geese". The word "knight" was once pronounced with its "k" and its "gh", roughly "k-neecht", and the spelling still shows sounds that speech has lost. "Nice" meant foolish in the fourteenth century, then precise, then pleasant, a long chain of semantic change. "Going to" shows grammaticalisation: it began as a phrase about movement, as in "I am going to London", and became a future marker, as in "I am going to eat". The Comparative Method

ID: JAH-LANHS-000024 · Ladder: complex · Status: established · Kind: subject explained in full record

Definition: The comparative method is the central technique of historical linguistics: the method by which linguists prove that languages are related, reconstruct their common ancestor, and trace how they changed. It works by comparing words and grammar across languages, looking for regular sound correspondences, and working backwards to the parent language. It is the method that established the Indo-European family, reconstructed Proto-Indo-European, and proved the relationships of hundreds of other language families. It is a scientific method in the full sense: it makes predictions, it can be checked, and it can be wrong.

History: The method was born in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. William Jones's observation in 1786 that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin were too similar to be coincidence gave it a starting point. The Danish scholar Rasmus Rask, in 1818, compared the Germanic languages with Greek and Latin and showed the correspondences were systematic. Jacob Grimm, in 1822, described the regular sound shifts of Germanic, the first sound law. Franz Bopp, in 1816, compared the grammar of Sanskrit, Greek, Latin, Persian and Germanic, showing that the resemblances extended to verb endings and pronouns. The Neogrammarians of the 1870s, led by Karl Brugmann and Hermann Osthoff, declared that sound laws were exceptionless, and that apparent exceptions must be explained by other changes, such as analogy or borrowing. In the twentieth century, the method was applied to families around the world: to Sino-Tibetan, Austronesian, Niger-Congo and Afro-Asiatic, and it was refined by the discovery of Hittite, which confirmed predictions the method had made. Today the method is also tested by computer, with large databases of words compared by statistical methods, and it remains the standard by which claims of language relationship are judged. In the twentieth century, the method was extended and tested. The discovery of Hittite confirmed its predictions, and the method was applied to families around the world, from Sino-Tibetan to Austronesian to Niger-Congo. The Neogrammarian claim that sound laws are exceptionless was challenged and refined: apparent exceptions were explained by analogy, borrowing and other changes, and the method became more careful about distinguishing inherited words from borrowed ones. In the twenty-first century, computers have transformed the method: large databases of words are compared by statistical methods, and the results are checked against the traditional method, which remains the standard. The method has also been used to date language families, by combining linguistic evidence with archaeology and genetics, and it continues to be the test by which claims of language relationship are judged.

Key concepts: A sound correspondence is a regular pattern linking sounds between related languages, such as English "t" matching Latin "t" and German "z". Other key ideas include the concept of a family tree: a diagram that shows how languages split from a common ancestor, with branches for each subgroup. A subgroup is a set of languages that share innovations, such as the Germanic languages, which share Grimm's law. A shared retention is a feature inherited from the ancestor and kept by several languages, which is weaker evidence than a shared innovation, because it could be a coincidence. Finally, the method has limits: it can reconstruct a language only as far back as the evidence allows, and beyond a certain point, around eight or ten thousand years, the evidence becomes too thin, which is why the question of whether all languages descend from one ancestor remains open. A sound law is a regular change that applies to all words meeting its conditions. A cognate is a word inherited from a common ancestor. Reconstruction is the recovery of the ancestor's sounds and words from its daughters. A proto-language is the reconstructed ancestor. Analogy is the remodelling of a form to match a pattern, which can create apparent exceptions to sound laws. A borrowing is a word taken from another language, which must be excluded from the comparison.

How it works: The method works in four steps. First, the linguist gathers words with the same meaning in the languages being compared, and identifies likely cognates, words that look similar and mean the same thing. Second, the linguist aligns the sounds and looks for regular correspondences: if English "t" matches German "z" in "ten" and "zehn", in "two" and "zwei", and in "tongue" and "Zunge", the pattern is real, and a single coincidence is not enough. Third, the linguist reconstructs the ancestor sound that could have produced all the attested sounds by regular changes, choosing the simplest explanation that fits all the evidence. Fourth, the reconstruction is tested: it must work for hundreds of words, it must be consistent with the grammar, and it must predict what new evidence, such as a newly discovered language, will show. Borrowings are filtered out, because they follow the rules of the lending language, not the borrowing one, and chance resemblances are rejected when they do not form regular patterns.

Worked examples: The correspondence of English "t" to German "z" is the textbook example: "ten" and "zehn", "two" and "zwei", "tongue" and "Zunge", "heart" and "Herz". The correspondence of English "f" to Latin "p" is another: "father" and "pater", "foot" and "ped-", "fish" and "piscis". The reconstruction of the word for mother, "meh2ter", explains English "mother", Latin "mater" and Sanskrit "matar" through the same sound laws. The method predicted the existence of the laryngeal sounds before Hittite was deciphered: the theory said that certain vowel differences could only be explained by lost consonants, and Hittite tablets, read in the twentieth century, showed those consonants in writing. A famous failure shows the method's honesty: the proposed link between Japanese and Korean, or between Indo-European and other families, has not been proved, because the correspondences are not regular enough.

Real applications: The comparative method matters because it is the only reliable way to prove that languages are related and to recover the history of peoples who left no writing. It gives historians a tool for tracing migrations, archaeologists a check on their theories, and anthropologists a way to study the spread of ideas. It matters for understanding the languages we speak: English spelling, the irregular verbs and the strange words all make sense once their history is reconstructed. For the wider sciences, the method is a model of how to infer an unseen past from present evidence, and its logic has been adapted to the study of manuscript traditions, folktales and even the evolution of computer code. For language documentation, it provides the framework for grouping the world's languages into families, the first step in describing them.

Common questions and misconceptions: Does the comparative method prove that all languages are related? No, it proves specific relationships where the evidence is regular and extensive, and it has not proved a single origin for all languages; that question remains open. Can the method reconstruct a language exactly? No, the reconstruction is a hypothesis, marked with an asterisk, and details remain debated, especially sounds that left no trace. A common misconception is that the method is just spotting similar words; it is not. Similar words can arise by borrowing or by chance, and only regular correspondences across hundreds of words count as proof. Another is that the method works on any two languages; it works only where the languages really are related, and applying it to unrelated languages produces nothing. It is also wrong to think the method is outdated: it remains the standard, and computers have made it more powerful, not less.
