What Journalism Is

Journalism is the gathering, checking, and publishing of news: the reporting of what has happened, to the people who need to know. The journalist finds the facts, checks them, and tells the story fairly, so the reader, the listener, and the viewer can understand the world and decide for themselves. Journalism is the work of the newspaper, the radio, the television, and the internet, and it is the work that holds power to account.

A newspaper has about 40 pages, and about 60 percent of them are advertising, so about 24 pages are advertisements, and about 16 are editorial. A reporter files about 3 stories a day, and 15 in a 5-day week. A 30-minute television bulletin has about 10 stories, so each story gets about 3 minutes. A radio bulletin of 5 minutes has about 5 stories, so each gets about 1 minute. The news is published every day, and the journalist works to the deadline: the story must be ready, checked, and true, on time.

A History of Journalism

The history of journalism is the history of the news: from the handwritten newsletter to the printed newspaper, from the telegraph to the television, from the radio to the internet. It is 400 years long, and it is the story of how the news became fast, then instant, then constant. The history has five ages: the printed, the political, the telegraphic, the broadcast, and the digital, and each age changed what the journalist could do.

From the first newspaper in 1605 to the telegraph in the 1840s is about 240 years, and from the telegraph to the radio in the 1920s is about 80 years. From the radio to the television in the 1950s is about 30 years, and from the television to the internet in the 1990s is about 40 years. From the first newspaper in 1605 to 2026 is 421 years. The ages are getting shorter: the printed age lasted 240 years, and the digital age is 30 years old, and it is still changing. Journalism is 400 years old, and the modern, digital journalism is less than 40 years old.

News Values

News values are the judgements that decide what is news: the timeliness, the impact, the proximity, the prominence, the conflict, and the human interest. A story with strong news values is published, and a story without them is not. News values are the gate of journalism: they decide what the audience hears, and what they do not. They are the most important judgement the journalist makes, and they are made every day.

A news meeting chooses about 10 stories from 100 possible stories: 1 in 10 is chosen, and 9 in 10 are not. A story with 5 strong news values is chosen, and a story with 1 is not. The 12 factors of Galtung and Ruge are the academic list, and the 6 values of the newsroom are the working list: the timeliness, the impact, the proximity, the prominence, the conflict, and the human interest. A local story has proximity but little impact, and a national story has impact but little proximity: the news values are weighed, and the balance decides.

Reporting

Reporting is the gathering of the news: the going to the place, the talking to the people, the reading of the documents, and the observing of the event. The reporter is the journalist who goes and finds: they ask, they listen, they watch, and they write. Reporting is the foundation of journalism: without the reporting, there is no news, only opinion. It is the work of the feet, the ears, and the eyes.

A reporter files about 3 stories a day, and 15 in a 5-day week. A story has about 500 words, so 3 stories are 1,500 words a day, and 7,500 words a week. A reporter on a beat covers 1 area, and they know it: the court reporter knows the court, and the parliamentary reporter knows the parliament. An interview takes about 30 minutes, and the notes are 2 pages, and the story is 500 words: the 30 minutes become the 500 words. The deadline is the rule: the story must be ready, checked, and true, on time.

Interviewing

Interviewing is the asking of questions to get the news: the reporter asks, and the person answers, and the answers become the story. The interview is the main tool of the reporter: most news comes from people, not from documents, and the interview is how the reporter gets it. Interviewing is a skill: the preparation, the questions, the listening, and the quoting, and the best interviewers listen more than they talk.

An interview takes about 30 minutes, and the notes are 2 pages, and the story is 500 words. A reporter does about 2 interviews a day, and 10 in a 5-day week. A press conference lasts about 30 minutes, and 20 reporters ask questions, so each reporter gets about 1 or 2 questions. The open question invites the story: 'What happened?' gets the story, and the closed question confirms it: 'Was it at 10?' gets the fact. The quote must be accurate: the words are reported exactly, and the meaning is kept, and the source is named, unless it is off the record.

Investigative Journalism

Investigative journalism is the journalism that digs: the reporter investigates what is hidden, follows the money, reads the documents, and reveals what power does not want known. It is the longest, hardest journalism, and the most important: it exposes the corruption, the abuse, and the wrong, and it changes the law and the government. Investigative journalism is the watchdog at its sharpest.

Watergate took 2 years: the break-in was in 1972, and Nixon resigned in 1974. The Panama Papers involved 400 journalists in 80 countries, so 5 journalists per country, on average. The Panama Papers had 11.5 million documents, and the journalists read them for a year, before publishing in 2016. An investigation takes months or years: the reporter follows the money, reads the documents, and protects the source, and the story is published only when the evidence is solid. The Watergate reporting won the Pulitzer Prize in 1973, and it is the most famous investigation in history.

Feature Writing

Feature writing is the journalism that tells the story in depth: the profile, the portrait, and the narrative, written with the scene, the character, and the detail. The feature is not the news of the day, but the story behind it: the person, the place, and the meaning. Feature writing is the journalism closest to literature, and it is the writing that the reader remembers.

A news story is about 500 words, and a feature is about 2,000 to 5,000 words: the feature is 4 to 10 times as long. A profile takes about 10 interviews, and the reporting takes a week, and the writing takes a week. A long read takes about 20 minutes to read, at 200 words a minute, so 4,000 words is 20 minutes. The feature is rewritten 3 times, on average: the first draft, the revision, and the polish, and each draft is shorter and clearer than the last.

The Newsroom

The newsroom is the place where the news is made: the room where the reporters, the editors, and the producers work, and where the stories are chosen, checked, and published. The newsroom is the organisation of journalism: the news meeting, the desk, the deadline, and the publication. It is the heart of the newspaper, the broadcast, and the online news, and it is where the journalism happens.

A newsroom has about 50 people: 30 reporters, 10 editors, and 10 producers and others. A news meeting chooses about 10 stories from 100, and the front page has about 5 stories. A newspaper is published every day, and the deadline is the evening: the stories must be ready by 6 in the evening, for the printing. A broadcast bulletin is made in 1 hour, and it is on air for 30 minutes: the newsroom works for 1 hour to make 30 minutes of news.

Ethics in Journalism

Ethics in journalism is the code of the journalist: the honesty, the fairness, the accuracy, and the independence that make the news trustworthy. The journalist must tell the truth, be fair to all sides, check every fact, and be independent of power and of money. Ethics is the trust of journalism: without it, the news is not believed, and the journalism fails. It is the most important part of the profession, after the accuracy.

A code has about 10 rules: the honesty, the fairness, the accuracy, the independence, the privacy, and the correction, and 4 more. The American code was written in 1923, and it is 103 years old in 2026. A correction is published the next day, and it is as prominent as the error: the error on the front page is corrected on the front page. The journalist is judged by the code: the breach is investigated, and the press council can censure, and the trust is the penalty for the breach.

Accuracy and Verification

Accuracy and verification are the heart of journalism: the getting of the facts right, and the checking of them before they are published. Accuracy is the correctness of every fact, and verification is the checking of it, against the source, the document, and the second source. A story with one wrong fact is a wrong story, and the correction is the admission. Accuracy is the trust of journalism, and verification is the work that earns it.

A story has about 20 facts: the names, the numbers, the dates, the places, and the quotes, and each must be checked. Two sources confirm the key fact: the first source says it, and the second source confirms it, independently. A fact-checker checks about 10 stories a day, and each story takes about 30 minutes to check. The reverse image search takes seconds, and the geolocation takes minutes: the false picture is found in minutes, and the true picture is proved by the map. One wrong fact makes the story wrong, and the correction is published the next day.

Sources and Confidentiality

Sources and confidentiality are the protection of the people who give the news: the source gives the information, and the journalist protects the source, by keeping the name secret. Confidentiality is the promise: the source speaks because they are protected, and the journalist keeps the promise, even in court. Without confidentiality, the sources would not speak, and the hidden wrong would stay hidden.

Deep Throat was protected for 30 years: from 1972 to 2005, the name was secret, and the source was known only as Deep Throat. A story has about 3 sources: the main source, the second source, and the document, and each is checked. The shield law protects the journalist in many countries, but not in all: the protection is partial, and the journalist can still be jailed in some places. The source speaks because of the promise: without the promise, the source would not speak, and the hidden wrong would stay hidden.

Press Freedom

Press freedom is the freedom of the journalist to report without fear: to publish the news, to criticise power, and to protect the source, without being jailed, censored, or killed. It is the freedom that makes journalism possible: without it, the news is the government's news, and the public does not know. Press freedom is the measure of a free society, and it is defended every day.

The World Press Freedom Index ranks 180 countries every year: the top country is the freest, and the bottom is the least free. Zenger was tried in 1735, and the First Amendment was written in 1791, 56 years later. The stamp tax was abolished in Britain in 1855, and the newspaper became cheap, and the readership grew. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, of 1948, says that everyone has the right to seek, receive, and impart information: it is the global statement of press freedom. In 2026, the freedom is still defended: the journalists are killed, jailed, and censored, in many countries, and the index records it.

Broadcast Journalism

Broadcast journalism is the journalism of radio and television: the news spoken and shown, as it happens, to the audience at home. The broadcast journalist reports live, presents the bulletin, and tells the story in pictures and in sound. Broadcast journalism is the most immediate journalism: the audience hears and sees the news, at the moment it happens, and the journalist is the witness, in person.

A 30-minute television bulletin has about 10 stories, so each story gets about 3 minutes. A 5-minute radio bulletin has about 5 stories, so each gets about 1 minute. The presenter reads at about 150 words a minute, so a 30-minute bulletin has about 4,500 words, and a 5-minute bulletin has about 750 words. The 24-hour channel has 48 half-hour bulletins a day, and the journalist works in shifts of 8 hours: 3 shifts cover the 24 hours. The live report is the heart of the broadcast: the reporter speaks for 2 minutes, from the scene, and the audience sees the event.

Photojournalism

Photojournalism is the journalism of the photograph: the picture that tells the news, without words, and the viewer understands the event at once. The photojournalist goes to the scene, and they photograph what they see, and the picture is published, and the audience understands. Photojournalism is the most direct journalism: the picture shows the event, and the viewer sees it, and the truth is in the image.

A photojournalist takes about 500 pictures a day, and 1 is published: 1 in 500 is chosen. A newspaper publishes about 20 photographs a day, and the front page has 1, the lead picture. The first war photographs were taken in 1854, in the Crimea, and the photograph is 170 years old in 2026. A digital camera takes 10 pictures a second, so a minute of shooting is 600 pictures, and the choice is the skill. The caption is 20 words, and it must be accurate: the what, the where, and the when, and the name.

Data Journalism

Data journalism is the journalism of the data: the reporter uses the numbers, the databases, and the spreadsheets to find the story, and to tell it with the chart and the map. The data journalist finds the pattern in the numbers, and the pattern is the news. Data journalism is the newest journalism, and the most precise: the numbers are the evidence, and the chart is the proof.

The Panama Papers had 11.5 million documents, and 400 journalists in 80 countries analysed them, so 5 journalists per country, on average. A spreadsheet has about 10,000 rows, and the journalist sorts them, and the pattern is found. A chart has about 5 bars, and the reader sees the pattern in seconds. Philip Meyer used a computer in 1967, and the data journalism is 60 years old in 2026. The data journalist finds the story in the numbers: the spending, the votes, and the deaths, and the numbers are the evidence.

Online and Social Media Journalism

Online and social media journalism is the journalism of the internet: the news published online, at once, and shared on social media, where the audience reads, comments, and shares. The online journalist publishes the story in minutes, updates it through the day, and talks to the audience in the comments. It is the fastest journalism, and the most interactive: the audience is not only the reader, but the participant.

An online story is published in minutes, and it is updated 5 times a day, on average. A story shared on social media reaches 1,000 people in an hour, and 10,000 in a day, if it is shared. The first news websites appeared in the 1990s, and online journalism is about 30 years old in 2026. A website publishes about 100 stories a day, and the audience reads 3 of them: the reader chooses, and the sharing decides. The comment is the audience's voice: 100 comments on a story is the conversation, and the journalist reads them.

Local Journalism

Local journalism is the journalism of the place: the town, the county, and the community, and the news that matters to the people who live there. The local journalist covers the council, the court, the school, and the club, and the reader knows the people and the places. Local journalism is the closest journalism: it is the news of the reader's own street, and it is the journalism that the community cannot do without.

A local paper has about 20 pages, and it is published weekly, so 52 issues a year. A local journalist covers 1 council, 1 court, and 10 schools, and they know them all. In the 20th century, there were thousands of local papers, and in the 21st, hundreds have closed: the news deserts are the places with no paper. A local story is read by 1,000 people in the town, and the national story is read by 1 million: the local is small, but it is the reader's own. The council meets 12 times a year, and the journalist attends every meeting.

Sports Journalism

Sports journalism is the journalism of sport: the match, the player, the team, and the result, reported for the fans. The sports journalist goes to the match, watches it, talks to the players, and writes the report, and the fan reads it, and relives the game. Sports journalism is the most read journalism: the sports pages are the first pages many readers turn to, and the match report is the story of the day.

A football match lasts 90 minutes, and the report is written in 30 minutes after the final whistle. A sports journalist covers about 2 matches a week, and 100 matches a year. The live blog updates every minute, so a 90-minute match has about 90 updates. The sports pages are about 10 pages of a 40-page paper, so a quarter of the paper is sport. The transfer window lasts about 2 months, and the journalist reports the moves, every day, until it closes.

War Reporting

War reporting is the journalism of war: the correspondent reports from the front, from the city under fire, and from the refugee camp, and the public learns what war is. The war reporter is the witness of the worst: they go where the danger is, and they tell the truth, so the public knows. War reporting is the most dangerous journalism, and the most necessary: without it, the war is unseen, and the public does not know.

The first war correspondent was in the Crimea in 1854, and war reporting is 172 years old in 2026. A dispatch takes a day to write, and it is sent by satellite, in minutes. The war reporter works in shifts of 12 hours, and the danger is constant: the flak jacket and the helmet are worn, always. The Crimean reporting forced the government to act: the public read of the suffering, and the hospitals were reformed. The war reporter is the witness: without them, the war is unseen, and the public does not know.

Journalism and the Law

Journalism and the law is the law that governs the journalist: the libel, the contempt, the privacy, and the official secrets, and the freedom that protects the reporting. The journalist must know the law, because the wrong word can mean the court, the fine, or the jail. The law is both the limit and the protection: it limits what can be published, and it protects the freedom to publish.

Zenger was tried in 1735, and the truth became a defence: the trial is 291 years old in 2026. A libel case can cost millions: the damages, and the costs, and the newspaper pays. The contempt of court applies from the arrest to the verdict: the journalist must not report the defendant's past, or the jury is biased. The story is checked by the lawyer before it is published: the risky story takes an hour to check, and the safe story takes minutes. The law is the last check: the journalist knows it, and the lawyer confirms it.

The Business of News

The business of news is the money of journalism: the advertising, the subscription, and the sale, which pay for the reporting. The newspaper is sold, and the advertising is sold, and the money pays the journalists. The business of news is in crisis: the advertising went online, and the money fell, and the newspapers closed. The new business is the subscription, the membership, and the voluntary contribution, and the journalist is paid by the reader, not the advertiser.

A newspaper of 40 pages has about 24 pages of advertising, which is 60 percent, and 16 pages of editorial. The advertising revenue fell by half in a decade: 100 became 50, in 10 years. A subscription costs about 10 pounds a month, and 120 pounds a year, and 10,000 subscribers pay 1.2 million pounds a year. The circulation of a national paper is about 1 million, and of a local paper about 10,000: the national is 100 times the local. The business is the reader: the subscriber pays, and the journalist is paid by the reader.

Journalism in Different Countries

Journalism in different countries is different: the press is free in some countries, and controlled in others, and the journalist is safe in some, and in danger in others. The systems are the models: the liberal, the democratic corporatist, and the polarised pluralist, and the state-controlled. Journalism in different countries is the comparison: what works, where, and why, and the freedom is the measure.

The World Press Freedom Index ranks 180 countries every year: the top is the freest, and the bottom is the least free. Hallin and Mancini described the three models in 2004, and the book is 22 years old in 2026. The liberal model is Britain and America, the democratic corporatist is northern Europe, and the polarised pluralist is southern Europe: 3 models, and the state-controlled is the fourth. A journalist is safe in the top 10 countries, and in danger in the bottom 10: the index is the measure, and the freedom is the difference.

The Future of Journalism

The future of journalism is the question of what journalism will become: the artificial intelligence, the automated story, the personalised news, and the citizen journalist. It is the future of the reporter, the editor, and the audience: will the reporter remain, will the editor remain, and will the audience trust the news? The future is uncertain, but the direction is clear: the technology will change the form, and the human will remain the heart.

An automated story is written in seconds: the sports result, and the financial report, written from the data, by the machine. A citizen journalist films the event on the phone, and the newsroom verifies it in minutes, and the public learns in an hour. The personalised news is chosen by the algorithm: the reader sees 10 stories, chosen from 100, and the choice is theirs. The trust is the value: the audience trusts the journalist who checks, and the trust is earned, story by story. The future is the human: the reporter, the editor, and the audience, and the technology is the tool.

How to Read the News

How to read the news is the skill of the citizen: the checking of the source, the story, and the claim, before believing it. The reader asks: who says it, what is the evidence, and is it true? The skill is the defence against the false news: the reader who checks is not fooled, and the reader who does not, is. How to read the news is the literacy of the citizen, and it is the most important skill of the digital age.

A reader reads about 3 stories a day, and each takes about 5 minutes, so 15 minutes of news a day. A false story is shared 10 times more than a true one, in the studies: the false spreads fast, and the true spreads slow. The fact-checker checks about 10 claims a day, and each takes about 30 minutes. The reverse image search takes seconds, and the check of the source takes a minute: the reader who checks is not fooled, and the check is the defence. The citizen reads the news every day, and the skill is used every day, for life.
