Refractive Index Method Rank 1 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:19-20 names twelve foundation stones. Ancient stone names do not map certainly onto modern minerals, so every identification in this pair keeps its alternatives beside it.

Engineering view

The refractive index method measures how much a gemstone bends light, using a refractometer and a contact liquid, and it is the first optical test in most gem identification.

Public practice places a polished facet flat on the refractometer hemisphere with a drop of contact liquid of known high index, then reads the shadow edge on the instrument scale. The reading is taken in sodium light where possible, temperature is noted, and doubly refractive stones show two readings whose difference is the birefringence.

A single reading never identifies a stone on its own; it narrows the candidates, and the other optical tests in this pair do the rest. Published index ranges for common gem minerals are the reference, and a reading outside a published range is a reason to re-measure, not to invent a new mineral.

print("Sine of incident angle: %s" % (sin_incident,))
print("Sine of refracted angle: %s" % (sin_refracted,))
print("Refractive index by Snell ratio: %s" % (n,))

Dispersion Measurement Rank 2 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:19-20 names twelve foundation stones. Ancient stone names do not map certainly onto modern minerals, so every identification in this pair keeps its alternatives beside it.

Engineering view

Dispersion measurement quantifies how a stone splits white light into spectral colours, expressed through readings at several wavelengths and summarised by the Abbe number.

Public practice reads the refractive index at the Fraunhofer F line (blue), the d line (yellow), and the C line (red), then computes dispersion as the F-minus-C difference and the Abbe number as (n_d minus 1) divided by that difference. High dispersion stones such as diamond show strong fire; low dispersion stones show little. The measurement needs a refractometer or spectrometer with wavelength control, and the wavelengths used are always stated, because a dispersion value without its wavelengths cannot be compared with any other.

Working demo program

# Dispersion Measurement
n_F = 2.435
n_d = 2.417
n_C = 2.407
dispersion = n_F - n_C
abbe = (n_d - 1) / dispersion
print("Dispersion n_F minus n_C: %s" % (round(dispersion, 4),))
print("Abbe number: %s" % (round(abbe, 1),))

Absorption Measurement Rank 3 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:19-20 names twelve foundation stones. Ancient stone names do not map certainly onto modern minerals, so every identification in this pair keeps its alternatives beside it.

Engineering view

Absorption measurement records which wavelengths a stone absorbs, using a spectroscope or spectrophotometer, and the resulting spectrum is a fingerprint for colour cause.

Public practice passes light through the stone and records the dark bands and cut-offs across the visible spectrum, noting strong, weak, and sharp lines separately. Chromium-bearing stones show characteristic red-region lines, iron-bearing stones show their own bands, and a spectrum with no diagnostic bands is still a result: it rules out the colour causes that would have shown bands. Spectra are compared against published reference spectra for known minerals, and an unmatched spectrum is reported as unmatched, not forced onto the nearest reference.

Color Measurement Rank 4 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Faith interpretation: Revelation 21:19-20 names twelve foundation stones. Ancient stone names do not map certainly onto modern minerals, so every identification in this pair keeps its alternatives beside it.

Engineering view

Colour measurement replaces vague colour words with measured values in a standard colour space, so two stones can be compared without arguing about what 'blue' means.

Public practice measures colour with a spectrophotometer under a stated illuminant and reports CIELAB coordinates: L* for lightness, a* for the red-green axis, and b* for the yellow-blue axis. Colour difference between a sample and a reference is the Delta E distance between their coordinates, and the illuminant and observer angle are always stated, because the same stone measures differently under different light. Colour words — hue, tone, saturation — are kept beside the numbers as descriptions, never instead of them.

Working demo program

# Color Measurement

dL = 2.0
da = 3.0
db = 4.0
delta_E = math.sqrt(dL**2 + da**2 + db**2)
print("Lightness difference dL: %s" % (dL,))
print("Red-green difference da: %s" % (da,))
print("Yellow-blue difference db: %s" % (db,))
print("Colour difference Delta E: %s" % (round(delta_E, 2),))

Engineering view

Surface finish specifications state how smooth, flat, and polished a gemstone surface must be, in measurable terms rather than by eye alone.

Public specifications name the polish grade, the flatness tolerance of table facets, the symmetry tolerance of the cut, and the allowed surface defects per facet, each with a measurement method — loupe inspection at stated magnification, plus interferometry or profilometry where flatness is critical. A finish specification also states what it does not cover: a polished surface can still carry internal inclusions, and a flawless-looking table can still hide a poor girdle. Finish, cut symmetry, and clarity are separate judgements, and this record keeps them separate.

Measurement Instruments Rank 6 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Engineering view

Measurement instruments are the tools that make gemstone optics measurable: refractometers, spectroscopes, spectrophotometers, polariscopes, and microscopes, each with its own calibration.

Public instrument practice names the instrument, its measurement range, its resolution, and its calibration date and standard for every reading set. A refractometer reads index to about a thousandth under good conditions; a spectrophotometer measures colour and absorption across the visible range; a polariscope reveals strain and double refraction; a microscope at stated magnification finds the surface and internal features the other instruments cannot. An instrument past its calibration date produces numbers, not measurements, and its readings are marked accordingly until it is recalibrated.

Mounting Optics Rank 8 · feasibility: achievable

Engineering view

Mounting optics covers how a setting, a backing, and a surrounding material change what the eye sees of a stone, without changing the stone itself.

Public mounting practice treats the setting as part of the optical system: a closed backing darkens a light stone, an open setting lets light enter from behind, a reflective cup lifts a pale stone's apparent saturation, and a dark mount deepens a transparent stone's body colour. Mounting choices are recorded with the stone's optical data, because the same stone measured loose and measured mounted can legitimately differ. A mounting that hides a stone's defects behind a reflective backing without saying so is misrepresentation, and this record requires the mounting to be described wherever mounted data is reported.

Engineering view

Sample-versus-model comparison checks a measured stone against its predicted optical model, and reports the difference rather than assuming the model is right.

Public comparison takes the measured index, dispersion, absorption, and colour of a sample and sets them beside the model's predicted values for the same stone class, then computes the difference per property. Differences within the stated tolerance confirm the model for that sample; differences outside it trigger re-measurement first, then model revision, and a model that is revised silently is not a model but a moving target. The comparison record keeps the sample identity, the model version, and the tolerance used, so any later reader can repeat the comparison.

Optical Validation Method Rank 10 · feasibility: demonstrated

Text-faithful view

Engineering view

The optical validation method is the procedure that decides whether a set of gemstone optical measurements can be trusted, before it is used for identification.

Public validation checks instrument calibration, repeats the key readings, compares them against a reference stone of known values, and reviews the record for missing fields — temperature, wavelength, illuminant, magnification — before the data is accepted. A data set that fails any check is returned for re-measurement, not patched by editing the numbers toward the expected value. Validation is a gate, not a polish: its job is to stop bad data, and a validation step that never rejects anything is not validating.

Engineering view

Engineered composite substitution designs manufactured materials that approximate a gemstone's optical behaviour where the natural stone is unavailable, impractical, or too fragile at the required scale.

Public substitution practice starts from the target optical values — index, dispersion, absorption, colour — and searches manufactured glasses, ceramics, and composites for the nearest match, then reports the residual difference per property rather than claiming identity. Nacre-inspired panels approximate pearl appearance, and engineered ceramics approximate jasper patterning, but a composite that matches one property and misses another is reported as a partial substitute, with the miss stated. This is labeled research-required at monumental scale: laboratory composites are demonstrated, while large architectural panels matching a named gemstone across all optical properties are still a research programme, not a catalogue product.

Engineering view

Spectral materials tracking follows each material's spectral signature through design, manufacture, and installation, so a substituted panel can be checked against its design target at any stage.

Public tracking assigns every material batch a spectral record — its measured transmission, reflection, and absorption curves — and links that record to the panels made from the batch and the positions where those panels are installed. A panel whose installed spectrum differs from its design spectrum beyond tolerance is flagged for review, and the batch record lets the reviewer trace whether the difference came from the material, the coating, or the installation. Tracking is bookkeeping with spectra attached; its value is that a later question about any panel has a measured answer.

Engineering view

Gemstone data provenance records where every optical value came from, so a later reader can tell a measured value from a copied value from an estimated value.

Public provenance practice attaches a source to every value: the instrument and operator for a measurement, the publication and page for a copied reference range, and the method note for an estimate. Values without a source are marked unsourced and excluded from identification decisions until they are sourced. Provenance is what separates a database from a scrapbook: the numbers may look identical, but only the sourced ones can be checked, and only checked numbers should decide an identification.

Engineering view

Alternative-identification optics measures the optical differences between the candidate modern minerals for one ancient stone name, so the identification choice is evidence-based rather than traditional.

For each ancient name with more than one plausible modern mineral, public practice measures the candidates side by side — index, dispersion, absorption, colour — and reports which properties separate them and which overlap. Where the candidates overlap on every measured property, the record says so plainly: the identification is then undecided by optics, and history or context must decide, or the question stays open. Keeping the question open is a result, not a failure, and this record never resolves an undecided identification by picking the most familiar candidate.

Engineering view

An optical record template is the fixed set of fields every gemstone optics record carries, so records from different stones can be compared and audited.

The template fields are: stone identity and alternative identifications, instrument set with calibration dates, index readings with temperature, dispersion wavelengths and values, absorption bands, colour coordinates with illuminant, finish grade with measurement method, mounting description where applicable, provenance per value, and validation result. A record missing its calibration dates or its provenance is incomplete, and a record missing its alternatives for an ancient name is incomplete for that name. The template is deliberately boring: its value is that the same questions get asked of every stone, every time.

Engineering view

Optics acceptance tests are the pass-or-fail checks a gemstone optics data set must clear before it is used for identification or design.

The acceptance set covers instrument calibration currency, index repeatability, dispersion wavelength statement, absorption scale statement, colour illuminant statement, finish method statement, mounting description for mounted data, provenance per value, alternative identifications for ancient names, and validation gate passage. Each test names its method and its pass condition in advance. A data set passes acceptance when every test passes or every failure is formally accepted with a reason and a re-measurement date; a data set with an unrecorded failure has not been accepted.
