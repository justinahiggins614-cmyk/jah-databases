A Poster With One Clear Message Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: buildable-now One-message poster design is established practice today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a poster creator that builds a poster around one clear message, with nothing on the page that fights it. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

A poster that tries to say three things says nothing: the reader's eye has nowhere to land, the words compete with each other, and the one thing that mattered gets lost among the rest. A poster works only when one message is biggest, one message is first, and everything else serves it. A creator that asks for the one message first, makes it the biggest thing on the page, and refuses to let anything else grow as large, turns a crowded poster into a clear one: the reader sees the message from across the room, understands it in seconds, and remembers it afterward. The discipline is simple, and it is the difference between a poster and a noticeboard full of noise.

Worked example: a poster for a school fair carries one message, SCHOOL FAIR SATURDAY, biggest on the page, with the time and place below it in smaller type.

History

The one-message poster is as old as printed notices, from town criers' bills to wartime posters that said one thing in letters a street away. The creator applies that old discipline, before anything is drawn.

Proposed solution

Ask for the one message before anything else; Make that message the biggest thing on the page; Keep every other element smaller and in service to it; Check that the message reads from across the room; Refuse to add a second message of the same size.

Dependencies

A way to name the one message; type sizes that keep it largest; a reading-distance check; a refusal for a second equal message.

Feasibility

Verification path

Show the poster to five people from across a room and check that at least four can repeat the one message; check that no other element on the page is as large as the message.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order; Give each record a one-line account of what it covers; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.
