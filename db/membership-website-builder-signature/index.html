A membership website: a person builds plain pages for members, keeps a member list of people who joined by their own choice, and writes in plain words what members receive and how to leave. Payments, where a site owner chooses to take them, run strictly between the site owner and their own payment provider, are opt-in, and never pass through this site, which never touches funds or card data. Every member page carries a plain note saying how to leave the membership. This record is the field guide index for the whole database: it lists all twenty-three content records in ladder order, simple to compounded to complex, and tells a reader where to start.

A database of twenty-three records needs a map, or a reader will not know where to start, which records are simple and which are complex, and how the records fit together. Without an index, the simple records that teach the design are buried beside the complex records that assume it, and a visitor who wants one idea must read the whole database to find it. The index solves this by listing every record in ladder order, with its place in the ladder, so a reader can start at the beginning and climb, or jump straight to the record they need.

Worked example, Signature design: a reader who wants the simplest starting point opens record 1, A First Membership Site With Three Plain Pages, and a reader who wants the hardest problem opens record 23, The Membership Website Championship Format, in the Signature edition.

History

Clubs with members are an old habit: a book of members, a list of what members receive, a gathering place kept for members only. The website form grows out of that habit, adding plain pages and a member list a person can join by choice. Field guides and indexes are as old as books themselves: a table of contents that tells a reader where everything lives. This index follows that habit, listing the twenty-three content records of this database in the order a reader should meet them.
