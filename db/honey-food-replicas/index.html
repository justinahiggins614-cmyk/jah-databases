Honeycomb Candy Stars and Coins Rank 1 · simple · confectionery · easy

Honeycomb candy is a classic fairground sweet: hot sugar and honey syrup is frothed with bicarbonate of soda, then sets into a crisp, bubbly sponge that shatters when bitten. This replica pours the foaming syrup into star and coin molds, so the finished candy keeps the shape of a star or a coin while keeping the real honeycomb texture inside. The honey in the syrup gives the candy a warm floral note and a deeper golden color than plain sugar honeycomb. Broken edges can be dipped in honey-sweetened chocolate for a dressed-up version.

Original food being replicated

Honeycomb candy (cinder toffee), the golden aerated candy made by adding bicarbonate of soda to hot sugar syrup.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 200 g granulated sugar
  • 80 g honey
  • 60 ml water
  • 1.5 teaspoons bicarbonate of soda, sieved
  • Star and coin silicone molds, lightly oiled
  • Optional: 100 g honey-sweetened chocolate for dipping

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Combine the sugar, honey, and water in a heavy saucepan and stir over low heat until the sugar dissolves completely.
  2. Boil without stirring until the syrup reaches 150 degrees C (hard crack stage), watching the color turn deep amber.
  3. Remove from the heat, whisk in the sieved bicarbonate of soda quickly until the mixture foams and triples in volume.
  4. Pour at once into the prepared molds, filling each cavity about three quarters full, and leave undisturbed until fully set.
  5. Turn out the stars and coins, trim any overflow with a sharp knife, and dip the edges in melted honey-sweetened chocolate if desired.
  6. Store in an airtight container, since honeycomb softens quickly in humid air.

Food science: why it works

At hard crack stage almost all the water has boiled out of the syrup, so the candy sets glassy and crisp. Bicarbonate of soda reacts with the hot acid syrup and releases carbon dioxide, and the gas bubbles are trapped as the syrup cools and hardens, which is what makes the sponge texture. Honey adds fructose and glucose, which brown faster through caramelization and Maillard reactions, so honeycomb colors more quickly than plain sugar candy and must be watched closely.

Safety

Hot sugar syrup causes severe burns; keep children away from the pan and never touch the foaming mixture. Honey is not for infants under 12 months. The candy is very high in sugar and is a treat food only.

Storage

Keep in an airtight container at cool room temperature for up to two weeks; humidity makes honeycomb sticky and soft, so do not refrigerate it.

Variations

  • Swap the honey variety for a different floral note, such as clover or wildflower honey.
  • Pour the foam into a lined tray and break it into shards for classic honeycomb pieces.
  • Dip whole pieces in honey-sweetened chocolate for a chocolate honeycomb bar style.

Honey Cake Shaped as a Watermelon Slice Rank 2 · simple · baking · easy

This trompe-l'oeil cake starts as a genuine honey spice cake, baked in a round pan and cut into wedges, then dressed to fool the eye. The crumb is tinted pink-red, the rind is a band of green-tinted honey buttercream, and dark chocolate drops stand in for watermelon seeds. The joke lands because the cake itself is honestly good: honey keeps the crumb moist for days, and warm spices carry the flavor. It is a party cake first and a visual trick second.

Original food being replicated

Honey cake, the moist spice cake sweetened with honey, here shaped and decorated to look like a slice of watermelon.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 250 g plain flour
  • 150 g honey
  • 100 g soft butter
  • 2 eggs
  • 1 teaspoon bicarbonate of soda
  • 1 teaspoon mixed spice
  • 120 ml milk
  • Red food coloring
  • Green-tinted honey buttercream for the rind
  • 30 g dark chocolate drops for seeds

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Cream the butter and honey, beat in the eggs, then fold in the flour, bicarbonate of soda, and spice with the milk.
  2. Tint the batter pink-red, pour into a lined round pan, and bake at 175 degrees C for 35 to 40 minutes until a skewer comes out clean.
  3. Cool completely, then cut the round into wedges like slices of a pie.
  4. Frost the curved edge of each wedge with green-tinted honey buttercream to form the rind, leaving the flat faces pink.
  5. Press chocolate drops into the pink faces as seeds, and chill briefly so the buttercream firms before serving.
  6. Serve on a plate as if it were fruit, and reveal the cake only when the first slice is cut.

Food science: why it works

Honey is hygroscopic, meaning it attracts and holds water, which is why honey cakes stay moist far longer than sugar-only cakes. The bicarbonate of soda reacts with the natural acidity of honey, releasing gas that lifts the crumb, while the sugars in honey brown through Maillard reactions with the flour proteins for a deep color and toasty flavor. Food coloring changes only appearance; the structure is set by the flour, eggs, and leavening.

Safety

Bake fully before decorating, and keep the cake refrigerated if the buttercream will sit out for more than two hours in warm weather. Honey is not for infants under 12 months.

Storage

Keep covered at room temperature for up to three days, or refrigerate for up to a week; bring to room temperature before serving.

Variations

  • Shape the cake as an orange slice with orange-tinted crumb and white pith buttercream.
  • Bake as cupcakes and decorate each one as a mini watermelon wedge.
  • Use a lemon honey cake base with yellow crumb for a lemon-slice version.

Honey Marzipan Apples and Mice Rank 3 · simple · confectionery · easy

Marzipan fruits are a confectioner's classic: almond paste shaped by hand, painted, and finished to look like miniature fruit. This replica binds the almond paste with honey rather than sugar syrup, which gives a softer, more fragrant paste that holds a shape well and tastes of honey and almonds together. Apples are rolled, dimpled, and stemmed with a clove, while mice are formed from a teardrop body, pointed ears, and a thin tail. The shapes are small enough to make by hand with no special molds.

Original food being replicated

Marzipan fruits and animals, the classic almond-paste confections, here bound with honey instead of sugar syrup.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 200 g finely ground almonds
  • 100 g icing sugar, sifted
  • 60 g honey
  • 1 teaspoon lemon juice
  • Food coloring in red, green, and brown
  • Whole cloves for apple stems
  • Thin pretzel sticks or licorice strings for mouse tails

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Mix the ground almonds and icing sugar, then work in the honey and lemon juice until a smooth, firm paste forms.
  2. Knead briefly on a surface dusted with icing sugar, and divide the paste into portions for each fruit or animal.
  3. For apples, roll portions into balls, dimple the top with a fingertip, tint red or green, and press a clove in as the stem.
  4. For mice, shape a teardrop body, pinch two small ears, add tiny eyes with a toothpick dot of coloring, and add a tail.
  5. Let the shapes dry uncovered for a few hours so the surface firms, then brush lightly with thinned honey for a shine if desired.
  6. Arrange on a plate as a miniature fruit bowl or mouse family.

Food science: why it works

Almond paste holds its shape because the almond oils and the fine particles form a dense, moldable mass, and honey acts as both binder and plasticizer: its sugars and small amount of water keep the paste pliable instead of crumbly. As the surface dries, a thin skin forms that protects the shape. Honey's fructose is sweeter than sucrose, so less added sugar is needed than in syrup-bound marzipan.

Safety

Contains tree nuts (almonds); keep away from anyone with nut allergy, and label the plate clearly. Honey is not for infants under 12 months.

Storage

Keep in an airtight container at cool room temperature for up to two weeks; the surface dries out if left uncovered.

Variations

  • Shape pears, strawberries, and oranges from the same paste with appropriate colors.
  • Roll the paste into small potatoes and dust with cocoa for marzipan potatoes.
  • Flavor the paste with orange zest or rose water for different classic marzipan styles.

Honey Nougat Bars Molded as Bricks Rank 4 · simple · confectionery · easy

Nougat is one of the oldest honey candies: whipped egg white is beaten into hot honey syrup until it turns white and chewy, then nuts are folded in. This replica sets the nougat in a rectangular mold and cuts it into bricks, so the finished candy looks like a tiny wall of golden bricks with visible nuts. The honey is not a flavoring here; it is the structural sugar of the candy, and its flavor defines the finished bar. A brick stack makes a playful edible building set for a dessert table.

Original food being replicated

Nougat, the chewy honey-and-nut candy of torrone and French nougat tradition, molded into brick shapes.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 250 g honey
  • 150 g granulated sugar
  • 60 ml water
  • 1 egg white
  • 200 g mixed toasted nuts (almonds, pistachios)
  • Rice paper for lining the mold
  • Icing sugar for dusting

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Boil the honey, sugar, and water to 130 degrees C (hard ball stage), while whipping the egg white to soft peaks in a separate bowl.
  2. Pour the hot syrup into the whipping egg white in a thin stream, beating constantly until the mixture turns thick, white, and glossy.
  3. Fold in the toasted nuts quickly, since the mixture stiffens as it cools.
  4. Press into a rice-paper-lined rectangular mold, smooth the top, cover with a second sheet of rice paper, and weight it lightly.
  5. Cool for at least four hours, then turn out and cut into brick shapes with a sharp, oiled knife.
  6. Dust lightly with icing sugar and stack like bricks.

Food science: why it works

Nougat sets through two mechanisms at once: the egg-white proteins form a foam that traps air, and the concentrated honey-sugar syrup becomes a viscous glass as it cools, locking the foam in place. Honey's mixture of glucose and fructose resists crystallization better than pure sucrose, which keeps the nougat chewy rather than grainy. Heating the syrup to hard ball stage removes enough water that the finished candy holds its shape at room temperature.

Safety

Hot syrup causes burns; pour in a thin stream and keep hands clear of the bowl. Contains egg and tree nuts. Honey is not for infants under 12 months.

Storage

Keep wrapped in wax paper in an airtight container at cool room temperature for up to three weeks; humidity softens nougat.

Variations

  • Use hazelnuts and dried cherries for a festive brick.
  • Press into round molds for nougat coins instead of bricks.
  • Dip half of each brick in honey-sweetened chocolate.

Honey-Glazed Fruit Replicas Rank 5 · simple · confectionery · easy

A honey glaze turns ordinary fruit into a jewel-like replica of itself: whole or halved fruit is dipped in a hot honey syrup that sets to a clear, shining coat. This record covers glazed strawberries, grapes, and citrus slices, where the glaze is the replica finish that makes the fruit look lacquered and precious. The honey glaze is thinner and more fragrant than a plain sugar glaze, and it clings in a thinner coat because honey flows more readily when warm. The result is eaten fresh, with the glaze adding sweetness and shine rather than hiding the fruit.

Original food being replicated

Glazed fruit, the candied and mirror-glazed fruit of the confectioner's window, here made as replicas with a honey glaze finish.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 500 g mixed fruit (strawberries, grapes, citrus slices), washed and fully dried
  • 150 g honey
  • 50 g granulated sugar
  • 2 tablespoons water
  • Wooden skewers
  • Baking paper for setting

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Dry the fruit completely, since any water on the surface will make the glaze slide off.
  2. Heat the honey, sugar, and water to 115 degrees C (soft ball stage) and keep it warm over very low heat.
  3. Skewer each piece of fruit and dip it into the glaze, turning once to coat evenly, then let the excess drip off.
  4. Set each piece on baking paper without touching its neighbors, and leave until the glaze firms to a tacky shine.
  5. Serve within a few hours, while the glaze is still glossy and the fruit is fresh.
  6. For citrus slices, glaze both faces and hang briefly to set before laying flat.

Food science: why it works

A glaze sets as the syrup cools and its sugars become more viscous, clinging to the fruit in a thin, transparent film. Honey's fructose keeps the film flexible and shiny rather than brittle, which is why a honey glaze stays tacky and bright instead of cracking like a hard candy coat. The glaze also slows moisture loss from the fruit surface for a few hours, though it cannot preserve the fruit for long.

Safety

Hot glaze burns; dip with skewers, not fingers, and keep children away from the pan. Honey is not for infants under 12 months. Wash fruit well before glazing.

Storage

Best eaten the same day; refrigerate glazed fruit for no more than one day, since condensation dulls the glaze.

Variations

  • Add a drop of lemon juice to the glaze for glazed citrus with extra shine.
  • Roll glazed grapes in finely chopped pistachios before the glaze sets.
  • Glaze small melon balls for a jewel-box fruit platter.

Hard Honey Candy Gems Rank 6 · compounded · confectionery · moderate

Hard candy is sugar boiled to the hard crack stage and cooled into glass, and this replica molds that glass into faceted gem shapes in jewel colors. Honey replaces part of the sugar, giving the gems a warm golden base color and a floral note that plain sugar candy lacks. Faceted silicone molds give the gems their cut faces, and a light dusting of edible luster or a second color poured in layers makes them look like real stones. They are eaten as sweets, not worn, and they dissolve slowly like any hard candy.

Original food being replicated

Hard candy, the glassy boiled sweet, here made with honey as the defining sugar and molded into gem shapes.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 300 g granulated sugar
  • 120 g honey
  • 100 ml water
  • Food coloring (ruby, emerald, sapphire)
  • Gem-shaped silicone molds, lightly oiled
  • Optional: edible luster dust

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Combine the sugar, honey, and water and boil without stirring to 150 degrees C (hard crack stage).
  2. Remove from the heat, divide into portions if coloring, and tint each portion quickly.
  3. Pour into the oiled gem molds, working fast before the candy begins to set.
  4. Cool completely until the gems are hard and glassy, then turn them out carefully.
  5. Brush with edible luster dust if a jewel shine is wanted, and wrap each gem individually.
  6. Store away from moisture, which makes hard candy sticky.

Food science: why it works

Hard candy is an amorphous sugar glass: at hard crack stage so little water remains that the cooled syrup cannot form crystals, and instead freezes into a clear, brittle solid. Honey's glucose and fructose interfere with sucrose crystallization, which helps the candy stay clear rather than grainy. Color is suspended in the glass, and facets catch the light, which is why a molded candy reads as a gem.

Safety

Boiling syrup causes severe burns; pour carefully and keep children away. Honey is not for infants under 12 months. Hard candy is a choking hazard for small children; supervise young eaters.

Storage

Wrap individually and keep in an airtight container at cool room temperature for up to two months; humidity is the main enemy.

Variations

  • Pour in two layers of different colors for two-tone gems.
  • Add a drop of fruit flavoring with the coloring for flavored gems.
  • Mold the same candy as coins, stars, or buttons.

Baklava Shaped as a Flower Box Rank 7 · compounded · baking · moderate

Baklava is built from dozens of buttered phyllo sheets layered with chopped nuts, baked crisp, and drenched in honey syrup. This replica bakes the pastry in a rectangular pan, then cuts and arranges it as a flower box: the golden top layer becomes the box, and piped honey cream and nut flowers sit on top like blooms. The shaping is in the cutting and the decoration, not in changing the pastry itself, so the baklava stays authentic in texture: shattering layers, rich nuts, and syrup that soaks through without making it soggy. Cross-links: see the baklava references in the network for the classic unshaped pastry.

Original food being replicated

Baklava, the layered phyllo, nut, and honey-syrup pastry of the eastern Mediterranean, shaped here as a flower box.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 400 g phyllo pastry, thawed
  • 200 g melted butter
  • 300 g chopped walnuts and pistachios
  • 1 teaspoon cinnamon
  • 250 g honey
  • 100 g granulated sugar
  • 100 ml water
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • Honey cream and extra chopped pistachios for flower decoration

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Layer half the phyllo sheets in a buttered rectangular pan, brushing each sheet with melted butter.
  2. Spread the nuts mixed with cinnamon over the layers, then layer the remaining phyllo on top, buttering each sheet.
  3. Score the top into the flower-box panels before baking, cutting through the top layers only.
  4. Bake at 175 degrees C for 40 to 45 minutes until deep golden and crisp.
  5. Boil the honey, sugar, water, and lemon juice into a syrup, then pour the hot syrup over the hot pastry.
  6. Cool completely, cut along the scored lines, and decorate the top with piped honey cream flowers and pistachio petals.

Food science: why it works

Phyllo crisps because its paper-thin sheets lose water quickly in the oven, and the butter between them fries each layer separately so they stay distinct instead of fusing. The honey syrup is absorbed by capillary action into the hot, dry layers, and as it cools its viscosity rises, which holds the syrup inside the pastry rather than letting it run out. Honey's acidity balances the richness of the butter and nuts, which is why baklava syrup is flavored with lemon.

Safety

Phyllo dries out fast; keep unused sheets covered with a damp cloth while working. Contains tree nuts and wheat. Honey is not for infants under 12 months.

Storage

Keep covered at room temperature for up to one week; refrigeration makes phyllo soggy, so avoid it.

Variations

  • Shape the pastry into individual diamond parcels instead of a box.
  • Use almonds and orange zest for a different regional style.
  • Top with dried rose petals for a floral presentation.

Honey Bread Shaped as a Wheat Sheaf Rank 8 · compounded · baking · moderate

Honey bread is an enriched dough in which honey feeds the yeast and sweetens the crumb, giving a soft loaf with a glossy, deep-brown crust. This replica shapes the dough into a sheaf: a bundle of long rolls tied with a dough band, with scored wheat-ear shapes laid across the top. The sheaf is baked as one piece, so it pulls apart into individual rolls at the table. The honey in the dough is doing real work, not just flavoring: it speeds the rise and deepens the crust color.

Original food being replicated

Honey bread, the soft enriched loaf sweetened with honey, shaped here as a wheat sheaf.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 500 g strong bread flour
  • 80 g honey
  • 300 ml warm milk
  • 7 g instant yeast
  • 50 g soft butter
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 egg for glazing
  • Sesame or poppy seeds for decoration

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Dissolve the honey in the warm milk, sprinkle in the yeast, and leave for five minutes until foamy.
  2. Mix with the flour, butter, and salt, knead for ten minutes until smooth and elastic, then rise covered for one hour.
  3. Divide the dough into long rolls, gather them into a bundle, and tie the bundle with a thin dough band.
  4. Shape small wheat ears from leftover dough by scoring oval pieces with a knife, and lay them across the top of the sheaf.
  5. Prove for 30 minutes, brush with beaten egg, sprinkle with seeds, and bake at 190 degrees C for 25 to 30 minutes.
  6. Cool on a rack; the sheaf pulls apart into rolls for serving.

Food science: why it works

Yeast ferments the sugars in honey and flour into carbon dioxide and alcohol, and the gas inflates the gluten network, which is what makes bread rise. Honey's sugars are already simple sugars, so yeast can use them immediately, which gives a faster, more vigorous rise than sugar alone. In the oven, the Maillard reaction between honey sugars and flour proteins browns the crust deeply, and the egg glaze adds shine.

Safety

Bake until the internal temperature reaches about 95 degrees C and the loaf sounds hollow when tapped. Honey is not for infants under 12 months. Contains wheat and milk.

Storage

Keep in a bread bag at room temperature for up to three days, or freeze sliced for longer storage.

Variations

  • Shape the same dough as a braided loaf or as individual honey rolls.
  • Add cinnamon and raisins for a spiced sheaf.
  • Bake as a round harvest loaf with a dough wheat-ear on top.

Honey Pectin Gummy Berries Rank 9 · compounded · confectionery · moderate

Gummy candies are usually set with gelatin, which requires animal death, so this replica sets them with pectin instead: a plant gelling agent from fruit that forms a soft, chewy gel with sugar and acid. The gummies are molded as berries, colored and flavored with real fruit juice, and sweetened with honey. Pectin gummies are slightly less rubbery than gelatin gummies and a little more tender, which suits small fruit shapes. The pectin gelling is stated plainly on the record, because the method matters to the suite theme.

Original food being replicated

Gummy candies shaped as berries, here set with pectin instead of gelatin.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 300 ml fruit juice (raspberry or blackberry)
  • 100 g honey
  • 50 g granulated sugar
  • 15 g high-methoxyl pectin mixed with 30 g sugar
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • Berry-shaped silicone molds
  • Fine sugar for coating

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Heat the juice, honey, and sugar to a simmer, then whisk in the pectin-sugar mixture gradually so it does not clump.
  2. Boil gently, stirring constantly, until the mixture reaches 106 degrees C and thickens noticeably.
  3. Stir in the lemon juice, which helps the pectin set, and remove from the heat.
  4. Pour into berry molds, working quickly before the pectin begins to set, and leave at room temperature for four hours.
  5. Turn out the berries, roll lightly in fine sugar if desired, and let them dry for another hour before packing.
  6. Store in an airtight container; pectin gummies firm up further over the first day.

Food science: why it works

High-methoxyl pectin forms a gel when sugar draws water away from the pectin chains and acid reduces their charge, letting the chains bond into a network that traps juice. That is why pectin gummies need both enough sugar and enough acid to set, and why lemon juice is added at the end. Unlike gelatin, which sets on cooling and melts at body temperature, a pectin gel is heat-stable once set, so pectin gummies hold their shape in warm weather.

Safety

Boil carefully, since the mixture bubbles vigorously; stir constantly to prevent scorching. Honey is not for infants under 12 months.

Storage

Keep in an airtight container at cool room temperature for up to two weeks; do not refrigerate, which makes pectin gummies weep.

Variations

  • Use agar instead of pectin for a firmer, vegan jelly with a shorter set time.
  • Mold as citrus segments, apples, or grapes from different juices.
  • Coat in sour sugar (sugar mixed with a little citric acid) for sour berries.

Fudge and toffee are two ends of the same craft: fudge is cooked, cooled, and beaten into a soft, crystalline sweet, while toffee is cooked further until it snaps. This record makes both from honey-sweetened bases in one session: the fudge is poured into a square pan and cut into squares, and the toffee is poured thin onto a tray and broken into shards. Original food being replicated

Fudge and toffee, the classic cooked-sugar sweets, here made with honey and shaped as squares and shards.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • For fudge: 200 g honey, 200 g granulated sugar, 120 ml cream, 60 g butter, 1 teaspoon vanilla
  • For toffee: 200 g honey, 150 g granulated sugar, 100 g butter, 1 tablespoon water
  • Square pan lined with baking paper

  • Sugar thermometer

Method: molding and shaping

  1. For fudge, boil the honey, sugar, cream, and butter to 116 degrees C (soft ball stage), then cool undisturbed to 45 degrees C.

Honey's glucose and fructose interfere with crystal growth, so honey fudge stays smooth more easily than sugar-only fudge.

Honey Chocolate Seashells and Coins Rank 12 · complex · confectionery · advanced

Chocolate molding is a chocolatier's craft: tempered chocolate is poured into molds, where it sets with a snap and a shine. This replica sweetens the chocolate with honey instead of refined sugar alone, using a honey-sweetened chocolate base poured into seashell and coin molds. Tempering is the skill that matters: the chocolate must be melted, cooled, and warmed again so the cocoa butter forms stable crystals, which is what gives the finished shell its snap and gloss. The honey flavor is gentle behind the chocolate, and the shapes make the sweets look like treasures.

Original food being replicated

Molded chocolate, the filled and solid chocolate shapes of the chocolatier, here sweetened with honey.

Honey-based replica recipe: ingredients

  • 300 g honey-sweetened chocolate (dark or milk)
  • Seashell and coin polycarbonate molds, clean and dry
  • Optional: gold luster dust
  • Optional: honey caramel for filling the shells

Method: molding and shaping

  1. Melt two thirds of the chocolate over a bain-marie to 45 degrees C, then remove from the heat.
  2. Stir in the remaining chocolate a little at a time until the temperature falls to 27 degrees C, then warm gently back to 31 degrees C for dark chocolate (29 degrees C for milk).
  3. Pour the tempered chocolate into the molds, tap out air bubbles, and scrape the tops level.
  4. For filled shells, pour, empty, and chill to form a shell, fill with a little honey caramel, then cap with more chocolate.
  5. Chill until fully set, then turn out; properly tempered chocolate releases cleanly with a shine.
  6. Brush with gold luster dust if a treasure finish is wanted.

Food science: why it works

Tempering controls cocoa butter polymorphism: cocoa butter can crystallize in several forms, but only the Form V crystal gives chocolate its snap, shine, and clean melt, and the melt-cool-rewarm cycle favors Form V over the softer, duller forms. Honey-sweetened chocolate tempers the same way as sugar-sweetened chocolate, because the tempering acts on the cocoa butter, not on the sweetener. Water is the enemy of tempering, since even a drop makes chocolate seize into a grainy paste.

Safety

Keep all equipment completely dry; water makes chocolate seize. Contains milk in milk chocolate; check labels for nut traces. Honey is not for infants under 12 months.

Storage

Variations

  • Fill the coins with honey nougat instead of caramel.
  • Use white honey-sweetened chocolate tinted with color for pastel shells.
