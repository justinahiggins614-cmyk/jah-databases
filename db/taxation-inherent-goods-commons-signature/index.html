JAH-TIGCS-000001 — Signature Commons Trust Charter

A Signature commons trust charter declares a shared resource held for all members, names a steward, and fixes a distribution rule before any revenue arrives.

Signature design. The charter works in three clauses: the resource clause names what is held in common, the steward clause names who manages it and how they report, and the distribution clause states who receives what share of earnings. No clause may be changed without a public reading of the change and a waiting period, so members always know the rules they live under. The demo prints a charter for an example commons with its three clauses.

Use and limits. Use this charter as a template for any group holding a resource in common. Do not use it as a legal document, because it is a design template, not a filed charter.

Design detail. The charter's waiting period for changes is its most protective clause, because a trust is most vulnerable not when it is founded but when it is quietly amended. A proposed change must be read aloud at a public meeting, posted in writing for a fixed period, and then voted on, so that no member learns of a change only after it has taken effect. The steward's reports follow the same discipline: a quarterly statement of holdings, earnings, and distributions, written so that a member without financial training can check the arithmetic. A trust run this way can survive disagreements, because the rules for changing the rules are themselves trustworthy.

The demo's three bids show the mechanism working: the winner pays the second-highest bid, the losers pay nothing, and every bidder's best strategy is to state their true value, which is why the rule is used for teaching even where real auctions are more complex.

A Signature fee fairness test compares a fee against the cost of the service it funds and flags any fee that exceeds twice the service cost for review.

Signature design. The test's steps are to list the service, total its cost, state the fee, and compute the ratio, and any ratio above the threshold is printed as flagged rather than passed. The demo tests three example fees and prints their verdicts.

Use and limits. Use this test as a template for reviewing public fees. Do not use it as a legal finding, because courts apply their own standards to fees.

Design detail. The fee test's threshold of twice the service cost is deliberately simple, because a test that needs an accountant to run will not be run by the citizens it is meant to serve. A fee at or below cost passes, a fee modestly above cost passes with a note, and a fee above twice the cost is flagged for review, which means a public explanation rather than an automatic repeal. The flagged fee may still be justified, for example where a fee also funds enforcement or reserves, but the justification must be written and published, so that a flagged fee cannot survive on habit alone. The test's power is therefore procedural: it forces an explanation, not a verdict.

A Signature dividend distributor takes a fund's earnings, reserves a share for the fund's growth, and divides the rest equally among all members.

Signature design. The distributor's rule is equal shares and public arithmetic: every member can recompute their dividend from the published earnings and the published member count. The demo distributes example earnings among example members and prints each share.

Use and limits. Use this distributor as a template for commons dividend tools. Do not use it as a payment system, because real payments need real accounts and controls.

Design detail. The distributor's equal shares rule is stated before any earnings arrive, because a distribution rule chosen after the money appears will favor whoever is in the room. Equal shares also simplify the arithmetic to the point where any member can verify it: earnings minus reserve, divided by members, equals the dividend, and the distributor prints all three numbers. The reserve exists so that the fund can grow and can survive a poor year without cutting dividends to zero, and its size is itself a published rule rather than a discretionary holdback. A distributor run this way turns a windfall into an institution, which is the point of a commons trust.

JAH-TIGCS-000015 — Signature Wind Lease Board

A Signature wind lease board tracks a lease through its stages, area identification, environmental review, auction, construction plan, and operation, and prints the stage and its conditions at each step.

Signature design. The board's rule is that no stage may be skipped and no stage's conditions may be dropped at a later stage, so that a lease reaching operation still carries its earliest environmental conditions. The demo advances an example lease through its stages and prints the final condition list.

Use and limits. Use this board as a template for staged leasing tools. Do not use it as a real lease record, because real leases are issued by government agencies.

Design detail. The wind lease board's carried conditions are its memory: a bird survey required at the area stage still binds at the operation stage, a fishing notice promised at auction still binds after construction, and a decommissioning bond posted early still stands when the turbines age. Boards that drop early conditions are how projects lose public trust, because communities remember promises that developers forget. The board therefore prints the full condition list at every stage, and any proposal to drop a condition must be posted as a change with reasons, so that the project's history is visible to anyone who checks it.

JAH-TIGCS-000016 — Signature Identity Rights Card

A Signature identity rights card lists a person's protected attributes, the uses that need consent, and the income from licensed uses, keeping the attribute and the income in separate columns.

Signature design. The card's rule is the separation the public record explains: the attribute column is never taxed, and only the income column is taxable, so that a reader can see at once what is protected and what is charged. The demo prints a card for an example person with one licensed use and its income.

Use and limits. Use this card to teach the attribute-versus-income distinction. Do not use it as a legal document about any real person, because its example is invented.

Design detail. The identity card's two columns exist because the most common confusion about identity is the confusion between an attribute and its income. A name is not money, a signature is not money, and DNA is not money, but a licensed name can earn money, a signed endorsement can earn money, and a consented genetic study can be funded with money, and the tax law follows the money rather than the attribute. The card keeps the attribute column untaxed and the income column taxable, and it lists the consent that authorized each licensed use, so that a reader can see both what is protected and what was agreed, which is the whole of the identity question in one view.
