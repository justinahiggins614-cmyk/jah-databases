What Abstract Algebra Is

ID: JAH-ABAL-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-abstract-algebra#JAH-ABAL-000001 k=5f3345fe

Definition: Abstract algebra is the study of algebraic structures: sets with operations that obey rules, like groups, rings, and fields. Instead of studying numbers, it studies the patterns that numbers share with symmetries, permutations, and polynomials. A group is a set with one operation; a ring has two; a field has two with division. Abstract algebra is the mathematics of structure, and it is the language in which modern mathematics is written.

History: Abstract algebra began with the solving of equations: the quadratic formula is ancient, the cubic was solved in the 16th century, and the quintic resisted solution for 300 years. In the 1820s, Abel proved that the general quintic cannot be solved by radicals, and Galois, in 1832, explained why: the symmetries of the equation decide whether it can be solved. In the 19th century, Cayley defined the group in 1854, Dedekind defined the ring and the ideal, and the subject became abstract: it studied structures, not numbers. In the 20th century, abstract algebra became the foundation of mathematics, and it is now used in cryptography, coding theory, and physics.

Key concepts: The key ideas are group, ring, field, operation, structure. A group is a set with one operation that is associative, has an identity, and has inverses. The integers under addition are a group; the symmetries of a square are a group. A ring is a set with two operations, addition and multiplication, that obey the usual rules. The integers are a ring; the polynomials are a ring. A field is a ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse: division is possible. The rational numbers are a field; the integers are not. An operation combines two elements to give a third: addition combines two numbers to give their sum. An operation must be closed: the result must be in the set. A structure is a set with operations and rules. Abstract algebra studies structures: what they have in common, and how they differ. Worked examples: The integers under addition form a group: the sum of two integers is an integer, addition is associative, 0 is the identity, and every integer has an inverse, its negative. The integers under multiplication do not form a group: 0 has no inverse, and 2 has no inverse in the integers. For understanding structure: the theorems of abstract algebra apply to every example, from numbers to symmetries to codes.

Binary Operations

ID: JAH-ABAL-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-abstract-algebra#JAH-ABAL-000002 k=9cb4a1d0

Definition: A binary operation combines two elements of a set to give a third element of the same set: addition combines two integers to give an integer, and multiplication combines two rationals to give a rational. The operation must be closed: the result must be in the set. The properties of an operation — associativity, commutativity, identity, inverses — decide what kind of structure the set is.

History: Binary operations are as old as arithmetic: addition and multiplication are the first operations, and they were studied by the Greeks. In the 20th century, the operation became the foundation of algebra: a structure is a set with operations, and the theorems are about the operations. Today the binary operation is the first definition of abstract algebra, and it appears in every structure.

Groups

Subgroups

ID: JAH-ABAL-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-abstract-algebra#JAH-ABAL-000004 k=4fb3d7eb

Definition: A subgroup is a subset of a group that is itself a group, under the same operation: it contains the identity, it is closed under the operation, and it contains the inverse of every element. The even integers are a subgroup of the integers under addition; the rotations are a subgroup of the symmetries of a square. Subgroups are the parts of a group, and they are the first step in understanding its structure.

History: Subgroups were implicit in the work of Galois in 1832, who studied the subgroups of the group of an equation, and they were made explicit by Cayley in 1854. In the 19th century, Lagrange proved that the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group, and Sylow proved his theorems about the subgroups of prime-power order in 1872. In the 20th century, the subgroup became the central tool of group theory: the lattice of subgroups describes the structure of a group, and the classification of finite simple groups is the classification of groups with no non-trivial subgroups. Today subgroups are used in cryptography, in physics, and in the theory of equations.

It is the order of the group divided by the order of the subgroup. A coset is a translate of a subgroup: the set gH, for a subgroup H and an element g. The cosets partition the group. Cyclic Groups

ID: JAH-ABAL-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-abstract-algebra#JAH-ABAL-000005 k=114dedd6

Definition: A cyclic group is a group generated by a single element: every element is a power of the generator. The integers under addition are cyclic, generated by 1; the rotations of a square are cyclic, generated by a 90-degree rotation; and the group of order n is cyclic exactly when it has an element of order n. Cyclic groups are the simplest groups, and they are completely understood: every cyclic group is either the integers or the integers modulo n.

In the 20th century, cyclic groups became the foundation of cryptography: the Diffie-Hellman key exchange works in a cyclic group, and the discrete logarithm problem is the problem of finding the exponent. Today cyclic groups are used in cryptography, in coding theory, and in the theory of numbers.

Permutation Groups

ID: JAH-ABAL-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-abstract-algebra#JAH-ABAL-000006 k=196bffae

Definition: A permutation is a rearrangement of a set: a bijection from the set to itself. The permutations of n objects form a group, the symmetric group S_n, with composition as the operation. It has n! elements. Every group is a subgroup of a symmetric group, by Cayley's theorem, so permutations are the universal example of a group. Permutation groups are the groups of the theory of equations, and they are the groups of symmetry.

History: Permutation groups were studied by Lagrange in 1770, in his work on the roots of equations, and by Ruffini in 1799, who used them to prove that the quintic cannot be solved by radicals — though his proof had a gap. Galois, in 1832, made the permutation group the central object of the theory of equations: the Galois group of an equation is a permutation group, and it decides whether the equation can be solved. Cayley proved in 1854 that every group is a subgroup of a symmetric group. Today permutation groups are used in cryptography, in the theory of equations, and in combinatorics.

Key concepts: The key ideas are permutation, symmetric group, cycle, transposition, Cayley's theorem. A permutation is a rearrangement: a bijection from a set to itself. The permutation (1 2 3) -> (2 3 1) is a cycle of length 3. The symmetric group S_n is the group of all permutations of n objects, with composition. It has n! elements. Definition: A field is a commutative ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse: division is possible, except by zero. The rational numbers are a field; the real numbers are a field; and the complex numbers are a field. The finite fields, with p^n elements for a prime p, are the fields of cryptography and coding theory. Fields are the setting of the theory of equations, and they are the most familiar structure in algebra.

History: Fields were implicit in the work of Gauss in 1801, on the rational numbers, and they were defined by Dedekind in the 1870s, in his work on algebraic number theory. Galois, in 1832, studied the finite fields, which are now called Galois fields. In the 20th century, the field became the central object of the theory of equations: the Galois group of an equation is the group of automorphisms of its splitting field. Today fields are used in cryptography, where the finite fields protect data, and in coding theory, where they build error-correcting codes.

Definition: An ideal is a subset of a ring that is a subgroup under addition and absorbs multiplication: if a is in the ideal and r is in the ring, then r a is in the ideal. History: Ideals were introduced by Dedekind in the 1870s, in his work on algebraic number theory, where he used them to restore unique factorisation. Today ideals are used in number theory, in geometry, and in coding theory.
