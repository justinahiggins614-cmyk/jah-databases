What Abstract Algebra Is

Abstract algebra is the study of algebraic structures: sets with operations that obey rules, like groups, rings, and fields. Instead of studying numbers, it studies the patterns that numbers share with symmetries, permutations, and polynomials. A group is a set with one operation; a ring has two; a field has two with division. Abstract algebra is the mathematics of structure, and it is the language in which modern mathematics is written.

The integers under addition form a group: the sum of two integers is an integer, addition is associative, 0 is the identity, and every integer has an inverse, its negative. The integers under multiplication do not form a group: 0 has no inverse, and 2 has no inverse in the integers. The rational numbers under addition and multiplication form a field: every non-zero rational has an inverse. The symmetries of a square form a group of 8 elements: 4 rotations and 4 reflections. The set {0, 1} under addition modulo 2 is a group of 2 elements: 1 + 1 = 0.

Binary Operations

A binary operation combines two elements of a set to give a third element of the same set: addition combines two integers to give an integer, and multiplication combines two rationals to give a rational. The operation must be closed: the result must be in the set. The properties of an operation — associativity, commutativity, identity, inverses — decide what kind of structure the set is.

Addition on the integers is a binary operation: it is closed, associative, and commutative, with identity 0, and every integer has an inverse, its negative. Subtraction on the integers is closed, but it is not associative: (5 - 3) - 2 = 0, while 5 - (3 - 2) = 4. Multiplication on the integers is closed, associative, and commutative, with identity 1, but only 1 and -1 have inverses. Division on the non-zero rationals is closed, but it is not associative: (8/4)/2 = 1, while 8/(4/2) = 4. The operation of taking the maximum on the integers is closed, associative, and commutative, with identity the smallest integer — but there is no smallest integer, so there is no identity.

Groups

A group is a set with one binary operation that is associative, has an identity element, and has an inverse for every element. The integers under addition are a group; the non-zero rationals under multiplication are a group; the symmetries of a square are a group; and the permutations of n objects are a group. Groups are the mathematics of symmetry, and they are the first structure of abstract algebra.

The integers under addition form a group of infinite order: the operation is closed, associative, with identity 0, and the inverse of n is -n. The non-zero rational numbers under multiplication form a group: the identity is 1, and the inverse of q is 1/q. The symmetries of a square form a group of order 8: 4 rotations (0, 90, 180, 270 degrees) and 4 reflections. The permutations of 3 objects form a group of order 6: there are 3! = 6 permutations. The set {1, -1} under multiplication is a group of order 2: 1 is the identity, and each element is its own inverse.

Subgroups

A subgroup is a subset of a group that is itself a group, under the same operation: it contains the identity, it is closed under the operation, and it contains the inverse of every element. The even integers are a subgroup of the integers under addition; the rotations are a subgroup of the symmetries of a square. Subgroups are the parts of a group, and they are the first step in understanding its structure.

The even integers are a subgroup of the integers under addition: 0 is even, the sum of two even integers is even, and the negative of an even integer is even. The rotations are a subgroup of the symmetries of a square: the identity rotation is a rotation, the composition of two rotations is a rotation, and the inverse of a rotation is a rotation. The set {1, -1} is a subgroup of the non-zero rationals under multiplication. The subgroup generated by 2 in the integers under addition is the even integers: the powers of 2 under addition are the multiples of 2. The group of order 6 has subgroups of orders 1, 2, 3, and 6, by Lagrange's theorem: the order of a subgroup divides 6.

Cyclic Groups

A cyclic group is a group generated by a single element: every element is a power of the generator. The integers under addition are cyclic, generated by 1; the rotations of a square are cyclic, generated by a 90-degree rotation; and the group of order n is cyclic exactly when it has an element of order n. Cyclic groups are the simplest groups, and they are completely understood: every cyclic group is either the integers or the integers modulo n.

The integers under addition are cyclic, generated by 1: every integer is a multiple of 1. The group Z/6Z, the integers modulo 6, is cyclic, generated by 1: the powers of 1 under addition are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The rotations of a square form a cyclic group of order 4, generated by a 90-degree rotation: its powers are 0, 90, 180, and 270 degrees. The roots of unity of order 4 are 1, i, -1, -i: they form a cyclic group generated by i, because i^2 = -1, i^3 = -i, and i^4 = 1. The group Z/6Z has generators 1 and 5: 5 is a generator because 5 is coprime to 6, and its powers are 0, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1.

Permutation Groups

A permutation is a rearrangement of a set: a bijection from the set to itself. The permutations of n objects form a group, the symmetric group S_n, with composition as the operation. It has n! elements. Every group is a subgroup of a symmetric group, by Cayley's theorem, so permutations are the universal example of a group. Permutation groups are the groups of the theory of equations, and they are the groups of symmetry.

The symmetric group S_3 has 3! = 6 elements: the identity, three transpositions, and two 3-cycles. The permutation (1 2 3) has order 3, because applying it three times gives the identity. The permutation (1 2)(3 4) in S_4 has order 2, the least common multiple of 2 and 2. The transposition (1 2) and the 3-cycle (1 2 3) generate S_3: every permutation of 3 objects is a product of them. The sign of a permutation is +1 if it is a product of an even number of transpositions, and -1 if odd: the 3-cycle (1 2 3) is the product of two transpositions, so its sign is +1, and it is even.

Lagrange's Theorem

Lagrange's theorem says that the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group: if H is a subgroup of a finite group G, then |H| divides |G|, and the index of H is |G|/|H|. The proof is that the cosets of H partition G, and each coset has the same size as H. Lagrange's theorem is the first theorem of group theory, and it is the tool that limits the possible subgroups.

A group of order 6 has subgroups of orders 1, 2, 3, and 6, because those are the divisors of 6: it cannot have a subgroup of order 4 or 5. A group of order 7, which is prime, is cyclic: it has no non-trivial subgroups, so any non-identity element generates it. In the group Z/12Z, the element 4 has order 3, because 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 = 0 modulo 12, and 3 divides 12. The subgroup generated by 4 is {0, 4, 8}, of order 3, and its index is 12/3 = 4: there are 4 cosets. The order of an element of a group of order 10 must divide 10, so it is 1, 2, 5, or 10.

Homomorphisms and Isomorphisms

A homomorphism is a map between groups that preserves the operation: f(a * b) = f(a) * f(b). An isomorphism is a homomorphism that is bijective: it is a relabelling, and the two groups are the same in structure, even if their elements are different. The kernel of a homomorphism is the set of elements that map to the identity, and the image is the set of outputs. Homomorphisms are the maps of group theory, and isomorphisms are the sameness.

The map f(x) = 2x from the integers under addition to the even integers under addition is an isomorphism: it is bijective, and f(a + b) = 2(a + b) = 2a + 2b = f(a) + f(b). The map from the integers under addition to Z/6Z, sending n to n modulo 6, is a homomorphism: its kernel is the multiples of 6, and its image is all of Z/6Z. The logarithm is a homomorphism from the positive reals under multiplication to the reals under addition: log(ab) = log(a) + log(b). The determinant is a homomorphism from the invertible matrices under multiplication to the non-zero reals under multiplication: det(AB) = det(A) det(B). The sign map from S_n to {+1, -1} is a homomorphism: its kernel is the alternating group A_n.

Normal Subgroups and Quotient Groups

A normal subgroup is a subgroup that is invariant under conjugation: for every g in the group and every h in the subgroup, g h g^-1 is in the subgroup. The quotient group G/N is the group of cosets of a normal subgroup N, with the operation defined by (aN)(bN) = (ab)N. Quotient groups are the way to divide a group: they are the groups that a homomorphism can produce, by the first isomorphism theorem.

The subgroup of even integers is normal in the integers under addition, because the group is abelian, and every subgroup of an abelian group is normal. The quotient Z/2Z is the group of cosets: the even integers and the odd integers, with the operation that even + even = even, even + odd = odd, and odd + odd = even. The alternating group A_n is normal in the symmetric group S_n: its index is 2, and a subgroup of index 2 is always normal. The quotient S_n/A_n is the group {+1, -1}, of order 2: it is the sign map. The centre of a group, the set of elements that commute with everything, is always normal.

The Isomorphism Theorems

The isomorphism theorems are the three theorems that describe homomorphisms and quotients: the first says that the image of a homomorphism is isomorphic to the group modulo its kernel; the second says that the product of a subgroup and a normal subgroup is a subgroup, and its quotient is the quotient of the subgroup; and the third says that the subgroups of a quotient correspond to the subgroups of the group that contain the kernel. They are the most used theorems in algebra.

The map from Z to Z/6Z, sending n to n modulo 6, has kernel 6Z and image Z/6Z, so by the first isomorphism theorem, Z/6Z is isomorphic to Z/6Z — the theorem is trivial here, but it is the pattern. The map from the integers under addition to the group {+1, -1}, sending even numbers to +1 and odd numbers to -1, has kernel the even integers, so Z/2Z is isomorphic to {+1, -1}. The second isomorphism theorem says that for the subgroup H of the rotations and the normal subgroup N of the identity in the symmetries of a square, HN/N is isomorphic to H. The third isomorphism theorem says that (Z/12Z)/(6Z/12Z) is isomorphic to Z/6Z: the quotient of a quotient is a quotient.

Rings

A ring is a set with two operations, addition and multiplication, that obey the usual rules: addition is a commutative group, multiplication is associative, and multiplication distributes over addition. The integers are a ring; the polynomials are a ring; and the matrices are a ring. Rings are the second structure of abstract algebra, after groups, and they are the setting of number theory and of the theory of equations.

The integers form a ring: addition is a commutative group, multiplication is associative, and multiplication distributes over addition. The units are 1 and -1, and there are no zero divisors. The polynomials with real coefficients form a ring: the units are the non-zero constants, and there are no zero divisors. The 2-by-2 matrices form a ring: addition is a commutative group, multiplication is associative, but it is not commutative, and there are zero divisors: [[1, 0], [0, 0]] times [[0, 0], [0, 1]] is the zero matrix. Z/6Z is a ring with zero divisors: 2 times 3 = 6 = 0 modulo 6.

Fields

A field is a commutative ring where every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse: division is possible, except by zero. The rational numbers are a field; the real numbers are a field; and the complex numbers are a field. The finite fields, with p^n elements for a prime p, are the fields of cryptography and coding theory. Fields are the setting of the theory of equations, and they are the most familiar structure in algebra.

The rational numbers form a field: every non-zero rational a/b has the inverse b/a. The integers do not form a field: 2 has no inverse in the integers. The field with 2 elements, F_2, is {0, 1} with 1 + 1 = 0: it is the field of bits, and it is used in coding theory. The field with 3 elements, F_3, is {0, 1, 2} with arithmetic modulo 3: 2 + 2 = 1, and 2 times 2 = 1. The complex numbers form a field: every non-zero complex number a + bi has the inverse (a - bi)/(a^2 + b^2). The real numbers are a field of characteristic 0; F_2 has characteristic 2.

Integral Domains

An integral domain is a commutative ring with no zero divisors: the product of two non-zero elements is never zero. The integers are an integral domain; the polynomials over a field are an integral domain; and Z/6Z is not, because 2 times 3 = 0 modulo 6. Integral domains are the rings where cancellation works: if a is non-zero and ab = ac, then b = c. They are the rings that can be embedded in a field of fractions.

The integers are an integral domain: the product of two non-zero integers is never zero, and cancellation holds. Z/6Z is not an integral domain: 2 times 3 = 0 modulo 6, so 2 and 3 are zero divisors, and cancellation fails: 2 times 1 = 2 and 2 times 4 = 8 = 2 modulo 6, but 1 is not 4. The polynomials over the reals are an integral domain: the product of two non-zero polynomials is non-zero. The field of fractions of the integers is the rationals: every rational is a fraction of integers. The ring Z[sqrt(-5)] is an integral domain but not a unique factorisation domain: 6 = 2 times 3 = (1 + sqrt(-5))(1 - sqrt(-5)), two different factorisations.

Ideals and Quotient Rings

An ideal is a subset of a ring that is a subgroup under addition and absorbs multiplication: if a is in the ideal and r is in the ring, then r a is in the ideal. The quotient ring R/I is the ring of cosets of an ideal I, with the operations defined on cosets. Ideals are the subgroups of ring theory: they are the kernels of ring homomorphisms, and the quotient rings are the images. The ideal is the most important concept in ring theory.

The even integers form an ideal in the integers: the sum of two even integers is even, and the product of any integer with an even integer is even. The quotient Z/2Z is the ring {0, 1} with 1 + 1 = 0: it is the field with 2 elements. The multiples of 6 form an ideal in the integers, and the quotient is Z/6Z, the ring of integers modulo 6. The ideal generated by x in the polynomial ring is the set of polynomials with zero constant term. The quotient of the integers by the ideal of multiples of 5 is Z/5Z, which is a field, because 5 is prime and the ideal is maximal.

Polynomial Rings

A polynomial ring is the ring of polynomials in one or more variables, with coefficients in a ring or a field: the polynomials with real coefficients form a ring, and the polynomials with coefficients in a field form an integral domain. Polynomial rings are the setting of the theory of equations: a polynomial equation is an equation in a polynomial ring, and its roots are the solutions. The polynomial ring is the most important ring after the integers.

The polynomial x^2 - 1 has degree 2 and roots 1 and -1: it factors as (x - 1)(x + 1). The polynomial x^2 + 1 has no real roots, and it is irreducible over the reals: it factors over the complex numbers as (x - i)(x + i). The polynomial x^3 - 2 has one real root, the cube root of 2, about 1.26, and two complex roots. The quotient of the real polynomials by the ideal generated by x^2 + 1 is the complex numbers: x becomes i, and the quotient is a field. A polynomial of degree n has at most n roots, by the factor theorem: each root gives a linear factor.

Field Extensions

A field extension is a field containing another field: the complex numbers are an extension of the reals, of degree 2, because i satisfies x^2 + 1 = 0. The degree of an extension is the dimension of the larger field as a vector space over the smaller. Field extensions are the setting of Galois theory: the Galois group of an extension is the group of automorphisms, and it decides whether an equation can be solved by radicals.

The complex numbers are an extension of the reals of degree 2: every complex number is a + bi, with a and b real, so {1, i} is a basis. The field Q(sqrt(2)) is an extension of Q of degree 2: every element is a + b sqrt(2), with a and b rational. The tower law says that the degree of Q(sqrt(2), sqrt(3)) over Q is 4: it is the product of the degrees of Q(sqrt(2)) over Q and Q(sqrt(2), sqrt(3)) over Q(sqrt(2)), which are 2 and 2. The splitting field of x^2 - 2 over Q is Q(sqrt(2)): it is the smallest field containing both roots, sqrt(2) and -sqrt(2). The minimal polynomial of sqrt(2) over Q is x^2 - 2.

Galois Theory

Galois theory is the theory of the symmetries of a field extension: the Galois group is the group of automorphisms of the extension, and it decides whether a polynomial equation can be solved by radicals. The fundamental theorem of Galois theory says that the subgroups of the Galois group correspond to the intermediate fields, and the equation is solvable by radicals exactly when the Galois group is solvable. Galois theory is the culmination of abstract algebra.

The Galois group of x^2 - 2 over Q is the group of order 2: the automorphisms are the identity and the map sending sqrt(2) to -sqrt(2). The group is abelian, so it is solvable, and the equation is solvable by radicals: the solutions are plus and minus sqrt(2). The Galois group of the general quintic is S_5, the symmetric group on 5 letters, which is not solvable: that is why the quintic cannot be solved by radicals. The Galois group of x^2 + 1 over Q is of order 2: the automorphisms are the identity and complex conjugation. The fundamental theorem says that the subgroups of the Galois group of x^2 - 2 correspond to the intermediate fields: the group has subgroups of orders 1 and 2, corresponding to Q(sqrt(2)) and Q.

Symmetry Groups

A symmetry group is the group of symmetries of an object: the rotations and reflections that leave it unchanged. The symmetry group of a square has 8 elements; of a cube, 24 rotations; and of a circle, infinitely many rotations and reflections. Symmetry groups are the groups of geometry, chemistry, and physics: they describe what is unchanged, and they are the most concrete groups in mathematics.

The symmetry group of a square is the dihedral group D_4, of order 8: 4 rotations (0, 90, 180, 270 degrees) and 4 reflections (across the two diagonals and the two midlines). The symmetry group of an equilateral triangle is D_3, of order 6: 3 rotations and 3 reflections. The rotation group of a cube has 24 elements: the identity, 9 rotations about face axes, 8 about body diagonals, and 6 about edge axes. The symmetry group of a circle is infinite: every rotation and every reflection is a symmetry. The 17 wallpaper groups classify the symmetries of a repeating pattern in the plane.

Group Actions

A group action is a way for a group to act on a set: each element of the group permutes the set, and the action respects the group operation. The symmetries of a square act on its vertices; the rotations of a cube act on its faces; and a group acts on itself by multiplication. Group actions are the way groups are used: they turn an abstract group into a concrete set of permutations, and they are the tool for counting with symmetry.

The symmetries of a square, of order 8, act on its 4 vertices: the orbit of a vertex is all 4 vertices, and the stabiliser of a vertex has order 2, because 4 times 2 = 8, by the orbit-stabiliser theorem. Burnside's lemma counts the colourings of a square's vertices with 2 colours, up to symmetry: the identity fixes 16 colourings, the 180-degree rotation fixes 4, the 90-degree rotations fix 2 each, and the reflections fix 4 or 8, and the average is (16 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 4 + 4 + 8 + 8)/8 = 6, so there are 6 colourings up to symmetry. The group of order 6 acts on itself by multiplication: the orbit of the identity is the whole group, and the stabiliser is trivial.

The Sylow Theorems

The Sylow theorems describe the subgroups of prime-power order in a finite group: the first says that a group of order p^n m has a subgroup of order p^n, a Sylow p-subgroup; the second says that all Sylow p-subgroups are conjugate; and the third says that the number of Sylow p-subgroups divides m and is 1 modulo p. The Sylow theorems are the main tool for classifying finite groups, and they are the deepest theorems of elementary group theory.

A group of order 6 = 2 times 3 has Sylow 2-subgroups of order 2 and Sylow 3-subgroups of order 3: the number of Sylow 3-subgroups divides 2 and is 1 modulo 3, so it is 1: there is exactly one Sylow 3-subgroup, and it is normal. A group of order 12 = 2^2 times 3 has Sylow 2-subgroups of order 4: their number divides 3 and is 1 modulo 2, so it is 1 or 3. A group of order 15 = 3 times 5 has a unique Sylow 3-subgroup and a unique Sylow 5-subgroup, both normal, so the group is cyclic: every group of order 15 is cyclic. A group of order 7 is prime, so it is cyclic, by Lagrange's theorem.

Abstract Algebra in Cryptography

Abstract algebra in cryptography is the mathematics of secrecy: the groups, rings, and fields that encrypt, authenticate, and agree on keys. RSA is the arithmetic of the ring Z/nZ; Diffie-Hellman is the discrete logarithm in a cyclic group; and elliptic-curve cryptography is the group of points on an elliptic curve. The security of a cipher is an algebraic claim: breaking it would require solving a hard problem in the structure.

RSA with p = 3 and q = 11 has n = 33 and phi = 20: with e = 3 and d = 7, encrypting m = 2 gives 2^3 = 8 modulo 33, and decrypting gives 8^7 = 2,097,152, which is 2 modulo 33. Diffie-Hellman with prime 23 and base 5: Alice chooses 6, sends 5^6 = 15,625, which is 8 modulo 23; Bob chooses 15, sends 5^15, which is 19 modulo 23; the shared secret is 8^15 = 19^6 = 2 modulo 23. An elliptic curve over F_7, y^2 = x^3 + 2x + 3, has points that form a group: the group law is the chord-and-tangent rule. A 256-bit key has 2^256 possible values, about 1.16 x 10^77, which is too many to try one by one.

Abstract Algebra in Geometry

Abstract algebra in geometry is the mathematics of shapes through their equations: the ring of polynomials on a variety describes the variety, and the ideal of the variety is the set of polynomials that vanish on it. Algebraic geometry is the study of the correspondence between geometry and algebra: the points are the maximal ideals, and the functions are the elements of the ring. Geometry is algebra, and algebra is geometry.

The circle is the variety of x^2 + y^2 - 1 = 0: the points (x, y) with x^2 + y^2 = 1. Its ideal is generated by x^2 + y^2 - 1, and its coordinate ring is the ring of polynomials in x and y, modulo that polynomial. The parabola y = x^2 is the variety of y - x^2 = 0: its ideal is generated by y - x^2. The point (1, 2) is the variety of x - 1 = 0 and y - 2 = 0: its ideal is the maximal ideal (x - 1, y - 2). The Nullstellensatz says that the ideal of the variety of an ideal I is the radical of I: the geometry determines the algebra, up to radicals.

A History of Abstract Algebra

Abstract algebra has no single founder: it is the coming together of the theory of equations, the theory of numbers, and the theory of symmetry. The solving of equations is ancient, the group is from the 19th century, and the ring and the field are from the 1870s. The subject took its modern form in the 1920s, when Noether made the structure the central object, and it is now the language in which modern mathematics is written.

The timeline: the cubic solved, 1545; Abel's proof, 1824; Galois's theory, 1832; Cayley's group, 1854; Dedekind's rings, 1870s; Noether's algebra, 1920s; the classification completed, 2004. The span from the cubic to the classification is about 460 years. The subject's parts are old, but the subject itself is new: it was the 20th century that brought equations, numbers, and symmetry together into one subject, and it is the computer that keeps it growing, through cryptography and coding theory.

Abstract Algebra in the Modern World

Abstract algebra in the modern world is everywhere: the secure connection, the error-correcting code, and the particle are all abstract algebra. The modern world runs on groups, rings, and fields: the encryption is a group, the code is an ideal, and the particle is a representation. Abstract algebra is the invisible infrastructure of modern life, and it is the language in which modern mathematics is written.

A secure connection begins with a key agreement: Alice and Bob agree on a shared secret by Diffie-Hellman, in a group, and then they encrypt the data with AES, in the finite field with 256 elements. An error-correcting code protects a CD from scratches: the code is a Reed-Solomon code, an ideal in a polynomial ring, and it can correct up to half the redundancy in errors. A particle is a representation of the symmetry group of spacetime: the electron is a representation of the Lorentz group, and its spin is the representation's label. A blockchain links each block to the previous one by a hash: changing a block would require recomputing all the hashes after it, which is infeasible.
