What Discrete Mathematics Is

ID: JAH-DSCMS-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-discrete-mathematics-signature#JAH-DSCMS-000001 k=cc2e9ff0

Definition: Discrete mathematics is the mathematics of separate, countable things: integers, graphs, sets, and logical statements, rather than the continuous quantities of calculus. It is the mathematics of computers: algorithms, data structures, cryptography, and networks are all discrete. Where calculus studies change, discrete mathematics studies structure, counting, and logic. It is the mathematics of the finite: what can be counted, listed, and decided in a finite number of steps.

History: Discrete mathematics is as old as counting, but it became a subject in its own right with the rise of computers in the 20th century. Logic goes back to Aristotle and Boole; combinatorics to Pascal and Euler; graph theory to Euler's bridges of 1736. In the 1930s, Turing and Church founded computability theory, and in the 1940s and 1950s, Shannon founded information theory and computer science made discrete mathematics essential. The first textbooks called discrete mathematics appeared in the 1970s, and today it is the first mathematics course for computer science students everywhere, covering the logic, sets, counting, and graphs that computing is built on.

Key concepts: The key ideas are discrete, graph, algorithm, combinatorics, logic. Discrete means separate and countable: the integers are discrete, while the real numbers are continuous. Discrete mathematics studies the countable. A computer stores everything as discrete bits: a file is a finite sequence of 0s and 1s, and a program is a finite sequence of instructions. A graph is a set of vertices joined by edges. It models networks, maps, and relationships of every kind. An algorithm is a finite sequence of steps that solves a problem. Discrete mathematics studies which algorithms exist and how fast they are. Combinatorics is the mathematics of counting: how many ways things can be arranged, chosen, or combined. Logic is the study of valid reasoning: propositions, connectives, and proofs. It is the foundation of computing. Logic and Propositions

ID: JAH-DSCMS-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-discrete-mathematics-signature#JAH-DSCMS-000002 k=f6665a2c

Definition: A proposition is a statement that is either true or false, like 2 + 2 = 4 or Paris is the capital of France. Logic studies how propositions combine with and, or, not, if-then, and if-and-only-if, and which arguments are valid. A truth table lists every combination of truth values and the result. Logic is the foundation of mathematics and of computing: every program is logic made executable.

History: Logic began with Aristotle's syllogisms in the 4th century BCE, the first systematic study of valid argument. It was revived by the Stoics, developed by medieval logicians, and transformed by George Boole in 1854, who showed that logic is algebra: propositions can be added and multiplied like numbers. Frege's Begriffsschrift of 1879 introduced quantifiers, and Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica of 1910 tried to derive all of mathematics from logic. In the 1930s, Gödel and Turing showed the limits of logic, and in the 1940s, Shannon showed that Boolean logic describes electrical circuits, founding digital computing.

Key concepts: The key ideas are proposition, truth table, conjunction, disjunction, implication. A proposition is a statement that is true or false, but not both. 2 + 2 = 4 is true; 2 + 2 = 5 is false; What time is it? is not a proposition. A truth table lists every combination of truth values for the variables and the resulting value of a compound proposition. A conjunction is p and q: true exactly when both p and q are true. Its symbol is ∧. A disjunction is p or q: true when at least one of p and q is true. Its symbol is ∨. An implication is p implies q, written p → q: false only when p is true and q is false. Its contrapositive is not q implies not p. Together these ideas give the vocabulary the rest of the record uses: every later section returns to them.

Fourth, check for tautologies (always true), contradictions (always false), and contingencies. Fifth, use the table to test whether an argument is valid: the conclusion must be true in every row where the premises are true. Worked examples: The proposition p and q, with p true and q false, is false, because a conjunction needs both parts true. The implication p → q with p true and q false is false; in the other three rows it is true. De Morgan's law says not (p and q) is equivalent to (not p) or (not q): if p is true and q is false, then not (p and q) is true, and (not p) or (not q) is false or true, which is true. Real applications: Digital circuits are logic: every AND, OR, and NOT gate is a logical connective, and a computer is a machine that evaluates truth tables at enormous speed. Databases query with logic: a search for documents matching A and B, or C, is a logical proposition evaluated over the database. Mathematics is written in logic: every proof is a chain of implications, and checking a proof means checking its logic. Law and philosophy argue with logic: the validity of an argument is a matter of its form, not of its content.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is p → q the same as q → p? No: the converse is different. p → q says if p then q; q → p says if q then p. They are equivalent only when p and q are equivalent. What is a tautology? A proposition that is true in every row of its truth table, like p or not p. Tautologies are the laws of logic. Why is a false statement implying anything true? By convention: an implication is false only when the antecedent is true and the consequent false. A false antecedent makes the implication vacuously true. Can a proposition be neither true nor false? In classical logic, no. Some logics allow a third value, but classical logic — the logic of mathematics and computing — is two-valued.

Sets and Relations

ID: JAH-DSCMS-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-discrete-mathematics-signature#JAH-DSCMS-000003 k=ded80bb4

Definition: A set is a collection of distinct objects, called its elements: the set {1, 2, 3} has three elements. A relation is a connection between elements of sets: less than relates numbers, and is-a-friend-of relates people. Relations can be pictured as arrows, as tables, or as matrices, and their properties — reflexive, symmetric, transitive — decide what kind of structure they are.

History: Set theory was founded by Georg Cantor in the 1870s, who showed that infinite sets come in different sizes and that the real numbers are uncountable. Bertrand Russell's paradox of 1901 showed that naive set theory was inconsistent, and Zermelo and Fraenkel axiomatised it in the 1900s and 1920s. Relations were studied by De Morgan and Peirce in the 19th century, and by Schröder, who saw that relations are the general form of mathematical structure. In the 20th century, sets and relations became the language of all of mathematics, and the foundation of databases, where a table is a relation and a query is an operation on relations.

Key concepts: The key ideas are set, relation, reflexive, symmetric, transitive. A set is a collection of distinct objects. {1, 2, 3} is a set; {1, 1, 2} is not, because elements are distinct. A relation is a set of ordered pairs: a relation from A to B is a subset of A x B. Less than is a relation on the numbers. A relation is reflexive when every element relates to itself. Equality is reflexive; less than is not. A relation is symmetric when a related to b implies b related to a. Equality is symmetric; less than is not. A relation is transitive when a related to b and b related to c imply a related to c. Less than and equality are transitive. Worked examples: The set {1, 2, 3} has 3 elements and 2^3 = 8 subsets: the empty set, {1}, {2}, {3}, {1, 2}, {1, 3}, {2, 3}, and {1, 2, 3}. The relation is-a-friend-of is usually symmetric but not transitive: your friend's friend may not be your friend. Real applications: Databases are relations: a table is a set of rows, and a query joins relations, which is why the theory is called relational. Scheduling uses relations: a precedence relation says which tasks must come before which, and its transitive closure gives the full order.

Common questions and misconceptions: What is the empty set? The set with no elements, written ∅. It is a subset of every set, and it has 0 elements and 1 subset: itself. Is a relation a function? Not always: a function is a relation where each input has exactly one output. A relation can have many outputs per input, or none. What is an equivalence relation for? It partitions a set into classes of equivalent things: congruence modulo n partitions the integers into n classes, and equality partitions any set into singletons. Can a relation be both symmetric and antisymmetric? Yes: equality is both. A relation is antisymmetric when a related to b and b related to a imply a = b, and equality is the only relation that is both symmetric and antisymmetric on a set with more than one element.

Functions

ID: JAH-DSCMS-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-discrete-mathematics-signature#JAH-DSCMS-000004 k=3d7f844e

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a mapping ladder from a rule to an inverse, with 2x + 1 as the running example.

Definition: A function is a rule that assigns to each input exactly one output: f(x) = x^2 assigns to 3 the output 9. Functions are the central object of mathematics: they model change, transformation, and computation. A function is injective when different inputs give different outputs, surjective when every output is reached, and bijective when it is both — in which case it has an inverse.

History: Functions were implicit in the earliest tables of squares and cubes, but the modern definition — a set of ordered pairs with exactly one output per input — dates to the 19th century, from Dirichlet and Dedekind. The words injective, surjective, and bijective were introduced by Bourbaki in the 20th century. In the 1930s, Church and Turing defined computable functions, founding computer science: a program is a function from inputs to outputs. In the 20th century, functions became the language of all of mathematics, and category theory, founded in the 1940s, studies mathematics through functions and their composition.

Key concepts: The key ideas are function, domain, codomain, injective, bijective. A function assigns to each input exactly one output. f(x) = x^2 is a function; the relation x^2 + y^2 = 1 is not, because one x can give two y values. The domain is the set of allowed inputs. The domain of f(x) = 1/x is all real numbers except 0. The codomain is the set where outputs live. The range is the set of outputs actually reached, which may be smaller. A function is injective when different inputs give different outputs: f(x) = 2x is injective, and f(x) = x^2 is not, because f(-1) = f(1). A function is bijective when it is both injective and surjective: it pairs inputs and outputs one-to-one, and it has an inverse. Third, test injectivity: do different inputs ever give the same output?. Fourth, test surjectivity: is every output in the codomain reached?. Fifth, if both hold, the function is bijective and has an inverse. Sixth, verify the answer by testing a value: compute the function of a known input and check the output. Worked examples: The function f(x) = 2x + 1 from the integers to the integers is injective, because 2a + 1 = 2b + 1 implies a = b, but it is not surjective, because no integer x gives 2x + 1 = 2: the outputs are the odd integers only. The function g(x) = x^3 from the reals to the reals is bijective: it is strictly increasing, so it is injective, and every real has a cube root, so it is surjective, and its inverse is the cube root. The function h(x) = x^2 from the reals to the reals is neither injective nor surjective onto the reals, but from the non-negative reals to the non-negative reals it is bijective.

Real applications: Programming is functions: a program is a function from inputs to outputs, and a programming language is a way of writing functions. Cryptography uses bijections: an encryption function must be bijective, so that decryption — the inverse — exists and recovers the message. Databases use functions: a query is a function from tables to tables, and a key is a function from rows to values. Science models with functions: the position of a planet is a function of time, and the temperature is a function of place.

Common questions and misconceptions: Is every rule a function? No: a rule must give exactly one output per input. The rule that assigns to x the square roots of x is not a function, because it gives two outputs. What is the difference between domain and range? The domain is where inputs come from; the range is the outputs actually reached. The codomain is where outputs are allowed to live, and it can be larger than the range. Can a function be its own inverse? Yes: f(x) = 1/x is its own inverse, and f(x) = -x is too. Such functions are called involutions. Why do functions matter in computing? Because a program is a function: it takes inputs and produces outputs, and the theory of computable functions is the theory of what programs can do.

Counting and Combinatorics

ID: JAH-DSCMS-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-discrete-mathematics-signature#JAH-DSCMS-000005 k=f510361c

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds a counting ladder from books to poker hands, with C(52, 5) as the running example.

Definition: Combinatorics is the mathematics of counting: how many ways can things be arranged, chosen, or combined? The basic tools are the multiplication principle (if one choice has m options and another has n, there are m times n combinations), the addition principle, permutations (ordered arrangements), and combinations (unordered selections). The binomial coefficient C(n, k) counts the ways to choose k things from n.

History: Combinatorics is ancient: Indian mathematicians counted arrangements in the 6th century BCE, and the binomial coefficients appear in the work of Pingala, around 200 BCE. Pascal's triangle, studied by Pascal in the 1650s and by Chinese mathematicians centuries earlier, organises the binomial coefficients. Euler founded graph theory and counted with generating functions in the 1700s. In the 20th century, combinatorics became central to computing: counting is what algorithms do, and the analysis of algorithms is combinatorics. Today combinatorics is used in probability, computer science, biology, and operations research.

Key concepts: The key ideas are multiplication principle, permutation, combination, factorial, binomial coefficient. If one choice has m options and a second has n, there are m times n combinations. Two dice have 6 times 6 = 36 outcomes. A permutation is an ordered arrangement. The number of permutations of n distinct things is n! (n factorial). A combination is an unordered selection. The number of ways to choose k things from n is C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n-k)!). The factorial n! is 1 times 2 times ... times n. 5! = 120. By convention 0! = 1. The binomial coefficient C(n, k) counts k-subsets of an n-set. C(5, 2) = 10, and the coefficients form Pascal's triangle. Worked examples: The number of ways to arrange 5 books on a shelf is 5! = 120. The number of ways to choose 2 books from 5 is C(5, 2) = 10. A password of 4 distinct digits has 10 times 9 times 8 times 7 = 5,040 possibilities. A committee of 3 chosen from 10 people has C(10, 3) = 120 possibilities. The number of hands of 5 cards from a 52-card deck is C(52, 5) = 2,598,960. Pascal's triangle gives the coefficients: the row 1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1 is C(5, 0) through C(5, 5).

Real applications: Probability uses combinatorics: every probability that counts outcomes needs combinations and permutations. Computer science counts with combinatorics: the number of possible inputs, the number of states, and the running time of an algorithm are all combinatorial. Genetics counts with combinatorics: the number of possible DNA sequences of length n is 4^n, and the number of gene orders is a factorial. Lotteries are combinatorics: the odds of winning are 1 over the number of combinations.

Common questions and misconceptions: When do I use permutations and when combinations? Permutations when order matters, combinations when it does not. Arranging books is a permutation; choosing a committee is a combination. What is 0!? 1, by convention: there is exactly one way to arrange nothing, and the convention makes the formulas work. Why is C(n, k) = C(n, n-k)? Because choosing k things to take is the same as choosing n - k things to leave behind. The symmetry is visible in Pascal's triangle. How big do the numbers get? Very fast: 20! is about 2.4 x 10^18, and C(52, 5) is 2,598,960. Combinatorics is the study of numbers that grow fast.

Permutations and Combinations

ID: JAH-DSCMS-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: math and subject explained in full record · JCC: JAHDB/world-discrete-mathematics-signature#JAH-DSCMS-000006 k=15ec8873

Signature treatment: The Signature edition adds an ordering ladder from books to PINs, with P(5, 3) versus C(5, 3) as the running contrast.

Definition: A permutation is an arrangement in which order matters: the number of permutations of n distinct objects taken k at a time is P(n, k) = n!/(n-k)!. A combination is a selection in which order does not matter: the number of combinations is C(n, k) = n!/(k!(n-k)!). The difference is division by k!: a combination counts each ordering once, while a permutation counts every ordering separately.

History: Permutations and combinations were studied by Indian mathematicians in the 6th century BCE, in the context of poetic metre, and by Arab mathematicians in the Middle Ages.
