Chlamydia JAH-SCIIE-000001 curable

Chlamydia is a common bacterial infection passed through sexual contact. Many people have no symptoms at all, which is why testing matters even when you feel well.

Symptoms and signs: Signs, when present, can include unusual discharge, burning or discomfort when passing urine, pain or discomfort in the lower abdomen or pelvis, and pain during sex. Many infections cause no signs at all.

Testing: Testing is usually a nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT) on a urine sample or a genital swab, as advised by a clinician. A clinician decides which sites to test based on the kind of sexual contact.

CURE STATUS: Curable. Chlamydia can be cleared with antibiotics prescribed by a clinician. Finishing the prescribed course and returning for any retest the clinician advises are part of the pathway.

IMMUNITY STATUS: No vaccine exists for chlamydia. A past infection does not protect against a future one, so prevention and retesting still matter after treatment.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician for testing, take only the antibiotics the clinician prescribes, tell recent sexual partners so they can be tested and treated, avoid sex until the clinician says treatment is complete, and return for the retest the clinician advises.

Partner care: recent sexual partners should be offered testing and treatment, because untreated partners can pass the infection back. A clinician or clinic can help with partner notification.

Prevention: use condoms consistently, test regularly when sexually active with new or multiple partners, and test again after treatment as advised.

In plain words: this record tells you three things separately so they never blur together: whether the condition can be cleared, whether a vaccine can protect against it, and what the clinician-led pathway is. If any of the three is unclear, ask a clinician to explain it for your situation.

See a clinician direction: if you have symptoms, a partner has been diagnosed, or you may have been exposed, see a clinician promptly for testing and advice. Do not try to treat this on your own, and do not use leftover medicines or someone else's prescription.

Gonorrhea JAH-SCIIE-000002 curable

Gonorrhea is a bacterial infection passed through sexual contact. It can affect the genitals, rectum, or throat, and it often causes no symptoms.

Symptoms and signs: Signs, when present, can include unusual discharge, burning when passing urine, soreness, and rectal discomfort or discharge. Throat infection usually causes no signs.

Testing: Testing is usually a NAAT on a urine sample, swab, or site-specific sample, and a clinician may also arrange culture so drug resistance can be checked. A clinician decides which sites to test.

CURE STATUS: Curable. Gonorrhea can be cleared with antibiotics prescribed by a clinician. Drug resistance is watched closely, so follow-up testing after treatment matters and a clinician may change the plan if the first treatment does not clear the infection.

IMMUNITY STATUS: No vaccine exists for gonorrhea. A past infection does not protect against a future one.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician for testing, take only the antibiotics the clinician prescribes, return for the follow-up test the clinician advises so any resistant infection is caught, tell recent sexual partners so they can be tested and treated, and avoid sex until the clinician says treatment is complete.

Partner care: recent sexual partners should be offered testing and treatment. Because resistance is possible, partners also need follow-up testing as their clinician advises.

Prevention: use condoms consistently, test regularly when sexually active with new or multiple partners, and test at every exposed site a clinician recommends.

Syphilis JAH-SCIIE-000003 curable

Syphilis is a bacterial infection passed through sexual contact and, in pregnancy, from parent to child. It develops in stages, and early stages can be easy to miss.

Symptoms and signs: Signs can include a painless sore at the site of contact, a rash (often on the palms or soles), fever, swollen glands, and sores in the mouth or genital area. Later stages may cause no signs for years while damage continues.

Testing: Testing is by blood tests (serology) as advised by a clinician, sometimes with direct examination of a sore. A clinician interprets the blood-test pattern to judge the stage and whether treatment has worked.

CURE STATUS: Curable. Syphilis can be cleared with antibiotics prescribed by a clinician, with the regimen chosen by the clinician for the stage of infection. Follow-up blood tests check that treatment has worked.

IMMUNITY STATUS: No vaccine exists for syphilis. A past infection does not protect against a future one, and reinfection is possible.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician for blood testing, take only the antibiotics the clinician prescribes for the stage found, attend the follow-up blood tests the clinician schedules, tell recent sexual partners so they can be tested and treated, and avoid sex until the clinician says treatment is complete.

Partner care: recent sexual partners should be offered testing and treatment, and partners in pregnancy need urgent clinician assessment because syphilis can pass to the child.

Prevention: use condoms consistently, test regularly when sexually active with new or multiple partners, and test in pregnancy as clinicians recommend.

Trichomoniasis JAH-SCIIE-000004 curable

Trichomoniasis is an infection caused by a tiny parasite, passed through sexual contact. It is one of the most common curable infections of this kind.

Symptoms and signs: Signs, when present, can include unusual discharge, itching or soreness around the genitals, discomfort when passing urine, and discomfort during sex. Many people have no signs.

Testing: Testing is usually a NAAT or microscopy on a genital sample, as advised by a clinician.

CURE STATUS: Curable. Trichomoniasis can be cleared with medicines prescribed by a clinician. Partners are treated at the same time so the infection is not passed back.

IMMUNITY STATUS: No vaccine exists for trichomoniasis. A past infection does not protect against a future one.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician for testing, take only the medicines the clinician prescribes, make sure sexual partners are treated as the clinician advises, avoid sex until the clinician says treatment is complete, and return for any retest advised.

Partner care: sexual partners should be offered testing and treatment even if they have no symptoms, because untreated partners can pass the infection back.

Prevention: use condoms consistently and test when symptoms appear or after a partner is diagnosed.

Herpes (HSV-1 and HSV-2) JAH-SCIIE-000005 manageable

Herpes is caused by the herpes simplex viruses, HSV-1 and HSV-2, passed through skin-to-skin and sexual contact. It causes outbreaks of sores that come and go.

Symptoms and signs: Signs can include blisters or sores around the mouth or genitals, tingling or itching before an outbreak, and flu-like feelings during a first outbreak. Outbreaks vary in frequency and severity from person to person.

Testing: Testing is usually a swab or laboratory test of a sore during an outbreak, or a blood test as advised by a clinician. A clinician explains what each test can and cannot show.

CURE STATUS: No cure exists for herpes. Antiviral medicines cannot remove the virus from the body, but they can shorten outbreaks and reduce how often outbreaks happen.

IMMUNITY STATUS: No vaccine exists for herpes. A past infection does not protect against a future one, and either virus type can be passed on even without visible sores.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician for testing and advice, discuss antiviral medicines the clinician may prescribe to treat outbreaks or to suppress them, learn the early signs of an outbreak, tell sexual partners, and use condoms and avoid contact during outbreaks as the clinician advises.

Partner care: tell sexual partners so they can learn their own status and discuss prevention with a clinician. Suppressive treatment and condom use, as a clinician advises, lower but do not remove the chance of passing the virus on.

Prevention: use condoms consistently, avoid sexual contact during outbreaks and when early warning signs appear, and talk with a clinician about suppressive treatment where appropriate.

HIV JAH-SCIIE-000006 manageable

HIV is a virus passed through sexual contact and blood that affects the body's defence system over time. With modern treatment, people with HIV can live long, healthy lives.

Symptoms and signs: Early infection may cause a flu-like illness, but many people have no signs for years. Later untreated infection can cause weight loss, fevers, night sweats, and repeated infections. Only testing can show HIV status.

Testing: Testing is by blood tests, including antigen-antibody tests, as advised by a clinician. Clinics also offer rapid tests, and a clinician confirms any reactive result with follow-up testing.

CURE STATUS: There is no general cure for HIV. Antiretroviral medicines prescribed by a clinician suppress the virus and protect health, but they must be taken as prescribed and do not remove the virus from the body.

IMMUNITY STATUS: No vaccine exists for HIV. A past exposure does not protect against infection, and prevention depends on the steps below.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician promptly for testing, start and stay on the antiretroviral treatment the clinician prescribes, attend regular follow-up and laboratory checks, and discuss with a clinician how effective treatment that keeps the virus suppressed means HIV cannot be passed to a sexual partner. People at risk can ask a clinician about pre-exposure prevention medicines, and anyone with a recent possible exposure should seek urgent clinician care about post-exposure options.

Partner care: tell sexual partners so they can be tested and, where appropriate, discuss prevention medicines with a clinician. With sustained viral suppression on treatment, onward sexual transmission does not occur, which clinicians describe as undetectable equals untransmittable.

Prevention: use condoms consistently, test regularly, ask a clinician about pre-exposure prevention medicines if at risk, and seek urgent clinician care after a possible exposure.

HPV JAH-SCIIE-000007 manageable

HPV, human papillomavirus, is a very common virus passed through sexual and skin-to-skin contact. Most infections clear on their own, but some types can cause warts or, over time, cancers.

Symptoms and signs: Many infections cause no signs. Low-risk types can cause warts on the genitals or around the anus. High-risk types usually cause no signs until cell changes or cancer develop, which is why screening matters.

Testing: Testing depends on the situation: a clinician examines warts directly, and cervical screening programmes use HPV testing and cell examination as local guidelines advise. There is no single general HPV test for everyone, and a clinician explains which test applies.

CURE STATUS: There is no cure for HPV itself. Clinicians treat what the virus causes, such as removing warts and treating abnormal cell changes, while the body's response clears most infections over time.

IMMUNITY STATUS: Vaccine-induced protection exists for HPV. Vaccination can protect against new infection with the common cancer-causing and wart-causing types covered by the vaccine. The vaccine does not treat an infection that is already present, and screening is still advised as clinicians recommend.

WAYS TO FIX: See a clinician for examination and screening as guidelines advise, have warts or abnormal cell changes treated by a clinician, ask a clinician about vaccination for yourself and for eligible family members, and use condoms, noting that condoms lower but do not remove HPV risk because uncovered skin can still transmit it.

Partner care: tell sexual partners so they can consider vaccination and screening as clinicians advise. Because HPV is so common and often silent, a new diagnosis does not by itself show when or from whom infection came.

Prevention: vaccination before exposure gives the strongest protection, condoms lower risk, and regular screening catches cell changes early, when treatment works best.

Mycoplasma genitalium JAH-SCIIE-000008 curable

Mycoplasma genitalium is a bacterial infection passed through sexual contact. It can cause irritation of the urinary passage and genital tract, and it is sometimes found when other infections have been ruled out.

Symptoms and signs: Signs, when present, can include discharge, burning when passing urine, and pelvic discomfort. Many infections cause no signs.

Testing: Testing is by NAAT where a clinician judges it appropriate, sometimes with resistance testing. A clinician decides when this test is useful, since it is not part of every routine screen.

CURE STATUS: Curable. Mycoplasma genitalium can be cleared with antibiotics prescribed by a clinician, but drug resistance is common, so the clinician may choose the antibiotic based on resistance testing and arrange a test of cure.

Hepatitis C is a virus that affects the liver. It spreads mainly through blood contact, and it can also be passed through sex, especially where blood may be present.
