What a Flight Is: Routes, Legs, and Flight Numbers Rank 1 · simple

Foundations of scheduled air travel: routes, legs, and flight numbers

A traveller looking at a departure board can be confused by the words flight, route, leg, and flight number, because they sound alike but describe different things. A route is the path an aircraft follows between two airports, a leg is one takeoff-to-landing segment of that path, and a flight number is the label an airline gives to a scheduled service, which may cover one leg or several legs. The problem this record solves is to separate those ideas clearly, so a reader can tell what is being sold, what is being flown, and what is being tracked. Without that separation, a direct flight with a stop can be mistaken for a nonstop flight, and a single flight number can be mistaken for a single takeoff and landing. The record explains each term in plain language, shows how they fit together on a real journey, and gives the reader a reliable way to read any published schedule without guessing.

History

Scheduled air services began in the early twentieth century with short hops carrying mail and a small number of passengers, and the language of routes and flight numbers grew up with them. As airlines began to publish timetables, they needed a short, repeatable way to name each service, so the flight number became the standard label, usually an airline designator followed by digits. International bodies later standardised the two-letter and three-letter airline codes and the three-letter airport codes that appear beside every flight number. Over time, networks grew from simple point-to-point hops into connected systems with through services, stopovers, and shared numbers, and the distinction between a flight, a leg, and a route became more important. Today the same vocabulary is used in timetables, booking systems, departure boards, and air traffic control, and it remains the basic grammar of commercial aviation.

The flight record

Consider a published service from New York John F. Kennedy International Airport to London Heathrow Airport. The route joins those two airports across the Atlantic, and in its simplest form it is flown as a single leg: one takeoff in New York and one landing in London, under one flight number. If the same flight number were instead used for a service that stopped in another city on the way, the route would still be New York to London, but it would contain two legs, and the passenger would need to know whether they stay on the same aircraft or change aircraft at the stop. The flight number stays the same across both legs in that case, which is why the number alone does not prove the journey is nonstop. Reading the timetable leg by leg removes the ambiguity: the departure time, the intermediate arrival and departure times, and the final arrival time each belong to a leg, while the flight number belongs to the whole scheduled service.

Methods

Start with the two airports: name the departure airport and the arrival airport using their public three-letter codes and city names.; Trace the route between them, noting whether it is flown nonstop or with one or more intermediate stops.; Split the route into legs, where each leg runs from one takeoff to the next landing.; Attach the flight number, and check whether that one number covers a single leg or several legs in sequence.; Read the published times for each leg, noting departure time, arrival time, and any change of aircraft or terminal.; Confirm the operating airline, because the airline selling the ticket is not always the airline flying the aircraft.; Cross-check the result against the published timetable and the airport departure board before treating it as final..

Results as reported

As reported in published aviation references, a flight is a scheduled air service identified by a flight number, a route is the path between its departure and arrival airports, and a leg is one takeoff-to-landing segment of that service. A nonstop flight has one leg, a direct flight may have more than one leg under one flight number, and a connecting journey uses more than one flight number. The New York to London city pair is reported as a long-haul transatlantic route of about 5,570 kilometres, normally flown as a single leg at jet cruise speed. These distinctions are stable across published timetables and are used the same way by airlines, airports, and air traffic control.

Skeptical analysis

The main risk in this topic is taking a flight number at face value. A reader who assumes that one flight number always means one takeoff is likely to be surprised by a direct service with a stop, and a reader who assumes that direct means nonstop will misjudge the journey time. Published timetables resolve this, but only if the reader looks at the legs and the times rather than the number alone. Another caution is that flight numbers are reused and renumbered over time, so a number remembered from one year may describe a different service in another year. The record therefore treats the timetable as the authority and the number as a label, and it advises checking the legs on the day of travel rather than relying on memory.

Science proof

A flight is a physical journey through the air, and its route is governed by the geometry of a sphere. The shortest path between two points on the Earth follows a great circle, and the formula for that distance uses the latitudes and longitudes of the two airports together with the radius of the Earth. In practice the flown route is longer than the great-circle distance, because air traffic control, weather, wind, and restricted airspace shape the actual track. A leg is the portion of that journey between two landings, and its duration depends on distance, cruise speed, climb and descent, and the wind along the way. The flight number is not a physical quantity at all: it is an administrative label that lets airlines, airports, and air traffic control refer to a scheduled service without ambiguity. The difference is the wind effect on that simulated run: a headwind costs time, a tailwind saves it, and the demo shows the arithmetic either way. A real flight plan adds climb, descent, reserves, and the actual winds of the day, which is why the demo says plainly that its wind is illustrative, not a forecast.

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'What a Flight Is: Routes, Legs, and Flight Numbers'
distance_km = 5570
cruise_kmh = 850
seats = 180
seed = 201
claimed_note = 'As reported: a single flight number can cover one or more legs along a published route, and the same number may be reused on different days.'

def still_air_time():
    return distance_km / cruise_kmh

def simulate():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    total = 0.0
    segment = distance_km / 24.0
    for _ in range(24):
        wind = rng.uniform(-60.0, 60.0)
        ground_speed = max(100.0, cruise_kmh + wind)
        total += segment / ground_speed
    return total

still = still_air_time()
adjusted_a = simulate()
adjusted_b = simulate()
result = {"title": title, "distance_km": distance_km,
          "cruise_kmh": cruise_kmh, "seats": seats, "seed": seed,
          "still_air_hours": round(still, 3),
          "adjusted_hours": round(adjusted_a, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Flight plan: " + title)
print("Route distance: %d km  Cruise speed: %d km/h  Seats: %d" % (distance_km, cruise_kmh, seats))
print("Still-air flight time: %.2f hours (distance divided by cruise speed)" % still)
print("Simulated adjusted flight time (fixed seed %d, 24 hourly wind segments): %.2f hours" % (seed, adjusted_a))
print("Wind effect on this simulated run: %+.2f hours" % (adjusted_a - still))
print("Claimed fact as reported: " + claimed_note)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:

else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")

The busiest passenger air routes in the world by seats and travellers

Asking which air route is the busiest sounds simple, but the answer depends on what is counted. Some rankings count the number of seats offered, some count the number of passengers carried, and some count the number of flights, and those three measures can give different winners. Short domestic routes with very frequent departures can carry more passengers in a year than famous long-haul routes, which surprises readers who expect the longest or best-known routes to lead the list. The problem this record solves is to explain how busyness is measured, why short high-frequency domestic routes dominate the rankings, and how to read a busiest-routes list without being misled by the choice of measure. It names the routes that are routinely reported at the top and explains the geography and demand behind them.

History

Busy air routes grew out of dense populations, island geography, and business travel. In South Korea, the short hop between Seoul Gimpo and Jeju Island became one of the most flown routes in the world, because Jeju is a major holiday destination reached far faster by air than by sea and land. In Japan, the route between Tokyo Haneda and Sapporo New Chitose grew on business and leisure demand across a long island nation. In Australia, the Melbourne to Sydney route became a classic business shuttle between the two largest cities. As aircraft grew larger and airlines added frequencies, these routes came to be served many times a day by several airlines, and aviation analysts began to publish annual rankings of seats and passengers that made the pattern visible to the public.

The flight record

The Seoul Gimpo to Jeju route is the classic example of a busiest route. The distance is only about 450 kilometres, and the flight takes roughly an hour, yet the route is served very frequently every day because Jeju Island draws large numbers of holiday travellers from the Seoul area and the sea crossing is slow by comparison. Airlines operate the route with narrow-body jets in high-density layouts, and several carriers compete on the same city pair, so the total number of seats in a year is very large even though each individual flight is short. The same logic explains Tokyo Haneda to Sapporo and Melbourne to Sydney: large populations, strong business or leisure demand, and no faster practical alternative for most travellers. The lesson is that frequency and demand, not distance, make a route busy.

Results as reported

As reported in published annual rankings, the busiest passenger air routes in the world are short domestic routes with very high frequencies. Seoul Gimpo to Jeju is routinely reported at or near the top by seats and by passengers, with Tokyo Haneda to Sapporo and Melbourne to Sydney also reported among the leaders. The Seoul to Jeju distance is about 450 kilometres, and the flight takes roughly an hour, yet the route carries far more travellers in a year than most long-haul routes because it is flown many times a day. The pattern is consistent across years: dense populations, island or resort destinations, and strong business demand produce the busiest routes, and the ranking changes only slowly from year to year.

Skeptical analysis

Busiest-route lists are easy to misread, because the winner changes with the measure. A route that leads by seats may not lead by passengers if its aircraft fly with many empty seats, and a route that leads by flights may not lead by passengers if it uses small aircraft. Rankings also mix years, and a route that led in one year may fall back in another as airlines add or cut frequencies. Some lists cover domestic routes only, while others mix domestic and international routes, which changes the result. The honest reading is therefore to ask three questions of any list: what was counted, which year does it cover, and does it include international routes. With those answers stated, the dominance of short, frequent domestic routes is a stable and well-supported finding.

Airline scheduling: hub-and-spoke networks, banks of flights, and airport slots

An airline cannot simply fly every city pair that passengers might want, because there are too many pairs and too few aircraft. The problem is how to connect hundreds of cities with a limited fleet while keeping aircraft, crews, and gates busy and giving passengers useful connections. The answer used by most large airlines is the hub-and-spoke system, in which flights gather at a central hub airport in coordinated waves called banks, so that passengers arriving on one flight can connect to many departing flights. The problem this record solves is to explain hubs, banks, and slots in plain language, to show why an airline would route a passenger through a hub rather than flying direct, and to explain the limits that airport slots and gate capacity place on the schedule.

History

The hub-and-spoke system grew in the United States after airline deregulation in 1978, when carriers were free to reshape their networks. American Airlines and Delta Air Lines were among the early builders of large hubs, gathering flights at airports such as Dallas Fort Worth and Atlanta so that a single arrival could feed dozens of departures. The idea spread around the world, and major airports such as London Heathrow, Dubai International, and Singapore Changi became hubs for their home carriers. At busy airports, the demand for takeoff and landing times outgrew the runways, so the system of airport slots developed: an airline holds a permission to use a runway at a particular time, and the schedule must fit inside the slots it holds. Banks of flights, in which many arrivals land in a short window and many departures leave soon after, became the visible rhythm of a hub airport.

The flight record

Atlanta Hartsfield-Jackson is a classic hub example, and a journey from Atlanta to Chicago shows how the system works in miniature. The flight itself is a short domestic leg of about 975 kilometres, but its place in the schedule matters as much as its distance: it may arrive in a bank of inbound flights at the hub, or depart in a bank that carries connecting passengers onward to many other cities. A passenger flying from a small city to another small city may therefore travel through Atlanta even though the direct distance is shorter, because the hub gives them a single connection with many onward choices. The slot system constrains this design, because each arrival and departure at a busy hub needs a runway slot at the right time, and the banks must be timed so that gates, staff, and baggage systems can handle the surge.

Skeptical analysis

Hub-and-spoke scheduling has real drawbacks that should not be hidden. A passenger routed through a hub travels farther and spends longer than on a direct flight, and a delay at the hub can cause missed connections across the network. Congestion at the busiest hubs can make the bank pattern fragile, and the slot system can protect established airlines while making it hard for new carriers to enter a busy airport. Low-cost carriers have shown that point-to-point flying can work well on dense routes without a hub at all. The fair conclusion is that hubs are a trade: they buy connection choice and network coverage at the price of longer journeys and concentrated delay risk, and the balance suits some airlines and routes better than others.

A published flight schedule looks like a simple table, but it is easy to read it wrong. Airports: runways, terminals, gates, and the journey of a flight on the ground

A flight is often imagined as only the time in the air, but half of the journey happens on the ground at the airport. The aircraft must be guided to a runway, take off, and later land, taxi to a gate, and be turned around for its next flight, while passengers check in, pass through checks, and find their gate. The problem this record solves is to explain the airport as a system: the airfield with its runways and taxiways, the terminal with its check-in, departures, and arrivals, and the gate where the aircraft meets the passengers. It shows how those parts fit together, why an airport needs both an airfield and a terminal, and what a passenger should expect at each stage of the journey.

History

Early airfields were little more than grass fields with a hangar and a windsock, and passengers walked to the aircraft across the grass. As air travel grew, airports built paved runways, control towers, and terminal buildings, and the modern airport took shape in the middle of the twentieth century. The jet age demanded longer runways and larger terminals, and the growth of hub networks demanded airports that could handle banks of connecting flights and large numbers of transferring passengers. Today the largest airports, such as Atlanta Hartsfield-Jackson, London Heathrow, and Dubai International, handle tens of millions of passengers a year, and their terminals, runways, and baggage systems are designed as one coordinated system. The airport codes that identify them, three letters such as ATL, LHR, and DXB, are published and used worldwide.

The flight record

Consider a medium-haul flight of about 2,000 kilometres departing from a large airport. The passenger checks in at the terminal, passes through the departure checks, and walks to a gate in one of the piers or satellites. The aircraft is parked at the gate, its crew prepares it for departure, and baggage is loaded into the hold. After boarding, the aircraft pushes back, taxis to the runway, and takes off, and the flight lasts about two and a half hours at jet cruise speed. On landing at the destination, the aircraft taxis to an arrival gate, the passengers leave the aircraft, and checked bags are delivered to the reclaim belts. The whole journey, from terminal to terminal, depends on the runway, the taxiways, the gate, and the terminal working together, and a delay in any one of them can delay the flight.

History of scheduled commercial aviation

When did scheduled air travel actually begin, and what separates a scheduled airline flight from an earlier demonstration, a joyride, or a one-off charter? The question matters because aviation history is full of firsts that overlap: first powered flight, first passenger carried, first airmail, and first scheduled service are four different milestones, and they are often confused with one another. A scheduled flight means a service that is advertised in advance, runs on a published timetable, and carries paying passengers on a regular route, whether or not every seat is sold. This record works through that definition and applies it to the crossing of Tampa Bay in Florida, where a small flying boat began carrying passengers between St. Petersburg and Tampa on a fixed schedule in early 1914. The problem is to show what the service was, who ran it, how far it flew, and why historians treat it as the first scheduled airline flight, while also being honest about the earlier airship services that complicate the claim.

History

On 1 January 1914, the St. Petersburg-Tampa Airboat Line flew its first scheduled service across Tampa Bay in Florida. The pilot was Tony Jannus, flying a Benoist XIV flying boat built by Thomas Benoist, and the single passenger was Abe Pheil, a former mayor of St. Petersburg, who had won the first ticket at auction. The crossing covered about 29 kilometres of open water and took about 23 minutes, where the same journey by road or rail at the time took many hours around the bay. The line ran a published timetable for a few months in early 1914, carrying passengers and light freight between the two cities, and it is widely recorded as the first scheduled airline service using a heavier-than-air aircraft. Earlier scheduled airship services had operated in Germany, which is why careful histories qualify the claim to heavier-than-air flight. The service ended after a short season, but the date, the route, and the timetable are preserved in published aviation histories and museum records.

The aircraft carried only one passenger beside the pilot, so the whole service was a one-seat operation, and the timetable offered regular crossings rather than a single celebration flight. Flight mathematics, great-circle distance, and flight time

Every flight plan starts with two questions: how far is it, and how long will it take. On a flat map the shortest line between two cities looks straight, but the Earth is a sphere, so the shortest path between two points on its surface is an arc of a great circle, the largest circle that can be drawn around the globe through both points. A flight that follows that arc travels less distance, burns less fuel, and arrives sooner than one that follows a straight line on a flat chart. The problem this record solves is how to compute that distance from the latitude and longitude of the two endpoints, and how to turn the distance into a flight time once a cruise speed is known. The calculation must be simple enough to check by hand, honest about what it leaves out, and clear about why real flights still differ from the textbook answer.

History

Spherical geometry is far older than aviation. Ancient Greek scholars worked out that the Earth is round and measured its size, and navigators have used great-circle sailing for centuries, because a ship that follows a great circle crosses an ocean faster than one that holds a constant compass heading. Early aviators inherited the same mathematics: the great pioneers of long-distance flight planned their routes as great-circle arcs, even when the wind and the limits of their aircraft forced them off the ideal line. As commercial aviation grew, flight planning moved from paper charts and tables to computers, but the underlying formula did not change. Modern flight-planning systems still start with the great-circle distance and then adjust it for winds, air-traffic routing, and the climb and descent at each end of the flight.

Flight planning, fuel, aircraft weight, and range

A flight plan is an arithmetic problem with a cruel twist: the fuel an aircraft needs is itself heavy, and carrying that fuel burns more fuel. Fuel planning became a science when long-distance flight became routine. The early ocean crossings were planned with slide rules and generous margins, because a miscalculation over water left nowhere to land, and the pilots of those flights watched their fuel gauges the whole way. Commercial aviation turned that craft into a regulated discipline: aviation authorities publish rules for the reserve fuel a flight must carry, airlines employ dispatchers who build each flight plan, and manufacturers publish performance data for each aircraft type showing how fuel burn changes with weight, altitude, and speed. Aviation weather, winds, jet streams, and turbulence

The sky is not empty space; it is moving air, and a flight moves through that air rather than over still ground. A headwind slows an aircraft over the ground, a tailwind speeds it up, and a jet stream, a narrow river of fast air high in the atmosphere, can change a flight time by an hour or more. Turbulence shakes the aircraft when the air is uneven, and storms, icing, and low visibility can close a route altogether. The problem this record explains is how weather shapes a flight: how pilots and dispatchers read the wind, why the same route takes a different time in each direction, and what turbulence is and how crews deal with it. The aim is a reader who can look at a flight time and understand how much of it is the aircraft and how much is the sky.

History

Weather has shaped flight since the first balloons, and the great long-distance pioneers learned its power the hard way, fighting headwinds over oceans with no weather reports at all. The discovery of the jet streams came with high-altitude flight in the Second World War era, when aircraft climbing to new heights met winds far stronger than anyone had expected, and the post-war growth of commercial aviation turned weather forecasting into a core part of flight planning. Today dispatchers receive detailed wind and turbulence forecasts for every route, aircraft report the winds they meet back to forecasters, and pilots receive updated forecasts in flight. The science has improved enormously, but the basic facts have not changed: the wind is part of the flight plan, and no plan is complete without it.

Airline economics, load factor, seating, and fares

An aircraft costs nearly the same to fly whether it carries ten passengers or two hundred, because the fuel, the crew, the landing fees, and the lease on the aircraft must be paid either way. That single fact drives the economics of every flight: the airline must fill enough seats at high enough fares to cover the cost of the trip, and an empty seat on a departed flight is revenue lost forever, since it can never be sold again. The problem this record explains is how airlines think about that puzzle, through the load factor, the share of available seats that are filled, and through fares that change with demand, timing, and competition. A reader who understands load factor and fares can read an airline result and understand why a full flight can still lose money and why a half-empty flight can still be worth flying.

History

Airline economics began with airmail, when governments paid carriers to move mail and passengers were a sideline, and it changed completely when aircraft grew large enough to carry paying passengers profitably. The regulated era that followed set fares by rule and limited competition, and the deregulation of airlines in the United States in 1978, followed by similar changes elsewhere, let airlines set their own fares and choose their own routes. That freedom produced the modern system of yield management, in which fares on the same flight change constantly as seats sell and departure approaches, and it produced the low-cost carriers, which fill aircraft with dense seating, quick turnarounds, and point-to-point routes. Through every change, the load factor has remained the central number: the share of seats filled decides whether a route survives.

The flight record

Consider a representative route of about 1,500 kilometres, roughly the scale of a medium-haul flight within a continent, flown by an aircraft with about 180 seats. If 144 of those seats are filled, the load factor is 80 percent, which is a healthy figure for a scheduled service. If only 90 seats are filled, the load factor is 50 percent, and the same flight may lose money even though the aircraft is half full, because the operating cost has barely changed. The airline responds by changing fares, by changing the time of the flight, or by using a smaller aircraft on the route. The example shows why airlines watch the load factor so closely: a few percentage points of load factor can be the difference between a route that pays and a route that is cut.

Skeptical analysis

A high load factor is not the same as a profit, and a careful reader should not treat it as one. History

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'The Flights Database Field Guide Index'
distance_km = 1000
cruise_kmh = 850
seats = 180
seed = 224
claimed_note = 'As reported: this field guide indexes all twenty-three other records of the flights database pair, one plain sentence each, from routes and schedules through to the future of flight.'

def still_air_time():
    return distance_km / cruise_kmh

def simulate():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    total = 0.0
    segment = distance_km / 24.0
    for _ in range(24):
        wind = rng.uniform(-60.0, 60.0)
        ground_speed = max(100.0, cruise_kmh + wind)
        total += segment / ground_speed
    return total

still = still_air_time()
adjusted_a = simulate()
adjusted_b = simulate()
result = {"title": title, "distance_km": distance_km,
          "cruise_kmh": cruise_kmh, "seats": seats, "seed": seed,
          "still_air_hours": round(still, 3),
          "adjusted_hours": round(adjusted_a, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Flight plan: " + title)
print("Route distance: %d km  Cruise speed: %d km/h  Seats: %d" % (distance_km, cruise_kmh, seats))
