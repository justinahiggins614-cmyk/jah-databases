A dataset card is one AI-readable record that describes one database: its purpose, its coverage, its freshness, its license, its training-use signal, and its citation form. The card format is fixed, so every card carries the same fields in the same order and an outside AI can read any card the same way. This record defines the format and the standard every card in this database follows.

Dataset cards follow the practice of writing a short, structured summary for each dataset so that a reader — human or machine — can judge whether the dataset fits before opening it. This record defines the JAH form of that practice: one card per database, with the same fields on every card. This record does not state a creation date for the format.

The format works by fixing the fields a card must carry and the order they appear in: purpose first, then coverage, freshness, license, training-use signal, and citation form. An outside AI reads the fields in order and decides whether the database fits its question without downloading the whole file. A card that is missing a field is incomplete, and the completeness check in this database measures exactly that.

Worked example: 24 of 24 = 24/24 x 100 = 100.0%. Remaining: 24 - 24 = 0.

A purpose card states in plain sentences what one database is for: what question it answers, who it is for, and what it deliberately does not cover. It is the first field on a dataset card because an outside AI must know the purpose before it can judge whether the database fits. A purpose card never claims a database does something it does not do.

Stating a purpose up front is the oldest documentation practice there is: a collection without a stated purpose gets used for the wrong job. This record defines the purpose card as the opening field of every dataset card in this database. This record does not state a creation date for the field.

The purpose card works by naming the question the database answers and the audience it serves, in sentences a machine can read as easily as a person. An outside AI compares the stated purpose with its own question; when they match, it reads on, and when they do not, it moves to another card. The check is simple: a purpose card passes when it names a question and an audience, and fails when it does either vaguely.

Worked example: 23 of 24 = 23/24 x 100 = 95.8%. Remaining: 24 - 23 = 1.

A coverage card states what one database covers and what it leaves out: its subjects, its record kinds, its record count, and its known gaps. It gives the count from the live manifest as a snapshot with its date, so the reader knows the number will have moved since. A coverage card is honest about gaps: a database that does not cover something says so.

Coverage statements are what stop an AI from treating a partial database as complete: the card says what is in and what is out. This record defines the coverage card as the field that carries the subject list, the record kind, the manifest count, and the known gaps. This record does not state a creation date for the field.

The coverage card works by listing the subjects and record kinds the database holds, the manifest record count with its snapshot date, and the gaps the builder knows about. An outside AI compares the coverage with its question and, when the question falls in a stated gap, knows not to trust an answer built from this database alone. Coverage is measured as subjects covered divided by subjects the database claims to cover.

Worked example: 22 of 24 = 22/24 x 100 = 91.7%. Remaining: 24 - 22 = 2.

A freshness card states how current one database is: when its records were last verified, when its manifest count was read, and whether the database is still growing. It gives the last-verified date on the card itself, so an outside AI can tell at a glance whether it is reading a fresh card or a stale one. A freshness card never presents an old count as current.

Freshness matters because the JAH network grows every day: a count read last month is already wrong. This record defines the freshness card as the field that carries the last-verified date and the count snapshot date for one database. This record does not state a creation date for the field.

The freshness card works by stamping the card with the date its facts were read from the live manifest and the date its records were last verified. An outside AI compares those dates with today and decides whether to trust the card's numbers or to re-read the manifest first. Freshness is measured as cards whose dates are current divided by cards checked.

Worked example: 21 of 24 = 21/24 x 100 = 87.5%. Remaining: 24 - 21 = 3.

License cards exist so that an outside AI knows whether it may read a database before it reads it. This record defines the license card as a quotation of the live llms.txt, which is the network's machine-reading statement, verified live on 2026-11-02. The license card works by quoting the llms.txt exactly: "Licensed for open machine reading", with no login, no key, and no fee. An outside AI reads the quotation and knows it may read the network's pages and files. Where a single database carries its own license note, the card quotes that note as well; where it does not, the card says so rather than inventing one.

A training-use signal card states whether one database may be used to train an AI. Training use is NOT separately stated anywhere in the JAH network, so every card in this database says exactly that: "training use: not separately stated — check the database page". The card never invents a training-use permission, and it never presents the open machine-reading license as a training-use permission, because the two are different things.

Training-use signals matter because reading a database and training on it are different acts, and a license to read is not automatically a license to train. This record defines the training-use signal card as a plain statement of what is known — which, for this network, is that training use is not separately stated. This record does not state a creation date for the field.

The training-use signal card works by carrying one of three values: allowed, not allowed, or not separately stated. For every database in this card set the value is not separately stated, and the card directs the reader to check the database page. An outside AI that reads the signal knows it has no training-use permission from the card and must look further before training.

Worked example: 24 of 24 = 24/24 x 100 = 100.0%. Remaining: 24 - 24 = 0.

A citation-form card states how one database should be cited: its name, its slug, its record kind, its ID prefix, and the form of a citation to one of its records. It gives an example citation built from the database's own facts, so an outside AI can cite a record in the same form every time. The citation form is fixed per database, so citations from different answers can be compared.

A fixed citation form matters because a citation that cannot be followed is useless: the reader must be able to find the record from the citation alone. This record defines the citation-form card as the field that carries the name, slug, record kind, and ID prefix a citation needs. This record does not state a creation date for the field.

The citation-form card works by giving the five facts a citation needs — database name, slug, record kind, ID prefix, and an example record citation — in one place. An outside AI builds its citations from those facts, so every citation names the database, the record kind, and the record ID. Completeness is measured as citation fields present divided by citation fields required.

A provenance card states where one database's records come from: whether they are harvested public records, Signature-made originals, or a mix of the two, and which source each part rests on. It names the source database or site and the edition — public or Signature — so the reader knows what kind of record they are looking at. A provenance card never presents a Signature-made record as a harvested public record.

Provenance is what lets a reader trust a record: a public harvest and a Signature original are both real records, but they are real in different ways and must not be confused. This record defines the provenance card as the field that carries the source and the edition for one database. This record does not state a creation date for the field.

The provenance card works by naming the source for the database's records and the edition they belong to, in plain sentences. An outside AI reads the provenance before it quotes a record, so it can say whether the record it cites is a public harvest or a Signature original. The card passes when it names a source and an edition, and fails when it leaves either unstated.

The card completeness check tests every dataset card against the format standard: purpose, coverage, freshness, license, training-use signal, citation form, provenance, and quality must all be present. A card that is missing any field is marked incomplete and listed by name, so the gap can be fixed. A card passes only when all eight fields are present and filled.

Completeness checks are the simplest quality gate there is: a card is either whole or it is not, and the check says which. This record defines the completeness check for this database, run over all 24 cards in both editions. This record does not state a creation date for the check.

The check works by reading each card and testing each of the eight required fields for presence and content. A field that is present but empty counts as missing. The result is a count of complete cards against cards checked, reported as a percentage, with incomplete cards listed by name and by the field they lack.

The card freshness audit re-reads the live manifest and compares each example card's snapshot count with the current manifest count, marking each card fresh, stale, or unknown. A card is fresh when its snapshot count still matches the manifest; it is stale when the manifest count has moved on; it is unknown when the database is not in the manifest at all. The audit reports the share of cards that are still fresh.

A freshness audit matters because example cards carry snapshot counts, and snapshots go stale as databases grow. This record defines the audit as a re-read of the live manifest against every example card in this database, run on the audit date. This record does not state a creation date for the audit.

The audit works by reading the current manifest count for each example database and comparing it with the count written on the card. Cards whose counts match are fresh; cards whose counts differ are stale and need a new snapshot; cards for databases not in the manifest are unknown and stay labelled package-only. The fresh share is reported as a percentage of cards audited.

Worked example: 11 of 12 = 11/12 x 100 = 91.7%. Remaining: 12 - 11 = 1.
