DESCRIPTIONS
In this design the whiteboard is a loom: each person's strokes are threads, and the finished cloth is the group's thinking. A thread can be followed back to its author, and a thread that is pulled out leaves a visible gap. The design suits groups whose thinking is built together, stroke by stroke. This is a Signature design-study: an invented proposal for how the Mixed Reality Collaboration Platform could be arranged, labeled as a design throughout and never presented as established fact. The design works through 3 parts: Loom frame; Coloured threads; Pattern book. Each part is named so the design can be read, judged, and changed. The design is written for a reader who wants to imagine a different way the work could be done, and for a builder who wants a starting point, not a finished system. Nothing in this record claims the design is built, tested, or deployed; it is a design, and it says so wherever it appears.

MATH PROOF
The mathematical reasoning is plain arithmetic on the design's own figures. The figures are: cloths_kept = 12; colours = 8; looms = 1; threads = 480. Adding them in one order and adding them again in a different order gives the same total, because addition does not depend on order — a fact the demo in this record checks by computing the total twice, in two different ways, and comparing the two fingerprints. The fingerprint is the SHA-256 digest, a standard fingerprint of a piece of text: the same text always gives the same fingerprint. The demo prints both fingerprints and whether they match; the program recomputes the same total and the same fingerprint from the same figures. The arithmetic is machine-checked by the demo; the design claim is stated, not machine-checked.
total = sum of all figures; digest = SHA-256 of the total as text
The equation says: add all of the design's figures together to get the total, then take the SHA-256 fingerprint of that total written as text. The demo computes the total twice, in two different orders, and the two fingerprints must be identical.

The The Shared Loom design comes from asking what the Mixed Reality Collaboration Platform would look like if it were arranged around one idea instead of around the usual procedure. The idea is old in spirit — people have always arranged work around a single clear image — and new in its application to this subject. WORKFLOW
Problem: The design answers a question: A room where the whiteboard is a loom and every stroke is a thread. The question matters because the usual arrangement of the work hides something — a cost, a risk, a pleasure, a fairness — that the design brings into the open. The design does not claim to be the only answer, or the best answer; it claims to be one answer, clearly drawn, so the reader can compare it with the usual way and decide what is worth keeping.
Solution: The design is built in 4 steps, in order. step 1: Set the loom with the group's colours; step 2: Weave each stroke as a thread; step 3: Follow any thread back to its author; step 4: Keep the finished cloth with the room. Each step is a choice the designer made, and each choice is written so the reader can disagree with it and change it. The parts of the design are: Loom frame; Coloured threads; Pattern book. A reader who follows the steps will arrive at the same design, and a reader who changes a step will arrive at a different design — which is the point of a design-study: it is a place to start, not a place to stop.
Answer: What the design delivers is a clear picture: a The Shared Loom that can be imagined, discussed, and improved. The figures that describe this design are: cloths_kept = 12; colours = 8; looms = 1; threads = 480. Those numbers are the design's own checkable figures, and the demo in this record recomputes them from the figures alone, so any reader can verify them without trusting the record's word. A design whose figures check out is a design whose arithmetic is honest, even though the design itself remains a proposal.
Applies: The Shared Loom applies as a design-study wherever the Mixed Reality Collaboration Platform is imagined differently: when a team wants a new way to think about the work, when a builder wants a starting point, or when a reader wants to judge one arrangement against another. It does not apply as a specification, because it has not been tested; it applies as an idea, because ideas are what designs are for. It applies wherever a clear image helps people understand the work, and it stops applying where the image no longer fits.
