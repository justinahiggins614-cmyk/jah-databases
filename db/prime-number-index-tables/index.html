What a prime number is Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established The definition of prime numbers and the trial-division test are established mathematics, documented in Euclid's Elements and in every modern number theory text.

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 whose only divisors are 1 and itself.

Every table in this database rests on one definition, so the definition has to be exact before any table can be trusted. A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 that cannot be written as a product of two smaller whole numbers. The number 2 is prime, 3 is prime, and 5 is prime; the number 4 is not prime because 4 equals 2 times 2, and the number 1 is not prime because the definition requires a number greater than 1. This sharp edge matters: if 1 were counted as prime, the unique factorization of whole numbers would break, and every index table built on primes would carry a silent error. The record therefore states the definition, the exclusion of 1, and the reason for it, so that every later record in the pair can be read against a definition that does not move.

Take the number 29. Its square root is about 5.39, so only the divisors 2, 3, and 5 need testing: 29 is odd, its digits sum to 11 so it is not divisible by 3, and it does not end in 0 or 5. No divisor works, so 29 is prime, and it is the tenth prime in the table. Take the number 27 instead: its digits sum to 9, so it is divisible by 3, and 27 equals 3 times 9, so 27 is composite and never appears in the prime table. The same test on 97, whose square root is about 9.85, checks 2, 3, 5, and 7 and finds no divisor, so 97 is the twenty-fifth prime and the last prime below 100.

History

The definition of prime numbers reaches back to Euclid, whose Elements, written around 300 BCE, proved that there are infinitely many primes and that every whole number factors uniquely into primes. Eratosthenes, working in the third century BCE, gave the first practical sieve for listing them. The exclusion of 1 from the primes was settled much later, as number theory matured in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: mathematicians from Euler to Gauss used the definition that keeps unique factorization true, and the modern convention follows them. Prime tables themselves were computed by hand for centuries, with published tables reaching into the millions by the early twentieth century, before computers took over the work.

Method

Take a whole number n greater than 1 and test divisors from 2 up to the square root of n.; If any divisor divides n evenly, n is composite; if none does, n is prime.; Exclude 1 from the primes by definition, and exclude 0 and negative numbers from the table.; Record the result in the index table with the prime's position, its value, and its gap from the previous prime.; Check the entry against the unique factorization rule: every composite below the table limit must factor into listed primes..

A case passes when the stored classification matches the trial-division result, so the score measures the table's accuracy against the definition, not against opinion.

Reading a prime index table Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established The three-column prime index layout and its position convention are the established standard of published prime tables and the OEIS.

A prime index table pairs each prime with its position, so a reader can look up a prime by number or a number by prime.

A list of primes is only half a tool; the other half is knowing how to read it. A prime index table has three columns that matter: the position n, the prime p(n), and the gap g(n) = p(n) - p(n-1), the distance from the previous prime. To find the prime at position 10, read across row 10 and get 29; to find the position of 97, scan the prime column and read back 25. The gap column turns the table into a measurement instrument: most gaps are small, the gap 2 marks twin primes, and the occasional large gap is the subject of the gap records later in this pair. A reader who can move both ways through the table, position to prime and prime to position, can use every other record in the pair without confusion.

A reader wants the prime at position 20. The table row reads 20, 71, gap 2, because 71 follows 69? No: 71 follows the previous prime 67, so the gap is 4. Reading carefully, the row is position 20, prime 71, gap 4. The same reader wants the position of 43 and scans down the prime column to find 43 at position 14, with gap 2 from 41. Both lookups use the same three columns, and the gap column catches misreadings: if a gap ever reads 0 or negative, the table has an error.

History

Indexed prime tables with positions were a nineteenth-century innovation: once tables passed a million, position became the only practical way to refer to a prime. Derrick Norman Lehmer's 1914 table listed primes to 10,006,721 with their counts, and modern tables such as the UTM prime pages and the OEIS carry the same position convention. The gap column came later, when the study of prime gaps became a subject of its own in the twentieth century, and the three-column layout used here follows that mature convention.

Method

Find the position column and the prime column, and confirm the first row reads position 1, prime 2.; To look up by position, read across the row: position 10 gives prime 29.; To look up by prime, scan the prime column: 97 sits at position 25.; Read the gap column as the difference from the previous prime: the gap after 3 is 2, after 7 is 2, after 23 is 6.; Check any row by trial division of its prime and by counting positions from the top of the table..

Dataset

The dataset is a three-column index table for the first twenty-five primes: position, prime, and gap from the previous prime, with the first row carrying gap 0 by convention since there is no previous prime. The table is complete from position 1 to position 25, and the gap column sums to 95, which is 97 minus 2, a built-in consistency check on the whole table.

Scorer

The scorer is a table-lookup check: for each test case, state a position, a prime, or a gap, and compare against the stored three-column table. The score is the share of ten lookups answered correctly. A case passes when the stated value matches the table row exactly.

The prime counting function pi(x) Rank 4 · simple

Evidence: established The checkpoint values pi(100) = 25 through pi(10^6) = 78498 are established by repeated computation and published in the OEIS and standard references.

pi(x) counts how many primes are less than or equal to x, and its values anchor every prime table.

A table of primes answers which numbers are prime, but the counting function pi(x) answers how many there are, and that count is the anchor every table is checked against. pi(100) equals 25, pi(1000) equals 168, pi(10000) equals 1229, pi(100000) equals 9592, and pi(1000000) equals 78498. These five values are the checkpoints of this pair: any prime table that claims to list all primes up to a million must contain exactly 78,498 entries, and any table that disagrees is wrong. The function grows slowly and irregularly, roughly like x divided by the natural logarithm of x, and that slow growth is why prime tables stay practical even as the numbers get large.

A builder sieves up to 1000 and counts 168 survivors. That matches the published pi(1000) = 168, so the sieve is verified. The same builder estimates with 1000 over log 1000, about 1000 over 6.9, about 145, and sees the estimate is low by 23, which is normal: the approximation improves slowly as x grows. At a million, the estimate is about 72,382 against the true 78,498, still low by about 8 percent, showing that the prime number theorem describes the long run, not any single table.

History

The counting function was studied by Gauss, who as a teenager guessed from tables that pi(x) grows like x over log x, and by Legendre, who proposed a formula for it. Chebyshev proved in the mid-nineteenth century that pi(x) is bounded between constant multiples of x over log x. Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin proved the prime number theorem in 1896, confirming Gauss's guess. The exact values pi(1000) = 168 and pi(10^6) = 78498 were established by hand computation first and confirmed by machine computation many times since.

Fermat's little theorem says a^(p-1) is 1 modulo p for any prime p and any a not divisible by p.

Fermat's little theorem is the first deep fact about primes that a table can verify directly: if p is prime and a is not a multiple of p, then a raised to the power p-1 leaves remainder 1 on division by p. Wilson's theorem says (p-1)! leaves remainder p-1 modulo p for every prime p, a perfect but impractical primality test.

Wilson's theorem states that for a prime p, the factorial (p-1)! is congruent to -1 modulo p: for p = 5, 4! = 24, and 24 mod 5 is 4, which is -1 mod 5. For p = 7, 6! = 720, and 720 mod 7 is 6. Euler's totient function Rank 13 · compounded

Evidence: established Euler's totient function and Euler's theorem are established mathematics, and the RSA use of phi(pq) is established and documented since 1977.

Euler's totient phi(n) counts the numbers below n that share no factor with n, and it powers the RSA key steps.

Euler's totient function phi(n) counts how many integers from 1 to n are coprime to n, sharing no common factor greater than 1: phi(10) = 4, because 1, 3, 7, and 9 are coprime to 10; phi(12) = 4, because 1, 5, 7, and 11 are coprime to 12; phi(36) = 12; phi(97) = 96, because every number below a prime is coprime to it. The function has a clean formula from the prime factorization: if n = p1^a1 times p2^a2 and so on, then phi(n) = n times the product of (1 - 1/p) over the distinct primes dividing n. For a product of two distinct primes p and q, phi(pq) = (p-1)(q-1), which is the exact quantity RSA key generation needs, and the record works that case through with p = 3 and q = 11.

Compute phi(10): 10 = 2 x 5, so phi(10) = 10 x (1 - 1/2) x (1 - 1/5) = 10 x 1/2 x 4/5 = 4, matching the list 1, 3, 7, 9. Compute phi(36): 36 = 2^2 x 3^2, so phi(36) = 36 x 1/2 x 2/3 = 12. For RSA with p = 3 and q = 11, n = 33 and phi(33) = 2 x 10 = 20, so a public exponent e = 3 has inverse d = 7 modulo 20, since 3 x 7 = 21 = 1 mod 20, and the pair (3, 7) is a working toy RSA key.

The function became central to cryptography in 1977, when Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman used phi(pq) = (p-1)(q-1) in RSA: the public exponent e and the private exponent d are inverses modulo phi(pq), and the security of the system rests on the difficulty of factoring pq to find phi.

Evidence: established The Miller-Rabin test is established mathematics, published by Miller in 1976 and Rabin in 1980, and it is the standard primality test in cryptographic practice.

The Miller-Rabin test checks primality by testing bases against a strong form of Fermat's theorem, and it is the workhorse of prime generation.

The Fermat test has a weakness: Carmichael numbers pass it for every base, so a Fermat pass is not proof. The Miller-Rabin test fixes this by writing n - 1 as 2^s times d with d odd, then checking each base a: either a^d = 1 mod n, or a^(2^r d) = -1 mod n for some r below s. If neither holds, n is composite, proven, with no exceptions. If the test passes for a base, n is probably prime, and each new base cuts the error: a composite passes a random base with probability at most 1 in 4, so twenty bases make the error below one in a trillion. The record works the test on small numbers where the answer is known, so the reader can see a witness expose a composite and a clean pass confirm a prime.

No squaring reached 220, so base 2 is a witness and 221 is composite, which matches 221 = 13 x 17. Cramér's random model, proposed in 1936, pretends each integer n is prime with probability 1 over log n, independently, and then asks what the primes would look like. Primes in arithmetic progressions Rank 23 · complex

Evidence: established Dirichlet's theorem is established mathematics from 1837, and the small progression lists are established by direct checking.

Dirichlet proved every arithmetic progression with coprime first term and difference holds infinitely many primes.

An arithmetic progression is a sequence a, a + d, a + 2d, and so on. Dirichlet's theorem, proved in 1837, says that if a and d share no common factor, the progression contains infinitely many primes: the primes 1 mod 4 are 5, 13, 17, 29, 37, infinite in number; the primes 3 mod 4 are 3, 7, 11, 19, 23, also infinite. The progression 2 mod 4 holds only the prime 2, because every later term is even, and the progression 0 mod 3 holds only 3. The record lists the small progressions with their first primes and states the theorem, and it is honest about the gap between Dirichlet and the tables: the theorem proves infinitude but gives no formula for the next prime in a progression, so the tables still have to be computed term by term.

The primes 1 mod 4 below 50 are 5, 13, 17, 29, 37, 41. The primes 3 mod 4 below 50 are 3, 7, 11, 19, 23, 31, 43, 47. There are six of the first kind and eight of the second below 50, which is Chebyshev's bias in miniature: the 3 mod 4 primes lead early, though Dirichlet says both lists are infinite and the prime number theorem for progressions says they equalize in the long run.

History

Dirichlet proved the theorem in 1837 using L-functions, the first great application of analysis to number theory. The special case of primes 1 mod 4 versus 3 mod 4 had been studied by Euler and Legendre, and Chebyshev noticed in 1853 that primes 3 mod 4 seem to lead the race, a bias that was explained only in the twentieth century. The theorem is a cornerstone of analytic number theory.
