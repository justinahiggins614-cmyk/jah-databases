Sources of Islamic Tradition: Quran and Hadith Literature Rank 2 · simple

Islamic tradition draws on two principal categories of source, described here factually as categories, without quoting or inventing any passage. The Quran is the central religious text of Islam, held by Muslims to be the word of God as revealed, and it is the first source for Islamic belief and practice. Hadith literature is the body of reports about the sayings, actions, and approvals attributed to the Prophet, transmitted through chains of narrators and collected in works whose authenticity scholars grade and debate. This record describes these categories as a matter of public religious-studies fact, and it does not quote any passage: where a passage would be needed, the record paraphrases its general subject with attribution, or it says the passage is not quoted here. The distinction between the categories, and the scholarly grading of Hadith reports, is itself a fact about how Islamic scholarship works, not a ruling by this database.

Schools of Thought: An Overview Rank 5 · simple

Islamic law and practice are interpreted through schools of thought, and this record describes them neutrally, as a matter of public religious-studies fact, without presenting any school's view as settled for all Muslims. The four Sunni schools most commonly named in published references are the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali schools, each named after its founding jurist and each with its own methods of deriving rulings. Shia Islam has its own jurisprudential traditions, most prominently the Ja'fari school, and other communities, including Ibadi Islam, have their own traditions as well. Schools differ on details of ritual, including some details of Hajj and Umrah, and this suite's rule applies throughout: where schools differ, the record states that traditions differ, and it gives the general outline that published factual sources agree on. This record makes no sectarian claim, and it presents no school's ruling as the ruling of Islam as a whole.

Working demo program

import math
TITLE = 'Scholarship Centres in Mecca'
FROM_CITY = 'Umm Al-Qura University area, Mecca (reference)'
FROM_LAT = 21.3891
FROM_LON = 39.8579
MECCA_LAT = 21.4225
MECCA_LON = 39.8262
R = 6371.0
p1 = math.radians(FROM_LAT); p2 = math.radians(MECCA_LAT)
dp = math.radians(MECCA_LAT - FROM_LAT); dl = math.radians(MECCA_LON - FROM_LON)
a = math.sin(dp/2)**2 + math.cos(p1)*math.cos(p2)*math.sin(dl/2)**2
dist = 2*R*math.asin(math.sqrt(a))
y = math.sin(dl)*math.cos(p2); x = math.cos(p1)*math.sin(p2) - math.sin(p1)*math.cos(p2)*math.cos(dl)
bearing = (math.degrees(math.atan2(y, x)) + 360) % 360
print('Place: ' + TITLE)
print('From: ' + FROM_CITY)
print('Great-circle distance to Mecca: %.1f km' % dist)
print('Initial bearing to Mecca: %.1f degrees' % bearing)
print('Method: haversine great-circle arithmetic on published coordinates; road distance differs.')

The Islamic Calendar and Mecca Rank 8 · compounded

The Islamic calendar is a lunar calendar of twelve months, beginning with Muharram and ending with Dhul-Hijjah, and Mecca's pilgrimage year is set by it: Hajj falls in Dhul-Hijjah, Ramadan is the ninth month, and the festivals of Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha fall on the 1st of Shawwal and the 10th of Dhul-Hijjah respectively. Because the lunar year is about eleven days shorter than the solar year, Islamic months move through the solar seasons over about three decades, so the same Meccan festival falls in different seasons in different years. The start of each month depends on moon sighting and official announcement, so this record gives the calendar framework and its movement rule, and its demo converter is labelled as an arithmetic approximation, never as religious authority. This record is a fact record about a calendar system.

Ramadan in Mecca Rank 9 · compounded

Ramadan in Mecca is marked by fasting from dawn to sunset, by nightly Taraweeh prayers at the Grand Mosque, by iftar meals at sunset, and by a large increase in Umrah pilgrims, who come to Mecca in Ramadan in numbers that published descriptions describe as second only to Hajj. The Grand Mosque's Ramadan nights are among its most crowded, with prayers extending late into the night, and the last ten nights, including Laylat al-Qadr in tradition, draw still larger crowds. Published descriptions also note the provision of iftar meals and services for pilgrims and for the poor during the month. This record is a fact record about publicly observable practices and their scale; the spiritual significance of Ramadan is tradition, and the record presents it as tradition where it mentions it, without inventing doctrine about fasting rules, on which schools of thought differ in detail.

Friday Prayer at the Grand Mosque Rank 10 · compounded

Friday congregational prayer, Jumu'ah, at the Grand Mosque is the week's largest regular gathering in Mecca, with the mosque and its surrounding areas filling for the midday prayer and sermon. Published descriptions describe the sermon as delivered from the mosque, with its content varying by week, and the congregation as including Mecca residents, workers, and visitors, with numbers rising sharply in pilgrimage and Ramadan seasons. The Friday prayer replaces the midday prayer for those attending, according to published general descriptions, and its organisation, including crowd management and broadcast, is part of the mosque's regular weekly routine. This record is a fact record about a publicly observable weekly event; the religious rulings governing who must attend and what excuses attendance are matters on which schools of thought differ, and this general record does not settle them.

Preservation of Tradition Rank 12 · complex

Islamic tradition is preserved through several mechanisms that published references describe: the memorisation and recitation of the Quran, the transmission of Hadith through chains of narrators whose reliability scholars grade, the copying and now digitisation of manuscripts, and the teaching of students by scholars in mosque circles and universities. Each mechanism has its own checks: memorisation is checked against recitation, Hadith transmission is checked against narrator biographies and chains, manuscripts are checked against other copies, and teaching is checked by the student's own later teaching. This record is a fact record about publicly described preservation mechanisms; it does not claim that any particular text is perfectly preserved, because that claim belongs to theology and to scholarly debate, and this suite reports the mechanisms rather than settling the debate.

Glossary: Tradition Terms Rank 14 · complex

This glossary defines the tradition terms used across the Mecca scholarship and tradition pair, in plain language. The Quran is the central religious text of Islam. Hadith literature is the body of reports about sayings, actions, and approvals attributed to the Prophet. A school of thought, madhhab, is a jurisprudential tradition with its own methods. Ijtihad is independent legal reasoning by a qualified scholar, and a fatwa is a legal opinion given in answer to a question, which is not itself a law. Taraweeh is the nightly prayer performed in Ramadan, and Jumu'ah is the Friday congregational prayer. This record is a fact record of standard religious-studies usage, and where a term's meaning differs between schools, the glossary says traditions differ rather than giving one school's definition as universal.
