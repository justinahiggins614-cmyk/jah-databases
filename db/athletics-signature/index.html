Sprints Rank 1 · simple · running · beginner

The shortest races are also the simplest to understand and the hardest to master. From the blocks to the line, a sprinter has seconds to get everything right: a clean start, a powerful drive phase, and a relaxed top speed held to the finish. The 100 metres is the signature event, but the family runs from indoor 60 metres to the full-lap 400, where pacing joins power. What separates good sprinters from great ones is rarely effort; it is technique held together at maximum speed, when the body wants to tighten up. Train the start, train the speed, and train the calm.

History

Sprints have anchored athletics since the first modern Olympic Games in 1896, with the 100 metres as the best-known race in the sport.

Rules

Lane discipline applies across the sprint distances, false starts cost the race, and timing goes to hundredths of a second.

Techniques

The start, the drive phase, and relaxed speed are the three phases to train. Tension is the enemy at top speed.

Training

A week mixes starts, short accelerations, top-speed runs, and strength work, with full recovery between hard efforts.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 400
TIME_S = 48.0
print("400 metres pace example")
print("Distance: %d m, time: %.1f seconds" % (DISTANCE_M, TIME_S))
print("Average speed: %.2f m/s" % (DISTANCE_M / TIME_S))
print("Pace per 100 m: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 4.0))
print("Pace per 200 m: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 2.0))

Middle-Distance Running Rank 2 · simple · running · intermediate

The middle distances are where tactics and fitness collide. At 800 metres the race is two laps of controlled aggression; at 1500 metres it is nearly four laps of positioning, surges, and a final kick. Runners must be fast enough to sprint and fit enough to sustain it, and the best ones also read races: when to lead, when to sit, when to move. Sit too far back and the kick never reaches the leaders; lead too early and the field runs past in the straight. Training teaches the body to hold race pace while tired, which is the whole event in one sentence.

History

The middle distances grew from nineteenth-century mile racing into the Olympic 800 and 1500 metres, keeping their tactical character.

Rules

The 800 metres breaks from lanes after the first bend; the 1500 metres starts from a curved line with open positioning.

Techniques

Pacing, pack position, and kick timing decide races. The runner who moves at the right moment beats the runner who moves earliest.

Training

Race-pace intervals, tempo runs, and speed sessions build the blend of speed and endurance the events demand.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 1500
TIME_S = 240.0
print("1500 metres pace example")
print("Distance: %d m, time: %.1f seconds (4:00)" % (DISTANCE_M, TIME_S))
print("Pace per 400 m lap: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 3.75))
print("Pace per 1000 m: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 1.5))

Long-Distance Running Rank 3 · simple · running · intermediate

Long-distance running is the art of staying relaxed at speeds that should not be sustainable. The track events are 5000 and 10000 metres, and the road and country versions add hills, mud, and weather. Races are usually patient: even pacing, small surges, and a finish that only looks like a sprint because everyone is tired. The training is equally patient, built on daily mileage, one long run, and tempo work that teaches the body to hold a strong effort. Economy is everything; the runner who wastes least wins most.

History

Distance racing has anchored athletics since the early Olympic Games, with road and cross-country traditions running alongside the track events.

Rules

Waterfall starts and open positioning govern track distance races; road races use mass starts on measured courses.

Techniques

Stride economy, even pacing, and relaxed form under fatigue decide results. Surges test rivals without spending the race.

Training

Mileage, long runs, tempo efforts, and intervals form the weekly pattern, with recovery days protecting the load.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 10000
TIME_S = 1800.0
print("10000 metres pace example")
print("Distance: %d m, time: %.1f seconds (30:00)" % (DISTANCE_M, TIME_S))
print("Pace per 1000 m: %.1f seconds" % (TIME_S / 10.0))
print("Laps: %d, pace per lap: %.1f seconds" % (25, TIME_S / 25.0))

Hurdles Rank 4 · simple · running · intermediate

Hurdling is sprinting with obstacles, and the obstacles punish every technical flaw. Each race has ten hurdles: the short races are flat-out sprints over barriers, and the 400 metres version adds fatigue and stride management. The technique is a rhythm: a fixed stride count between hurdles, a fast lead leg, a trail leg snapped through low, and a body that stays low over the barrier. Hit a hurdle and it falls forward, costing time; run around one and the race is lost on rules. The best hurdlers look like they are barely hurdling at all.

History

Hurdle racing grew from obstacle races into standardised Olympic events with ten hurdles per race.

Rules

Hurdles are built to fall forward. Striking one costs time; avoiding one or deliberately clearing them away breaks the rules.

Techniques

Rhythm is everything: a set stride count, a quick lead leg, a fast trail leg, and minimal time in the air.

Training

Drills, rhythm runs, sprint work, and mobility training build the pattern until it survives fatigue.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_M = 400
HURDLES = 10
print("400 metres hurdles example")
print("Distance: %d m, hurdles: %d" % (DISTANCE_M, HURDLES))
print("Average gap between hurdles: %.1f m" % (DISTANCE_M / HURDLES))
print("Stride pattern example: 15 strides early, 17 late as fatigue changes stride length")

Steeplechase Rank 5 · simple · running · advanced

Add solid barriers and a water pit to a distance race and you get the steeplechase: 3000 metres, 28 barriers, and seven water jumps. The barriers do not fall, so runners must clear them cleanly, and the water jump rewards those who step on the barrier and drive far past the pit. It is a hybrid event: part distance race, part hurdling, part obstacle negotiation. Rhythm matters most; a runner who fights the barriers wastes the energy needed for the final lap. The race usually comes down to who is still smooth after the last water jump.

History

The event takes its name from cross-country races run between church steeples, later standardised to 3000 metres on the track.

Rules

Twenty-eight barriers and seven water jumps must be cleared. Stepping on barriers is allowed; bypassing them is not.

Techniques

Smooth barrier clearance and a long water-jump landing save time and energy. Rhythm between obstacles is the key skill.

Training

Distance work, hurdle drills, and repeated water-jump practice build the hybrid fitness the event needs.

Working demo program

WATER_DEPTH_CM = 70
print("Water jump example")
print("Water pit depth at the barrier: about %d cm, shallowing toward the exit" % WATER_DEPTH_CM)
print("Goal: land as far past the water as possible to avoid deep-water running")
print("Water jumps per race: 7, one per lap")

Relays Rank 6 · compounded · running · intermediate

Relays turn individual speed into a team skill. Four runners, one baton, and exchanges inside a marked zone: that is the whole event, and it is where medals are won and lost. In the sprint relay the exchanges happen at full speed, with the outgoing runner moving before the baton arrives. In the longer relay there is more time, but the runners are tired, which makes clean exchanges harder, not easier. A dropped baton or an exchange outside the zone ends the race on rules. The best relay teams practise exchanges until they are automatic.

History

Relays joined the Olympic programme in the early twentieth century and became the team centrepiece of every major championships.

Rules

The baton must change hands inside the zone. Drops, out-of-zone exchanges, and lane infringements can disqualify a team.

Techniques

Timing the outgoing runner's start, taking the baton without looking, and ordering legs by strength decide results.

Training

Exchange drills under pressure matter as much as sprint training; teams rehearse until exchanges are automatic.

Working demo program

LEGS = [48.5, 47.9, 48.2, 47.6]
print("4x400 metres relay example")
for i, t in enumerate(LEGS, 1):
    print("Leg %d: %.1f seconds" % (i, t))
print("Total: %.1f seconds (%.2f minutes)" % (sum(LEGS), sum(LEGS) / 60.0))
print("Fastest leg: %.1f seconds" % min(LEGS))

Marathon Rank 7 · compounded · running · advanced

The marathon is 42.195 kilometres of patience. The legend behind it is a messenger's run from Marathon to Athens; the reality is months of training distilled into one morning. Fuel is the hidden contest: stored energy runs low after about 30 kilometres, so runners drink and eat on the move and pace to survive the final stretch. Start too fast and the last ten kilometres punish every early second gained. Mass races now welcome tens of thousands of runners, but the event's logic never changes: respect the distance early, and it may respect you late.

History

The event recalls a legendary run to Athens, entered the first modern Games in 1896, and settled at 42.195 kilometres.

Rules

Certified measured courses and mass starts govern road marathons, with timing mats along the route.

Techniques

Pacing discipline and mid-race fuelling decide marathons. The runner who saves energy early has it late.

Training

Long runs, tempo work, and careful recovery build over months; the longest training runs usually stop short of race distance.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_KM = 42.195
PACE_MIN_PER_KM = 4.0
print("Marathon finish estimate")
print("Pace: %.1f minutes per kilometre" % PACE_MIN_PER_KM)
print("Finish time: %.1f minutes (%.2f hours)" % (DISTANCE_KM * PACE_MIN_PER_KM, DISTANCE_KM * PACE_MIN_PER_KM / 60.0))
print("10 km split: %.1f minutes" % (10 * PACE_MIN_PER_KM))

Race Walking Rank 8 · compounded · running · intermediate

Race walking looks simple and is anything but: one foot must stay on the ground, and the supporting leg must stay straight until it passes beneath the body. Judges watch every athlete, and warnings accumulate into disqualification. The speeds surprise newcomers; elite walkers cover 20 kilometres in well under 90 minutes without ever visibly running. The technique is a hip-driven stride with rapid turnover, and it breaks down under fatigue, which is exactly when judges watch hardest. It is a discipline of form held together for hours.

History

The discipline grew from pedestrian contests into Olympic racing, keeping its two defining rules: contact and a straight supporting leg.

Rules

Judges enforce contact and leg-straightness rules. Repeated violations bring disqualification.

Techniques

Hip drive, straight-leg action, and quick turnover generate speed legally. Form under fatigue is the deciding factor.

Training

Long walks and drills build the stride; strength work supports the hips and legs through long races.

Working demo program

DISTANCE_KM = 20
SPEED_KMH = 14.0
print("Race walk time estimate")
print("Distance: %d km, speed: %.1f km/h" % (DISTANCE_KM, SPEED_KMH))
print("Finish time: %.1f minutes" % (DISTANCE_KM / SPEED_KMH * 60.0))
print("5 km split: %.1f minutes" % (5 / SPEED_KMH * 60.0))

Long Jump Rank 9 · compounded · jumping · intermediate

Sprint, take off, fly, land: the long jump turns runway speed into distance. The measurement runs from the take-off board to the nearest landing mark, so accuracy at the board matters as much as speed. In the air, jumpers use hang or hitch-kick techniques to stay balanced and set up a feet-first landing that falls forward, protecting every centimetre. Cross the board and the jump fouls, however far it goes. Several attempts per competition mean a jumper can build through rounds, but the best legal jump is the only one that counts.

History

Jumping for distance is among the oldest athletics events, present at the first modern Games in 1896.

Rules

The board is the line: take off behind it or the jump fouls. Measurement goes to the nearest mark left in the sand.

Techniques

Speed on the runway, precision at the board, balance in the air, and a forward landing all add distance.

Training

Sprint work, take-off drills, bounding, and strength training build the jump.

Working demo program

ATTEMPTS = [7.82, 7.95, 0, 8.02, 7.90, 8.10]
print("Long jump series example (0 = foul)")
legal = [a for a in ATTEMPTS if a > 0]
print("Legal jumps: %s" % legal)
print("Best jump: %.2f m" % max(legal))
print("Average legal jump: %.2f m" % (sum(legal) / len(legal)))

Triple Jump Rank 10 · compounded · jumping · advanced

Hop, step, jump: three phases, one continuous movement, and a landing in the sand. The triple jump used to carry its old name openly, and the name still describes it perfectly. The skill is balance between phases: a hop that is too long wrecks the step, and a step that is too long wrecks the jump. Speed matters, but rhythm matters more, because the phases must flow without braking. Measurement matches the long jump, from board to nearest mark, and board fouls cost jumps. It is the most technical of the horizontal jumps.

History

The event dates to the first modern Games in 1896 under its older name, the hop, step, and jump.

Rules

The sequence is fixed: hop, step, jump. Board fouls do not count, and measurement goes to the nearest sand mark.

Techniques

Balanced phases and unbroken rhythm win. Overreaching on the hop destroys the phases that follow.

Training

Bounding, phase drills, sprint work, and strength training build the rhythm and power the event needs.

Working demo program

ATTEMPTS = [16.20, 16.45, 0, 16.60, 16.38, 16.72]
print("Triple jump series example (0 = foul)")
legal = [a for a in ATTEMPTS if a > 0]
print("Legal jumps: %s" % legal)
print("Best jump: %.2f m" % max(legal))
print("Improvement from first to best: %.2f m" % (max(legal) - legal[0]))

High Jump Rank 11 · complex · jumping · intermediate

Run a curve, plant one foot, and arch backwards over a bar: the high jump's modern technique, the Fosbury flop, won Olympic gold in 1968 and changed the event forever. The trick is physics: by arching head- and shoulders-first, the jumper's body passes over the bar while its centre of mass passes under it. Competitions raise the bar in stages, with three attempts per height and elimination after three straight failures. Jumpers may pass heights to save energy for bigger ones. Speed on the curve, power at take-off, and timing in the arch decide who clears what.

History

The event dates to the first modern Games in 1896. The flop technique arrived in 1968 and became standard within a decade.

Rules

Three attempts per height, elimination after three consecutive failures, and the bar rising in stages govern competitions.

Techniques

A curved run-up, a powerful plant, and a timed back arch carry the jumper over. The centre of mass passes under the bar.

Training

Curve sprinting, take-off drills, flexibility, and strength work build the jump.

Working demo program

ATTEMPTS = {"2.00": "cleared first try", "2.05": "cleared third try", "2.10": "three failures"}
print("High jump card example")
for height, result in ATTEMPTS.items():
    print("%s m: %s" % (height, result))
print("Final result: 2.05 m, eliminated at 2.10 m")

Pole Vault Rank 12 · complex · jumping · advanced

Sprint with a pole, plant it, ride its bend, and fly over a bar metres up: the pole vault is the most spectacular jump in athletics. Modern poles of fibreglass and carbon fibre are chosen by length and stiffness to fit the vaulter's speed and weight, and the pole's recoil does much of the lifting. The movement has distinct phases: plant, swing, rock-back, turn, and push-off, each timed to the pole's bend. Competitions raise the bar in stages with three attempts per height. Speed, strength, timing, and nerve all matter, and no other jump depends so much on equipment matched to athlete.

History

The vault grew from practical pole crossing into Olympic competition, transformed by flexible fibreglass poles in the twentieth century.

Rules

Three attempts per height, elimination after three consecutive failures, and a rising bar govern competitions.

Techniques

The vault is a sequence: plant, swing, rock-back, turn, push-off. Pole stiffness and length must fit the vaulter.

Training

Sprint work, pole drills, gymnastics strength, and short vaults build the event.

Working demo program

BAR_M = 5.80
APPROACH_M = 40
print("Pole vault approach example")
print("Bar: %.2f m, runway: %d m" % (BAR_M, APPROACH_M))
print("Vaulter must clear a bar higher than the grip height using pole recoil")
print("Attempts per height: 3")

Shot Put Rank 13 · complex · throwing · intermediate

Push a heavy ball as far as you can, from inside a circle, without leaving it: that is the shot put. Men throw 7.26 kilograms and women 4 kilograms, and the ball must stay against the neck and shoulder until release. Two techniques dominate: the glide, sliding back across the circle, and the rotation, spinning to build speed before the push. Step outside the circle or over the stop board and the throw fouls. Power comes from the legs and hips, driven through a braced body into one explosive push. Several attempts decide it; the best legal throw wins.

History

The event grew from stone putting into Olympic competition with standard weights: 7.26 kilograms for men, 4 kilograms for women.

Rules

The shot must remain at the neck until release, and the thrower must stay inside the circle.

Techniques

Glide or rotation builds speed; the release is a braced push, not a throw with the arm alone.

Training

Throwing practice, heavy lifting, and explosive drills build the power the event needs.

Working demo program

THROWS = [19.20, 19.85, 0, 20.10, 19.95, 20.30]

print("Legal throws: %s" % legal)
print("Best throw: %.2f m" % max(legal))
print("Average legal throw: %.2f m" % (sum(legal) / len(legal)))

Discus Throw Rank 14 · complex · throwing · intermediate

Spin across a circle and let a disc fly: the discus is the oldest throwing event still contested, and its beauty is rhythm. Men throw 2 kilograms, women 1 kilogram, and the discus must land inside a marked sector to count. The thrower builds speed through one and a half turns, keeping the discus flat so the air lifts it, then releases at an angle that carries it furthest. A cage surrounds the circle for safety, because a loose discus is dangerous. Balance through the spin matters more than brute strength; the smoothest throwers throw furthest.

History

The discus is the oldest throwing event in the modern Games, present since 1896 and rooted in ancient competition.

Rules

Sector landing, circle discipline, and standard weights govern the event. Throws outside the sector foul.

Techniques

Rhythm through the spin, a flat discus, and a clean release carry the throw. Balance beats brute force.

Training

Throwing practice, spin drills, and strength work build the event.

Hammer Throw Rank 15 · complex · throwing · advanced

A metal ball on a wire, spun three or four times and released: the hammer throw is the most violent-looking event in athletics, and also one of the most controlled. Men throw 7.26 kilograms, women 4 kilograms, and the hammer orbits the thrower, pulling hard on arms and core. The thrower must stay balanced and relaxed through the turns, then release at exactly the right moment. A cage surrounds the circle, and sector rules apply as in the other throws. The winners are not the strongest athletes in the field; they are the ones who stay smooth while the hammer tries to pull them off their feet.

History

The event grew from Celtic heavy events into Olympic competition with a standardised wire hammer.

Rules

Sector landing and circle discipline govern throws; weights match the shot put at 7.26 and 4 kilograms.

Techniques

Balance through three or four turns and a timed release decide throws. Relaxation under the hammer's pull is essential.

Training

Throwing practice, turning drills, and core strength build the event.

Javelin Throw Rank 16 · complex · throwing · advanced

Run, cross-step, and throw a spear further than anyone else: the javelin is the only throwing event from a runway, and it blends sprinting with throwing. Men throw 800 grams, women 600 grams, and the javelin must land tip-first inside the sector to count. The run-up builds speed, the cross-step sets the body, and the release over the shoulder sends the implement point-first into flight. Cross the foul line and the throw is lost. The best throwers are fast on the runway and clean at release; power alone, without timing, sends the javelin flat and short.

History

The event descends from ancient spear throwing and has been Olympic since the early modern Games, with standardised implements.

Rules

Tip-first sector landings count; foul-line crossings do not. Weights are 800 grams for men and 600 grams for women.

Techniques

Runway speed, cross-step timing, and a clean release decide throws. A flat landing wastes the whole effort.

Training

Throwing practice, sprint work, and shoulder strength build the event.
