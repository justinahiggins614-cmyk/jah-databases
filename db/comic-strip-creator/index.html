A Three-Panel Joke That Lands Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: buildable-now Three-panel joke structure is established practice in comics today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a comic strip creator that builds a joke in three panels: a setup, a turn, and a punchline, read left to right. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

A joke told in the wrong order does not land: the punchline comes first and the setup comes last, the reader is confused instead of amused, and the comic fails at the one thing it was made to do. A creator that builds every joke in three panels, with the setup on the left, the turn in the middle, and the punchline on the right, gives the joke its order back: the reader follows the story panel by panel, the turn surprises them, and the punchline lands. The three-panel shape is the oldest joke shape in comics, and the creator keeps it, because it works: setup, turn, punchline, and nothing else.

Worked example: a three-panel joke about a pencil has the pencil on the road in panel one, the question in panel two, and the answer, to draw a line, in panel three.

History

Three-panel jokes are as old as newspaper comics, where a joke had to fit in a strip three pictures wide and land before the reader turned the page. The creator keeps that old shape, because it still works.

Proposed solution

Split the joke into three parts: setup, turn, and punchline; Give each part its own panel, read left to right; Keep the setup short, so the punchline has room; Put the punchline in the last panel, and nowhere else; Test the joke on a reader who has not seen it before.

Dependencies

A three-panel shape; a setup, a turn, and a punchline; a left-to-right order; a fresh reader to test the joke.

Feasibility

Verification path

Show a three-panel joke to five people who have not seen it and check that at least four laugh or smile at the last panel; check that no one needed the order explained.

Feasibility: buildable-now Bubble ordering by reading order is established practice in comics today; only the design stands in the way.

A design for a comic strip creator that places speech bubbles so they read in the right order, top to bottom and left to right, without confusion. This is a design for a website that has not been built yet, not a real product.

A comic with bubbles in the wrong order cannot be read: the answer comes before the question, the joke is spoiled, and the reader has to guess which bubble to read first. The maker places bubbles by feel, and the feel is often wrong, especially when two characters speak in one panel. A creator that places bubbles in reading order, top to bottom and left to right, with a tail that points clearly to the speaker, and checks the order before export, removes the guessing: the reader follows the bubbles in order, the conversation makes sense, and the joke lands. The order is the comic's grammar, and the creator enforces it.

Worked example: a panel with two speakers has the first bubble at the top left and the second below it, each tail pointing to its speaker, and the conversation reads in order.

History

Speech bubbles have read top to bottom and left to right since the first comics, because that is how readers read. The creator enforces the old order, so the conversation always makes sense.

Proposed solution

Place bubbles in reading order: top to bottom, left to right; Point each bubble's tail clearly at its speaker; Keep one bubble per thought, and keep it short; Check the order before export, and fix any bubble out of place; Let the maker preview the reading order with numbered bubbles.

Dependencies

A reading-order rule for bubbles; tails that point to speakers; one short thought per bubble; a numbered preview of the order.

History

Field guide indexes descend from the tables of contents of books and the catalogues of libraries, where a list of what a collection holds came first and the collection itself came second; this index is the same old habit, written for a database of designs.

Proposed solution

List every record by number, in ladder order; Give each record a one-line account of what it covers; Mark each record simple, compounded, or complex; Point simple readers to records 1 to 8, and advanced readers to records 17 to 23; Keep the index beside the records, never apart from them.
