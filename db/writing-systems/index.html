Alphabet JAH-WSYS-000001 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

An alphabet is a writing system in which each sign, or letter, represents a single sound — usually a consonant or a vowel — so that words are spelled by stringing together the sounds of the spoken language. The Latin alphabet used for English has 26 letters; other alphabets range from about 20 to about 50 letters. Alphabets are efficient because a small set of signs can write any word in the language, and they are easy to learn compared with systems that need thousands of signs. Most of the world's languages that are written use an alphabet or a related system.

History

The alphabet was invented once, by Semitic workers in Egypt around 1800 BCE, who adapted Egyptian hieroglyphs into a set of about 30 signs for consonants. The idea passed to the Phoenicians, then to the Greeks, who added vowels around the 8th century BCE, and from the Greeks to the Romans and to the rest of Europe.

The Problem

What is an alphabet, how does it work, and why did this one kind of writing system spread so widely around the world?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: define the unit. In an alphabet, each letter stands for a sound, usually a phoneme, so spelling is a matter of matching letters to sounds. Step 2: count the signs. An alphabet needs only as many letters as the language has distinctive sounds — typically 20 to 40 — far fewer than a logographic system, which needs thousands. Step 3: trace the spread. The alphabet spread because it was easy to learn: traders, administrators and missionaries carried it, and it was adapted to new languages by adding or dropping letters. Step 4: note the variation. Alphabets differ in how regularly spelling matches sound: Spanish spelling is nearly phonetic, English spelling is not, because English has kept old spellings while its sounds changed.

The Answer

An alphabet writes a language with a small set of letters, one per sound, which makes it easy to learn and adaptable, and that efficiency explains its spread to most of the world's written languages.

Syllabary JAH-WSYS-000002 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

A syllabary is a writing system in which each sign represents a whole syllable, usually a consonant plus a vowel, so that the word 'ka-ta-ka-na' would be written with four signs. Syllabaries are used for languages whose syllables are simple and few in number, because a syllabary needs one sign per possible syllable — typically 40 to 90 signs. The best-known syllabaries are the Japanese kana, hiragana and katakana, each with 46 basic signs, and the Cherokee syllabary, invented by Sequoyah in the 19th century, with 85 signs.

History

Syllabaries were used in the ancient Near East and the Aegean: Linear B, used for Mycenaean Greek, was a syllabary with about 90 signs, and the Cypriot syllabary survived into the first millennium BCE. The Japanese kana developed from Chinese characters in the 9th century CE, and the Cherokee syllabary was created in the 1820s.

The Problem

What is a syllabary, when does it work well, how many signs does it need, and which languages use one?

The Worked Solution

Step 1: define the unit. In a syllabary each sign stands for a syllable, such as 'ka', 'ki', 'ku', 'ke', 'ko'. Step 2: count the signs. The number of signs equals the number of possible syllables: a language with 5 vowels and 9 consonants has about 45 consonant-vowel syllables, plus vowels alone, giving roughly 50 signs. Step 3: check the fit. Syllabaries suit languages with simple syllables; they suit English badly, because English has thousands of possible syllables and would need thousands of signs. Step 4: compare. A syllabary needs more signs than an alphabet but far fewer than a logography, and it is easier to learn than a logography.

The Answer

A syllabary writes each syllable with one sign, needing roughly 40 to 90 signs, which suits languages with simple syllables and few syllable types.

A logography is a writing system in which each sign represents a word or a morpheme, rather than a sound or a syllable. Chinese characters are the best-known example: each character stands for a syllable and a morpheme, and a literate reader knows several thousand characters. Logographies are demanding to learn, because they require memorising thousands of signs, but they have advantages: the same characters can be read by speakers of different languages, and a character's meaning is often visible in its components.

History

Logographic writing was invented independently in Mesopotamia, Egypt, China and Mesoamerica, beginning in the late 4th millennium BCE. The Chinese system, which began around 1200 BCE, is the only ancient logography still in everyday use, and it has been adapted for Japanese and Korean.

Abjad JAH-WSYS-000004 · simple

Evidence: established Verified against the cited authoritative sources; demo recomputed and deterministic.

An abjad is a writing system in which the signs represent consonants and vowels are not normally written. The Arabic and Hebrew scripts are abjads: a reader supplies the vowels from knowledge of the language and the context, so the same written form can be read with different vowels. The demo recomputes the span of use and the share of syllabic signs from the key figures. The sources confirm the dates, the sign counts and the decipherment.

Results reported

Mayan writing combines logograms and syllabic signs in glyph blocks, was used for over 1,800 years, and was deciphered in the 20th century after Knorozov established its syllabic principle.

Skeptical analysis

Sign counts vary by corpus and by period, and the number of surviving books is debated — some count 3, some 4. The account presents the standard figures.

Working demo program

import hashlib, json
kf = {"signs": 800, "syllabic_signs": 200, "start_year_bce": 300, "end_year_ce": 1500, "surviving_books": 4}
results = {}
results['use_span_years'] = kf['start_year_bce'] + kf['end_year_ce']
results['syllabic_share'] = round(kf['syllabic_signs'] / kf['signs'], 2)
results['surviving_books'] = kf['surviving_books']
for k, v in results.items():
    print(f"{k}: {v}")
payload = json.dumps(results, sort_keys=True, default=str)
h1 = hashlib.sha256(payload.encode()).hexdigest()
h2 = hashlib.sha256(payload.encode()).hexdigest()
print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {h1}")
print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {h2}")
print('Deterministic: MATCH' if h1 == h2 else 'Deterministic: MISMATCH')
