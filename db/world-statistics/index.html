What Statistics Is

ID: JAH-STTC-000001 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Statistics is the science of collecting, organising, analysing, and interpreting data, and of drawing conclusions under uncertainty. It gives us tools to summarise what we have observed, to judge whether a pattern is real or just chance, and to make predictions with a stated degree of confidence. Statistics has two broad branches: descriptive statistics, which summarises data, and inferential statistics, which uses a sample to say something about a larger population. It is used in every field that deals with data, from medicine and economics to sport and engineering.

History: People have counted and recorded data for thousands of years: censuses appear in ancient Egypt, China, and Rome, and the Domesday Book of 1086 surveyed England's land and people. The science began in the 17th century, when John Graunt studied London's bills of mortality in 1662 and founded demography, and when Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat worked out the mathematics of probability in 1654. In the 18th century, Thomas Bayes described how to update beliefs with evidence, and Pierre-Simon Laplace developed probability theory further. The 19th century brought the normal distribution, least squares, and correlation, through the work of Carl Friedrich Gauss, Adolphe Quetelet, and Francis Galton. In the 20th century, Ronald Fisher founded modern statistical inference, Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson developed hypothesis testing, and the computer made it possible to analyse data sets of a size earlier statisticians could not have imagined. The 20th century made statistics a profession: national statistical offices were founded, university departments were created, and the Royal Statistical Society, founded in 1834, and the American Statistical Association, founded in 1839, grew into large professional bodies. In the 1970s, John Tukey championed exploratory data analysis, arguing that a statistician should look at the data before modelling it. In the 1990s and 2000s, the rise of the internet and of cheap computing created data sets of a size that earlier statisticians could not have imagined, and the field merged with computer science into data science. Today, statistics is practised in every country and every industry, and its methods are taught in schools as well as universities.

Key concepts: A population is the whole group being studied, and a sample is the part of it that is actually measured. A parameter is a number describing a population, such as its mean, while a statistic is the corresponding number computed from a sample. Variation is the fact that measurements differ from one another, and it is the central problem of statistics: without variation, there would be nothing to study. Bias is a systematic error that pushes results in one direction, while random error is the noise that averages out with more data. Uncertainty is quantified, not ignored: a good statistical conclusion states how sure we are, for example with a confidence interval or a probability. A variable is a characteristic that can take different values, such as height or income, and an observation is one recorded value of a variable. The distribution of a variable describes how its values are spread, and its shape — symmetric, skewed, or bimodal — decides which summaries are appropriate. A model is a simplified description of how data were generated, and it is the bridge between the sample and the population. Finally, inference is the process of drawing a conclusion about a population from a sample, with a stated measure of uncertainty.

How it works: Statistics works by turning a question into data, then turning data into a conclusion with a measure of uncertainty. First the question is made precise: what is being measured, and in whom? Then data are collected, ideally by a method that gives every member of the population a known chance of being included, so the sample represents the population. The data are summarised with tables, graphs, and numbers such as the mean and the spread. Then a model is fitted or a test is run, and the result is reported with its uncertainty. The final step is interpretation: what the numbers mean in the real world, and what they do not prove. A statistical analysis that skips any step — a biased sample, a wrong model, an overstated conclusion — is unreliable. The reporting is part of the method: a statistical result is reported with the sample size, the method, and the uncertainty, so that the reader can judge how much to trust it. A result without its uncertainty is incomplete, and a result without its method cannot be checked. The whole process is iterative: an analysis often reveals a problem with the data or the question, and the analyst returns to an earlier step and corrects it. This is why statistics is described as a cycle rather than a straight line.

Worked examples: A school wants to know the average height of its pupils. Measuring every pupil would be a census; measuring a random sample of fifty and computing their mean height gives an estimate, and the spread of the sample lets the school say how precise that estimate is. A drug trial compares the recovery rate of patients who received a new treatment with those who received a placebo, and statistics judges whether the difference is larger than chance would produce. A factory measures the diameter of a sample of bolts to check whether the production line is still on target. A pollster interviews a thousand voters and reports that a candidate leads by three points, with a margin of error of three points. A fifth example is a weather forecast: the probability of rain is reported as a percentage, because the forecast is a statistical prediction with a stated uncertainty, not a certainty.

Real applications: Statistics is used wherever decisions depend on data. In medicine, it designs trials and analyses their results. In government, it runs censuses, surveys, and economic indicators. In business, it forecasts sales, measures quality, and tests advertising. In science, it separates real effects from noise in experiments. In sport, it measures performance and predicts outcomes. In everyday life, it underlies weather forecasts, opinion polls, and the recommendations of streaming services. The common thread is uncertainty: statistics does not remove it, but it measures it, so that decisions can be made with open eyes. Statistics also underlies quality control in manufacturing, where samples of a production run are measured to judge the whole run, and it underlies the auditing of financial accounts, where a sample of transactions is checked to judge the whole set of books.

Common questions and misconceptions: A common question is whether statistics can prove something. It cannot prove a claim in the way mathematics proves a theorem; it can only show that the evidence is strong or weak, and by how much. People ask whether statistics is the same as lying with numbers; it is not, though numbers can be misused, which is why the record on misleading statistics exists. Another misconception is that a large sample guarantees a correct answer — a large biased sample is still biased, and it can be confidently wrong. Some ask whether statistics is only about numbers; it is also about design, judgement, and communication, because a badly designed study produces numbers that mean nothing. People also ask whether statistics is a branch of mathematics. It uses mathematics, but it is a distinct science, because it deals with data, design, and judgement in a way that pure mathematics does not. Another question is whether a statistician needs to know the subject matter; the answer is yes, because the statistics must fit the problem, and a method that is technically correct can still be wrong for the question being asked.

Data and Variables

ID: JAH-STTC-000002 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Data are recorded observations, and a variable is a characteristic that can take different values from one observation to another. Height, age, income, and the colour of a car are all variables. Data come in types: quantitative data are numbers, such as height in centimetres, while qualitative or categorical data are labels, such as eye colour. Variables are also classified by their scale of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio. Getting the type right matters, because it decides which summaries and tests are valid.

History: The idea of classifying data by type developed with the science of measurement. In the 19th century, Adolphe Quetelet used measurements of people to show that human traits follow a bell-shaped distribution, and Francis Galton studied how traits are inherited. In the 1940s, the psychologist Stanley Smith Stevens proposed the four scales of measurement — nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio — in a paper published in 1946, and the classification has been standard ever since. The rise of computing in the 1960s and 1970s made large data sets common, and with them came the need for careful data management: cleaning, coding, and documenting data so that analyses could be checked. Today, data come from surveys, sensors, administrative records, and the web, and the first step of any analysis is still the same: understand what each variable is and how it was measured. The management of data became a discipline of its own in the 20th century. In the 1960s, the first large computerised data sets were created, and with them came the need for careful documentation: a codebook that records what each variable is, how it was measured, and what its values mean. In the 1980s, the study of missing data developed, with Donald Rubin showing how to think about why data are missing and how to handle it. In the 1990s and 2000s, the growth of administrative data — records collected by governments and companies for other purposes — created new opportunities and new problems, because such data were not collected for research and may be biased. Today, data management is recognised as a core part of statistics, and the principle that data should be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable is widely adopted.

Key concepts: A quantitative variable takes numerical values, and it can be discrete, taking only whole numbers such as the number of children, or continuous, taking any value in a range such as height. A categorical variable takes labels, such as sex or nationality. The nominal scale names categories with no order, such as blood type; the ordinal scale orders them, such as a rating from poor to excellent; the interval scale has equal steps but no true zero, such as temperature in Celsius; and the ratio scale has a true zero, such as weight. A data set is a table of observations, with each row an observation and each column a variable. Missing data are values that were not recorded, and how they are handled can change the result. The unit of observation is the thing being measured, such as a person, a household, or a firm, and it must be defined before data are collected. A data set is a table in which each row is an observation and each column is a variable, and the shape of the table — how many rows and columns — is the first thing an analyst checks. A codebook is the document that describes every variable, its coding, and its source, and without it a data set is uninterpretable. An outlier is an observation that is far from the others, and it may be an error, or it may be the most interesting observation in the data.

How it works: Working with data starts by defining each variable: what it measures, what values it can take, and what unit it is in. The data are then collected and recorded, ideally with a codebook that documents every variable and its coding. The data are cleaned: errors are found and corrected, impossible values are flagged, and missing values are counted. Then each variable is summarised on its own — a count and a bar chart for a categorical variable, a mean and a histogram for a quantitative one — before any relationship between variables is examined. This order matters: a summary of a variable that was coded wrongly, or that contains impossible values, will mislead everything that follows. The cleaning is done carefully: a value that is impossible, such as a negative age, is flagged and investigated, not silently deleted, because it may reveal a problem with the collection or the coding. A value that is missing is counted and its pattern is examined: if the missing values are concentrated in one group, the analysis may be biased. The data are then documented, so that another analyst can repeat the work. This stage is slow and unglamorous, but it is the foundation of everything that follows, and a study that skips it is built on sand.

Worked examples: A hospital records each patient's age in years, sex, blood pressure in millimetres of mercury, and whether they smoke. Age and blood pressure are quantitative and continuous; sex and smoking status are categorical. A survey asks people to rate a service from one to five: that is ordinal data, because five is better than four but not necessarily twice as good. A weather station records the temperature each hour in degrees Celsius, which is interval data, and the rainfall in millimetres, which is ratio data, because zero millimetres means no rain. A shop's sales records show the number of items sold each day, which is discrete quantitative data. Another example is a clinical trial, where each patient has variables for age, sex, treatment group, and outcome, and the data set is a table with one row per patient and one column per variable, plus a codebook that defines each one.

Real applications: Understanding data and variables is the foundation of every analysis. In medicine, it decides whether a patient's age, a blood test result, and a diagnosis are recorded in a way that can be analysed. In business, it decides whether sales figures, customer ratings, and product categories can be compared. In government, it decides whether census data can be used to plan services. In science, it decides whether an experiment's measurements are trustworthy. The same ideas underlie modern data science: a machine-learning model is only as good as the variables it is given, and a variable that is mismeasured or misclassified will mislead the model. Understanding data and variables also matters in machine learning, where the choice of variables — called features — decides what a model can learn, and in data journalism, where a reporter must understand a data set before writing about it. In every field, the quality of the analysis depends on the quality of the data and on whether the analyst understands what each variable means.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask whether numbers are always better than categories. They are not: a category such as a diagnosis can carry information that no number captures, and forcing it into a number can destroy the meaning. A common misconception is that an average is meaningful for any variable; averaging a nominal variable such as blood type is nonsense, and averaging an ordinal rating is questionable, because the steps may not be equal. Another question is what to do with missing data; the honest answer is to report how much is missing and why, and to check whether the missingness itself carries information. Some ask whether data can be too big; it can, if the size tempts an analyst to skip cleaning and checking. People also ask whether data can be too clean. They can, if cleaning removes real variation: a value that looks like an error may be a genuine extreme, and deleting it can bias the result. Another question is whether the scale of measurement really matters; it does, because it decides which operations are meaningful, and a method applied to the wrong scale can produce nonsense. Some ask whether big data makes these issues disappear; it does not, because big data are often collected for another purpose and can be badly biased.

Descriptive Statistics

ID: JAH-STTC-000003 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Descriptive statistics summarise and describe a set of data, without trying to generalise beyond it. The main tools are measures of centre, such as the mean, median, and mode; measures of spread, such as the range, the interquartile range, and the standard deviation; and graphs, such as histograms, box plots, and bar charts. Descriptive statistics are the first step of any analysis: before testing a hypothesis or fitting a model, a good analyst looks at the data and summarises what is there.

History: Descriptive statistics are as old as counting itself, but the modern tools developed in the 19th and 20th centuries. The mean and the normal distribution were studied by Carl Friedrich Gauss and Adolphe Quetelet in the 1800s. The median and the quartile were popularised by Francis Galton and later by statisticians working on income and social data. In the 1970s, John Tukey invented the box plot and championed exploratory data analysis — the idea that a statistician should look at the data, with graphs and simple summaries, before fitting any model. Tukey's book "Exploratory Data Analysis", published in 1977, changed how the subject is taught. Today, descriptive statistics are produced automatically by software, but the judgement of which summary to use, and what it means, remains human. The tools of descriptive statistics continued to develop in the 20th century. In the 1970s, John Tukey introduced the five-number summary — the minimum, the first quartile, the median, the third quartile, and the maximum — and the box plot that displays it. In the 1980s, the study of robust statistics developed, with methods that are not distorted by a few extreme values. In the 1990s, the growth of computing made it easy to compute descriptive statistics for large data sets, and to draw graphs automatically. Today, descriptive statistics are the first output of any analysis, and they are reported in the first table of almost every scientific paper, so that the reader can see who was studied and what the data look like before any test is run.

Key concepts: The mean is the sum of the values divided by how many there are, and it is the most familiar measure of centre. The median is the middle value when the data are ordered, and it is less affected by extreme values than the mean. The mode is the most frequent value. The range is the difference between the largest and smallest values. The interquartile range is the spread of the middle half of the data, from the first quartile to the third. The variance is the average squared distance from the mean, and the standard deviation is its square root, which is in the same units as the data. A percentile is the value below which a given percentage of the data falls. The five-number summary is the minimum, the first quartile, the median, the third quartile, and the maximum, and it gives a compact description of a distribution. The interquartile range is the difference between the third and first quartiles, and it measures the spread of the middle half of the data. Skewness measures the asymmetry of a distribution: a right-skewed distribution has a long tail to the right, as income does, while a left-skewed one has a long tail to the left. An outlier is an observation that lies far from the others, and it is often defined as a value more than one and a half interquartile ranges beyond the quartiles.

How it works: Descriptive statistics are computed in a fixed order: first the data are checked for errors and missing values, then a graph is drawn — a histogram for a quantitative variable, a bar chart for a categorical one — because a graph can reveal shape, clusters, and outliers that numbers hide. Then the centre and the spread are computed. The choice of summary depends on the shape: for a symmetric distribution, the mean and standard deviation are appropriate; for a skewed distribution, such as income, the median and interquartile range are better, because the mean is pulled toward the long tail. The summary is reported with the sample size, so the reader knows how much data it rests on. The graph is drawn before the numbers are computed, because a graph can reveal a shape that the numbers hide: two peaks, a cluster, or an outlier that would distort the mean. The choice of summary follows the shape: for a symmetric distribution, the mean and standard deviation are used; for a skewed one, the median and interquartile range; for a categorical variable, the counts and percentages. The summary is reported with the sample size, so the reader knows how much data it rests on, and with the units, so the numbers mean something.

Worked examples: The monthly salaries in a small company are 2200, 2400, 2500, 2600, 2700, 2900, 3200, 4500, and 15000. The mean is about 4389, but the median is 2700, and the median is the better summary, because one very high salary pulls the mean upward. The heights of ten pupils cluster around 170 centimetres with a standard deviation of about five, which tells us most pupils are within ten centimetres of the mean. A histogram of exam scores shows two peaks, suggesting the class contains two groups — perhaps two different courses — which a single mean would hide. Another example is the ages of the members of a club: if most members are in their twenties and a few are in their seventies, the mean age will be higher than the median, and the median is the better summary of the typical member.

Real applications: Descriptive statistics are used everywhere data are reported. A government report gives the median household income, not just the mean, because the median is not distorted by a few very high incomes. A hospital reports the average length of stay and its spread, so managers can plan beds. A sports commentator reports a player's batting average and their best score. A weather report gives the average temperature and the range. In research, descriptive statistics are the first table in a paper, showing the reader who was studied and what the data look like before any test is run. Descriptive statistics also underlie the dashboards used by businesses and governments, which show the key numbers — sales, unemployment, hospital admissions — with their recent history, so that a change can be seen at a glance. They are the language in which data are first described, and every more advanced analysis begins with them.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask whether the mean or the median is better. Neither is always better: the mean uses all the data and is the right choice for symmetric data, while the median is the right choice when the data are skewed or contain outliers. A common misconception is that the standard deviation is the average distance from the mean; it is close to that idea, but it is the square root of the average squared distance, which gives more weight to large deviations. Another question is whether a graph is necessary when the numbers are available; it is, because two very different data sets can have the same mean and standard deviation, a fact shown by Anscombe's quartet in 1973. People also ask whether the standard deviation or the interquartile range is better. Neither is always better: the standard deviation uses all the data and is the right choice for symmetric data, while the interquartile range is the right choice when the data are skewed or contain outliers. Another question is what to do with an outlier; the answer is to investigate it, not to delete it automatically, because it may be an error, or it may be the most important observation in the data.

Probability

ID: JAH-STTC-000004 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Probability is the mathematics of chance: a number between zero and one that measures how likely an event is. A probability of zero means the event cannot happen, a probability of one means it must happen, and a probability of one half means it is as likely as not. Probability underlies all of statistics, because statistical inference is the business of judging how likely an observed result is under different explanations. It also underlies gambling, insurance, weather forecasting, and risk assessment.

History: Probability theory began in 1654, when Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat exchanged letters about how to divide the stakes in an unfinished game of chance, and in doing so founded the subject. In 1657, Christiaan Huygens published the first book on probability. In the 18th century, Jacob Bernoulli proved the law of large numbers in 1713, showing that the proportion of successes approaches the true probability as the number of trials grows, and Thomas Bayes described how to update a probability when new evidence arrives. In the 19th century, Pierre-Simon Laplace wrote the first systematic treatise. In 1933, Andrey Kolmogorov published the axioms of probability, giving the subject a rigorous foundation that it still rests on today. Probability theory continued to develop in the 20th century. In 1933, Andrey Kolmogorov published the axioms that gave the subject its rigorous foundation, and in the same decade, the frequentist and Bayesian interpretations were formalised. In the 1950s, the theory of stochastic processes — sequences of random events over time — was developed, and it became the basis of the study of queues, of financial markets, and of the spread of disease. In the 1970s, the study of risk and decision theory grew, and in the 2000s, probability became the foundation of machine learning, where models are built on probabilistic reasoning. Today, probability is taught in schools and universities around the world, and it is the language in which uncertainty is expressed in every field that deals with data.

Key concepts: An event is an outcome or a set of outcomes, such as rolling a six. The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. The probability of an event is the number of favourable outcomes divided by the total number of equally likely outcomes, or more generally the long-run proportion of times the event occurs. Two events are independent if one does not affect the other, and they are mutually exclusive if they cannot both happen. Conditional probability is the probability of an event given that another has happened, and it is the basis of Bayes' theorem. The law of large numbers says that as the number of trials grows, the observed proportion approaches the true probability. The addition rule says that the probability of one event or another is the sum of their probabilities, if they are mutually exclusive, and the multiplication rule says that the probability of both is the product, if they are independent. The complement of an event is the event that it does not happen, and its probability is one minus the probability of the event. A random variable is a variable whose value is determined by chance, and its distribution describes the probabilities of its values. The expected value is the long-run average of a random variable, and it is the basis of the idea of a fair price in gambling and insurance.

How it works: Probability is calculated from a model of the situation. For a fair coin, the model says each side has probability one half, so the probability of two heads in two tosses is one half times one half, or one quarter, because the tosses are independent. For more complex situations, the model may be a distribution, such as the normal distribution, or it may be estimated from data. A probability is checked by its consequences: if a model says an event has probability one in a hundred, then in ten thousand trials it should occur about a hundred times. When the observed frequency differs greatly from the model's probability, the model is wrong and must be revised. The model is tested by comparing its predictions with what actually happens: if a model says an event has a probability of one in ten, then in a thousand trials it should occur about a hundred times, and a large discrepancy means the model is wrong. This is the link between probability and statistics: probability gives the model, and statistics checks the model against the data. When the model fails, it is revised, and the cycle repeats. This is why probability is not just a branch of mathematics but a tool for learning from experience.

Worked examples: The probability of rolling a six on a fair die is one in six, or about 0.167. The probability of drawing an ace from a shuffled pack is four in fifty-two, or about 0.077. If the chance of rain on any given day is 0.3, then the chance of rain on two consecutive days, assuming independence, is 0.09. A lottery ticket has a probability of winning that is tiny — for a typical six-number lottery, about one in fourteen million — which is why the expected return on a ticket is far less than its price. A medical test with a false positive rate of five percent will wrongly flag about five in every hundred healthy people. Another example is the probability that two people in a room share a birthday: with twenty-three people, the probability is already above one half, a result that surprises most people because the number of pairs grows quickly.

Real applications: Probability is used to price insurance, where the premium reflects the probability of a claim; to assess risk in engineering, where the probability of failure must be tiny; to forecast the weather, where the probability of rain is reported rather than a yes or no; and to run lotteries and casinos, where the odds are set so the house wins on average. In science, probability judges whether an experimental result is likely to have arisen by chance. In everyday life, it is the language of risk: the probability that a flight is delayed, that a share price rises, or that a medical treatment works. Probability also underlies the design of experiments, where it is used to calculate the sample size needed to detect an effect, and the analysis of surveys, where it is used to calculate the margin of error. In genetics, it is used to calculate the probability that a child inherits a trait, and in quality control, it is used to decide how many items to inspect.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask whether a run of bad luck makes good luck more likely. It does not, if the events are independent: a coin has no memory, and five heads in a row does not make a tail more likely — this is the gambler's fallacy. A common misconception is that a probability of one in a hundred means the event will happen once in a hundred trials; it means that on average it will, but the actual count varies. Another question is whether probability is subjective or objective; both views exist, and the subject accommodates them, with the frequentist view treating probability as a long-run frequency and the Bayesian view treating it as a degree of belief. People also ask whether probability can be used to predict a single event. It cannot predict a single outcome, but it can say how likely each outcome is, and that is enough to make a decision: a doctor can say a treatment has an eighty percent chance of working, even though the outcome for one patient is uncertain. Another question is whether the probability of an event changes when new information arrives; it does, and Bayes' theorem is the rule for updating it.

Probability Distributions

ID: JAH-STTC-000005 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: A probability distribution describes how probability is spread over the possible values of a variable. For a discrete variable, it lists the probability of each value; for a continuous variable, it is a curve, and the probability of a range is the area under the curve. Distributions are the models that statistics uses to describe data and to calculate probabilities. The most famous is the normal distribution, the bell curve, but there are many others: the binomial for counts, the Poisson for rare events, and the exponential for waiting times.

History: The normal distribution was first described by Abraham de Moivre in 1733 as an approximation to the binomial, and it was developed by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1809, which is why it is also called the Gaussian distribution. The binomial distribution was studied by Jacob Bernoulli in the early 18th century. The Poisson distribution was introduced by Simeon Denis Poisson in 1837. In the 19th century, Adolphe Quetelet showed that many human measurements, such as height, follow the normal distribution. In the 20th century, the chi-squared, t, and F distributions were developed for hypothesis testing, by Karl Pearson, William Gosset, and Ronald Fisher. Today, distributions are the working language of statistics, and software can compute probabilities for dozens of them. The family of distributions grew through the 20th century. In the 1920s, the t-distribution, developed by William Gosset under the pseudonym Student, gave a way to work with small samples. In the 1930s, the F-distribution was developed for comparing variances. In the 1950s, the exponential and Weibull distributions were developed for the study of lifetimes and reliability. In the 1970s, the generalised linear model unified the treatment of many distributions, showing that the normal, binomial, and Poisson distributions are all members of one family. Today, distributions are the working language of statistics, and software can compute probabilities for dozens of them, but the choice of distribution remains a matter of judgement, guided by the data and the situation.

Key concepts: A discrete distribution gives the probability of each value, and the probabilities add to one. A continuous distribution is described by a density function, and the probability of a value falling in a range is the area under the curve over that range. The mean of a distribution is its centre of gravity, and the variance is its spread. The normal distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped, defined by its mean and standard deviation, and about 68 percent of its values fall within one standard deviation of the mean. The central limit theorem says that the average of many independent measurements is approximately normally distributed, whatever the original distribution, which is why the normal distribution appears so often. A parameter is a number that defines a distribution, such as the mean and standard deviation of a normal distribution, or the rate of a Poisson distribution. A quantile is the value below which a given proportion of the distribution falls, and the median is the 50th percentile. The cumulative distribution function gives the probability that a variable is less than or equal to a value. A mixture distribution is a combination of two or more distributions, used when the data come from more than one group. The tail of a distribution is the region far from the centre, and the thickness of the tail decides how likely extreme values are.

How it works: A distribution is chosen by matching its shape to the data and the situation. If the data are counts of successes in a fixed number of trials, the binomial is the natural model. If the data are counts of rare events in a fixed time, the Poisson is natural. If the data are measurements with many small, independent influences, the normal is natural, and the central limit theorem justifies it. The parameters of the distribution are estimated from the data — for example, the mean and standard deviation of a normal distribution — and then the distribution is used to calculate probabilities, such as the chance that a new observation exceeds a threshold. The fit of a distribution is checked by comparing it with the data: a histogram of the data is overlaid with the density curve, and a quantile-quantile plot compares the quantiles of the data with the quantiles of the distribution. If the points fall on a straight line, the fit is good; if they curve away, the distribution is wrong. The parameters are estimated from the data, usually by maximum likelihood, and the uncertainty of the estimates is reported. A distribution that fits well is then used to calculate probabilities and to make predictions.

Worked examples: The heights of adult men in a population are approximately normally distributed with a mean of about 175 centimetres and a standard deviation of about 7 centimetres, so about 68 percent of men are between 168 and 182 centimetres tall. The number of heads in ten coin tosses follows a binomial distribution, and the probability of exactly five heads is about 0.246. The number of calls arriving at a call centre in an hour follows a Poisson distribution, and if the average is twenty, the chance of more than thirty is small but calculable. The time between buses follows an exponential distribution if the buses arrive at random. Another example is the distribution of the time between buses: if buses arrive at random, the waiting time follows an exponential distribution, and the chance of waiting more than ten minutes can be calculated from its rate.

Real applications: Probability distributions are used to model the world wherever chance is involved. In manufacturing, the normal distribution models the variation in the size of parts, and control charts flag a process that drifts. In finance, distributions model the returns on investments and the risk of losses. In medicine, distributions model the variation in blood pressure and the time until a patient recovers. In insurance, they model the number and size of claims. In quality control, they decide how many items to inspect and what to conclude from the sample. Distributions also underlie the control charts used in manufacturing, where the normal distribution sets the limits that flag a process out of control, and the models used in finance, where the distribution of returns decides the risk of a portfolio. In reliability engineering, the Weibull distribution models the lifetime of a component and predicts when it will fail.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask why the normal distribution is so common. The answer is the central limit theorem: when many small, independent influences add up, their sum is approximately normal, and most measurements are the sum of many small influences. A common misconception is that all data are normally distributed; they are not, and income, waiting times, and counts are often skewed, so the wrong distribution can give badly wrong probabilities. Another question is whether a distribution can be known exactly; it cannot, and the distribution is a model, chosen for its fit and its usefulness, not a fact of nature. People also ask how to choose between distributions. The choice is guided by the nature of the data — counts, waiting times, or measurements — and by the fit, checked with graphs and tests. Another question is whether a distribution can be tested; it can, with goodness-of-fit tests, though a large sample can reject even a good approximation, and a small sample can fail to reject a bad one. Some ask whether the normal distribution is overused; it is, and the field has developed many alternatives for data that are skewed or heavy-tailed.

Sampling

ID: JAH-STTC-000006 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Sampling is the practice of studying a part of a population in order to learn about the whole. Because measuring everyone is usually too expensive or impossible, a sample is taken, and statistics provides the methods to judge how well the sample represents the population. The key idea is random sampling: every member of the population has a known chance of being included, so the sample is not biased toward one group. The size and the method of the sample decide how precise the conclusions can be.

History: Sampling has a long history: censuses counted whole populations, but samples were used for estimation as early as the 18th century, when Pierre-Simon Laplace estimated the population of France from a sample of births. In the 19th century, the Norwegian statistician Anders Kiaer argued in 1895 that a representative sample could replace a full census for many purposes, a view that was controversial at the time. In the 1920s and 1930s, Jerzy Neyman developed the theory of random sampling and stratified sampling, and in 1934 he published the paper that founded modern survey sampling. The Literary Digest poll of 1936, which surveyed 2.4 million people and still predicted the wrong winner of the American presidential election, showed that a large biased sample is worse than a small random one. Today, sampling underlies opinion polls, market research, quality control, and official statistics. Sampling theory continued to develop through the 20th century. In the 1940s, the first national sample surveys were run in the United States and Britain, and the methods of stratification and clustering were refined. In the 1950s, the theory of non-response was developed, and methods for weighting and adjusting for it were introduced. In the 1960s and 1970s, the study of survey errors was systematised, and the total survey error framework listed all the sources of error in a survey. In the 1980s and 1990s, the rise of telephone and then online surveys created new challenges, because the sampling frame — the list from which the sample is drawn — became harder to define. Today, sampling is the foundation of official statistics, opinion polling, and market research, and the principles developed in the 1930s still apply.

Key concepts: A simple random sample gives every member of the population an equal chance of selection, and it is the baseline against which other methods are judged. A stratified sample divides the population into groups, or strata, and samples within each, which improves precision when the groups differ. A cluster sample selects whole groups, such as schools, and measures everyone in them, which is cheaper when the population is spread out. A systematic sample selects every nth member from a list. Sampling error is the difference between a sample result and the true population value, and it shrinks as the sample grows. Sampling bias is a systematic error in the selection, and it does not shrink with size. The sampling frame is the list or source from which the sample is drawn, and if it does not cover the population, the sample will be biased. The sampling unit is the thing selected, such as a household or a person, and it may differ from the unit of analysis. The design effect measures how much less precise a complex sample is than a simple random sample of the same size, because clustering and stratification change the precision. Weighting adjusts the sample so that it matches the population on known characteristics, and it is used when some groups are over- or under-represented.

How it works: Sampling starts by defining the population and the sampling frame — the list from which the sample is drawn. The method is chosen to fit the population and the budget: a simple random sample for a small, well-listed population; a stratified sample when groups differ; a cluster sample when the population is spread out. The sample size is chosen to give the precision needed, using a formula that depends on the variability of the population and the margin of error required. The sample is drawn by a random mechanism, not by convenience, and the response rate is recorded, because people who do not respond may differ from those who do. The results are then weighted if necessary, so that the sample matches the population. The precision of a sample is governed by its size and by the variability of the population: a more variable population needs a larger sample for the same precision, and the margin of error shrinks in proportion to one over the square root of the sample size. This means that quadrupling the sample only halves the margin of error, which is why very precise estimates are expensive. The sample size is therefore chosen to balance precision against cost, and the calculation is done before the survey runs, not after.

Worked examples: A pollster who wants to estimate voting intention interviews a random sample of a thousand voters, stratified by region and age, and reports the result with a margin of error. A factory inspects a random sample of a hundred bolts from a day's production, rather than testing every bolt, and uses the sample to judge the whole batch. A health survey samples households in clusters — whole neighbourhoods — because visiting scattered households would be too expensive. A biologist studying a lake samples water at randomly chosen points rather than at the convenient shore. Another example is a quality-control inspector who tests a random sample of a hundred items from a batch of ten thousand, and uses the number of defective items in the sample to judge whether the batch meets the standard.

Real applications: Sampling is used wherever a whole population cannot be measured. Governments run sample surveys to estimate unemployment, prices, and health. Pollsters predict elections. Market researchers test new products on a sample of consumers. Quality control inspects a sample of a production run. Auditors check a sample of transactions. Scientists sample the environment, the ocean, and the atmosphere. In each case, the value of the sample depends on how it was drawn: a random sample of a thousand can tell more than a convenient sample of a million. Sampling also underlies the auditing of accounts, where a sample of transactions is checked to judge the whole set of books, and the monitoring of the environment, where samples of air, water, and soil are taken to judge the state of a region. In each case, the value of the sample depends on how it was drawn, and a random sample is the gold standard.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask whether a bigger sample is always better. It is better for precision, but only if the sample is unbiased; the Literary Digest poll of 1936 surveyed 2.4 million people and still got the election wrong, because its sample was drawn from car owners and telephone subscribers, who were richer than the average voter. A common misconception is that a sample must be a large fraction of the population; in fact, a well-drawn sample of a thousand can represent a population of millions, because precision depends on the absolute size of the sample, not its fraction. Another question is whether sampling can ever be exact; it cannot, and a good report always states the margin of error. People also ask whether a sample can ever be representative by luck. It can, but a random sample is representative in a stronger sense: it is representative on average, and its error can be measured, while a convenience sample has no such guarantee. Another question is whether sampling is used in science; it is, wherever a whole population cannot be measured, from the fish in a lake to the stars in a galaxy. Some ask whether a census is better than a sample; a census can be worse, because it is harder to do well, and a well-run sample can be more accurate than a badly run census.

Estimation

ID: JAH-STTC-000007 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Estimation is the use of sample data to guess the value of a population parameter, such as a mean or a proportion, together with a measure of how precise the guess is. A point estimate is a single number, such as the sample mean; an interval estimate, such as a confidence interval, gives a range of plausible values. The quality of an estimate is judged by its bias — whether it is systematically too high or too low — and its variance — how much it would vary from sample to sample. Good estimation balances both.

History: Estimation theory grew with modern statistics in the 20th century. Ronald Fisher introduced the method of maximum likelihood in 1922, a general way of finding the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable, and he studied the properties that make an estimator good. Jerzy Neyman introduced confidence intervals in 1937, giving a way to report an estimate with a stated level of confidence. In the 1940s and 1950s, the theory of unbiased estimation and minimum variance was developed, and the Cramér-Rao bound showed the best precision any unbiased estimator can achieve. In the 1970s, the bootstrap, developed by Bradley Efron, gave a computer-based way to estimate the precision of almost any estimator. Today, estimation is routine in software, but the choice of estimator and the interpretation of its precision remain a matter of judgement. Estimation theory continued to develop through the 20th century. In the 1940s, the theory of sequential estimation was developed, in which the sample size is not fixed in advance but the study stops when the evidence is sufficient. In the 1950s, the study of robust estimation began, with methods that are not distorted by a few extreme observations. In the 1970s, the bootstrap gave a general computer-based method for estimating the precision of almost any estimator, and in the 1980s, the jackknife and other resampling methods were developed. Today, estimation is routine in software, but the choice of estimator and the interpretation of its precision remain matters of judgement, and the honest reporting of uncertainty is regarded as a mark of good practice.

Key concepts: A point estimate is a single best guess, such as the sample mean as an estimate of the population mean. An interval estimate is a range of values that plausibly contains the true parameter. The standard error is the standard deviation of an estimator across repeated samples, and it measures precision. Bias is the difference between the average of an estimator and the true value. The mean squared error combines bias and variance into a single measure of quality. Maximum likelihood estimation chooses the parameter value that makes the observed data most likely, and it is the most widely used general method. Efficiency measures how much information an estimator extracts from the data, and the most efficient unbiased estimator is the one with the smallest variance. Consistency means that an estimator approaches the true value as the sample grows, which is a minimal requirement for any useful estimator. Sufficiency means that an estimator uses all the information in the data about the parameter, and no other statistic can add anything. The likelihood function is the probability of the observed data as a function of the parameter, and it is the basis of maximum likelihood estimation. A robust estimator is one that is not greatly affected by a few extreme observations or by departures from the assumed model.

How it works: Estimation starts with a model: the data are assumed to come from a distribution with an unknown parameter, and the sample is used to estimate it. The estimator is chosen for its properties — ideally unbiased and with small variance — and the estimate is computed. The standard error is then calculated, either from a formula or by a method such as the bootstrap, and the interval estimate is built from the point estimate and its standard error. The result is reported with the method, the sample size, and the assumptions, so that the reader can judge how much to trust it. If the assumptions are wrong, the estimate can be biased, and the interval can be too narrow. The precision of an estimate is governed by the sample size and the variability of the data: a larger sample gives a smaller standard error, and a more variable population gives a larger one. The standard error is usually estimated from the sample itself, using the sample standard deviation, and the interval is built from the estimate and its standard error. The method is checked by simulation: data are generated from a known parameter, the estimator is applied, and the results are compared with the truth, to see whether the estimator is biased and whether the interval covers the truth as often as it should.

Worked examples: A survey of a thousand voters finds that 520 support a candidate, so the point estimate of the candidate's support is 52 percent, and the 95 percent confidence interval is roughly 49 to 55 percent. A factory measures the diameter of a hundred bolts and estimates the mean diameter as 10.02 millimetres with a standard error of 0.01, so the interval is 10.00 to 10.04. A medical trial estimates that a treatment reduces blood pressure by 8 millimetres of mercury on average, with a confidence interval from 5 to 11, which tells the reader both the size of the effect and its precision. Another example is a quality engineer who estimates the proportion of defective items in a production run from a sample of two hundred, and reports the estimate with a confidence interval, so the manager knows both the estimated defect rate and how precisely it is known.

Real applications: Estimation is used wherever a population value must be learned from a sample. Opinion polls estimate voting intention. Economists estimate unemployment, inflation, and growth. Medical researchers estimate the effect of a treatment. Engineers estimate the strength of a material from a sample of tests. Environmental scientists estimate the size of a fish population from a catch. In each case, the estimate is only as good as the sample and the method, and a responsible report gives the interval as well as the point, so the reader sees the uncertainty. Estimation also underlies the measurement of poverty, where a survey estimates the proportion of households below a threshold, and the measurement of the environment, where samples of air and water are used to estimate pollution levels. In each case, the estimate is reported with its uncertainty, so that the reader can judge how much to trust it.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask whether a point estimate is enough. It is not, because it says nothing about precision: an estimate of 52 percent from a thousand voters is far more informative than the same number from ten voters. A common misconception is that an unbiased estimator is always the best; a slightly biased estimator with much smaller variance can have a smaller mean squared error. Another question is whether the sample mean is always the best estimate of the population mean; it is the best in many cases, but for a skewed distribution the median can be more stable. Some ask what the standard error is; it is the standard deviation of the estimator, and it shrinks as the sample grows, roughly in proportion to one over the square root of the sample size. People also ask whether an estimate can be too precise. It can, if the precision is overstated: an interval that is too narrow gives a false sense of certainty, and it is worse than an honest wide interval. Another question is whether a Bayesian estimate differs from a frequentist one; it can, because a Bayesian estimate incorporates a prior, but with a large sample the two usually agree. Some ask whether the sample mean is always the best estimator; it is the best in many cases, but for a skewed distribution a trimmed mean or a median can be more stable.

Hypothesis Testing

ID: JAH-STTC-000008 · Ladder: simple · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Hypothesis testing is a formal way of deciding whether the evidence from a sample supports a claim about a population. A null hypothesis states that there is no effect or no difference, and an alternative hypothesis states that there is one. The data are summarised in a test statistic, and the p-value is the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one observed, if the null hypothesis were true. If the p-value is small, the null hypothesis is rejected; if it is not, the null is not rejected. The method is the workhorse of scientific research.

History: Hypothesis testing was developed in the 1920s and 1930s. Ronald Fisher introduced the idea of the significance test and the p-value in his 1925 book "Statistical Methods for Research Workers", suggesting 0.05 as a convenient threshold. Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson developed a different framework in 1933, based on two hypotheses and the errors of rejecting a true null hypothesis, called a Type I error, and of failing to reject a false one, called a Type II error. The two frameworks were later combined into the hybrid method taught today, though the combination is not without controversy. In the 2010s, the replication crisis in psychology and medicine led to a re-examination of the method, and in 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a statement warning against the misuse of p-values. The debate about hypothesis testing continued through the 20th century. In the 1950s and 1960s, the method spread to every science, and the 0.05 threshold became a routine standard, often applied without thought. In the 1970s, Jacob Cohen argued that researchers should report effect sizes and power, not just p-values, and in the 1980s, the study of publication bias showed that significant results were far more likely to be published than non-significant ones. In the 2010s, the replication crisis in psychology and medicine showed that many published findings could not be reproduced, and in 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a statement warning against the misuse of p-values. In 2019, hundreds of statisticians called for the term statistically significant to be abandoned. Today, the field is moving toward reporting effect sizes, confidence intervals, and the design of the study, rather than a single p-value.

Key concepts: The null hypothesis is the default claim, usually that there is no effect, and the alternative hypothesis is the claim the researcher hopes to support. The test statistic is a number computed from the data that measures how far the data depart from the null. The p-value is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed, assuming the null is true. The significance level, usually 0.05, is the threshold below which the null is rejected. A Type I error is rejecting a true null, and a Type II error is failing to reject a false null. Statistical power is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null, and it grows with sample size and effect size. The effect size is the magnitude of the difference or association, and it is what matters in practice, while the p-value only says whether the result could be due to chance. Power is the probability of detecting an effect of a given size, and it depends on the sample size, the effect size, and the significance level. A one-sided test looks for an effect in one direction only, while a two-sided test looks in either direction. Multiple testing is the problem that arises when many tests are run: some will be significant by chance, and the significance level must be adjusted. A preregistered study states its hypotheses and analysis in advance, which prevents the selective reporting of results.

How it works: A hypothesis test starts by stating the hypotheses and choosing the significance level before the data are examined. The data are collected, the test statistic is computed, and the p-value is found from the appropriate distribution. If the p-value is below the significance level, the null is rejected and the result is called statistically significant; otherwise, the null is not rejected, which is not the same as proving it true. The result is reported with the effect size and a confidence interval, because a p-value alone says nothing about how large the effect is. A small p-value from a huge sample can accompany a trivially small effect, and a large p-value from a small sample can hide a large one. The test is only as good as its design: a test run on a biased sample, or on data collected after the hypothesis was formed, can give a small p-value for a spurious reason. The assumptions of the test must be checked — for a t-test, that the data are roughly normal and the variances are similar — and if they fail, a different test is used. The result is reported with the test statistic, the degrees of freedom, the p-value, the effect size, and the confidence interval, so that the reader can judge the evidence for themselves.

Worked examples: A coin is tossed a hundred times and lands heads sixty times. The null hypothesis is that the coin is fair, so the expected number of heads is fifty. The test asks how likely sixty or more heads would be for a fair coin, and the answer is about 0.028, below 0.05, so the null is rejected and the coin is judged biased. A drug trial compares the recovery rate of two hundred patients on a new treatment with two hundred on a placebo, and the p-value tells whether the difference is larger than chance would produce. A factory tests whether a new machine produces bolts of the same mean diameter as the old one. Another example is a factory that tests whether a new process reduces the defect rate: the null hypothesis is that the defect rate is unchanged, and the test asks whether the observed reduction is larger than chance would produce.

Real applications: Hypothesis testing is used in every science that runs experiments. In medicine, it judges whether a treatment works. In psychology, it judges whether an intervention changes behaviour. In agriculture, it judges whether a fertiliser increases yield. In manufacturing, it judges whether a process change improves quality. In economics, it judges whether a policy had an effect. The method is powerful, but it is also widely misused, and the current debate about p-values is a reminder that a test is a tool for judgement, not a substitute for it. Hypothesis testing also underlies the regulation of medicines, where a drug must show a statistically significant benefit in a trial before it is approved, and the evaluation of policies, where a government tests whether an intervention had an effect. In each case, the test is a tool for judgement, not a substitute for it, and the design of the study matters more than the test itself.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask what a p-value really means. It is the probability of the data, or more extreme data, given that the null hypothesis is true — not the probability that the null is true, which is a common misreading. A p-value of 0.03 does not mean there is a three percent chance the null is true. Another misconception is that a non-significant result proves there is no effect; it only means the study did not find one, which may be because the effect is small or the sample is small. Some ask whether 0.05 is a magic number; it is not, and Fisher himself called it a convenient convention, not a law of nature. People also ask whether a p-value of 0.049 is meaningfully different from 0.051. It is not: the threshold is a convention, and a result just below it is barely different from one just above it, which is why the field is moving away from a sharp threshold. Another question is whether a significant result means the finding is true; it does not, because a significant result can be a false positive, especially when many tests are run or when the study is small. Some ask whether hypothesis testing is still useful; it is, when used with care, but it must be combined with effect sizes, intervals, and replication.

Confidence Intervals

ID: JAH-STTC-000009 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: A confidence interval is a range of values, computed from sample data, that plausibly contains the true value of a population parameter. A 95 percent confidence interval is built by a method that, if the study were repeated many times, would produce intervals containing the true value in about 95 percent of repetitions. It is the standard way of reporting an estimate with its uncertainty, and it is more informative than a single point estimate, because it shows both the size of the estimate and how precisely it is known.

History: Confidence intervals were introduced by Jerzy Neyman in 1937, in a paper that founded the modern theory of interval estimation. Neyman's idea was to turn the problem around: instead of asking how likely a parameter value is, ask which parameter values are consistent with the data. The method spread quickly, and by the 1950s it was standard in medical and social research. In the 1980s and 1990s, the bootstrap, developed by Bradley Efron, gave a way to build intervals for almost any estimator, even when no formula existed. In the 2010s, the debate about p-values increased the use of confidence intervals, because an interval shows both the size of an effect and its precision, while a p-value shows neither directly. Today, most scientific journals require intervals alongside point estimates. The use of confidence intervals grew steadily after Neyman's 1937 paper. Definition: Regression is a method for modelling the relationship between a response variable and one or more explanatory variables. Analysis of Variance

ID: JAH-STTC-000012 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Analysis of variance, usually called ANOVA, is a method for comparing the means of three or more groups. It works by splitting the total variation in the data into two parts: the variation between the groups, and the variation within the groups. If the groups differ more than would be expected from the variation within them, the test concludes that at least one group mean differs. ANOVA is the standard method for analysing experiments with several treatments.

History: ANOVA was developed by Ronald Fisher in the 1920s, as part of his work on the design of experiments at Rothamsted Experimental Station in England. Fisher published the method in his 1925 book "Statistical Methods for Research Workers" and in his 1935 book "The Design of Experiments", which founded the modern theory of experimental design. The F-test, which ANOVA uses, is named after Fisher. The method spread rapidly through agriculture, where it was used to compare fertilisers and crop varieties, and then through medicine, psychology, and industry. In the 1960s and 1970s, the method was extended to more complex designs, including two-way ANOVA, which studies two factors at once, and repeated-measures ANOVA, which studies the same subjects over time. ANOVA continued to develop through the 20th century. In the 1940s, the method was extended to more complex designs, including factorial designs, in which two or more factors are studied at once, and repeated-measures designs, in which the same subjects are measured several times. In the 1950s, the analysis of covariance was developed, which combines ANOVA with regression to adjust for a continuous variable. In the 1960s, the study of the robustness of ANOVA showed that it is fairly tolerant of departures from its assumptions, especially when the groups are of similar size. In the 1970s, the general linear model unified ANOVA and regression, showing that they are the same method with different kinds of predictors. Today, ANOVA is taught in every introductory statistics course, and it is the standard analysis for designed experiments.

Key concepts: The total sum of squares is the sum of the squared differences between each observation and the overall mean, and it is split into the between-group sum of squares and the within-group sum of squares. The mean square is a sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom. The F-statistic is the ratio of the between-group mean square to the within-group mean square, and a large F suggests the groups differ. The null hypothesis is that all group means are equal, and the alternative is that at least one differs. A significant F-test is followed by post-hoc tests, which ask which groups differ from which. A factor is a categorical variable whose levels are the groups being compared, such as the type of fertiliser. The degrees of freedom are the number of independent pieces of information in a sum of squares, and they decide the shape of the F-distribution. An interaction is the situation in which the effect of one factor depends on the level of another, and it is tested in a two-way ANOVA. A post-hoc test, such as Tukey's test, compares pairs of groups after a significant ANOVA, adjusting for the fact that many comparisons are being made. The effect size for ANOVA, such as eta-squared, measures how much of the total variation is explained by the groups.

How it works: An ANOVA starts by computing the group means and the overall mean. The between-group variation measures how far the group means are from the overall mean, and the within-group variation measures how far the observations are from their own group mean. The F-statistic is their ratio, adjusted for degrees of freedom. If the F-statistic is large enough that its p-value is below the significance level, the null hypothesis is rejected. The result is reported with the F-statistic, the degrees of freedom, the p-value, and the group means, so the reader can see both the test and the size of the differences. The assumptions of ANOVA are that the residuals are normally distributed, that the variance is the same in each group, and that the observations are independent. The assumptions are checked by plotting the residuals and by testing the equality of variances. If the variances differ greatly, a transformation or a different test, such as Welch's ANOVA, is used. If the data are not normal, a nonparametric test, such as the Kruskal-Wallis test, is used. The result is reported with the F-statistic, the degrees of freedom, the p-value, the group means, and the effect size.

Worked examples: A farmer tests three fertilisers on plots of wheat and compares the mean yields: the ANOVA asks whether the differences between the fertilisers are larger than the variation between plots given the same fertiliser. A drug trial compares the mean blood pressure reduction of three doses and a placebo, using ANOVA to test whether any dose differs. A teacher compares the mean exam scores of three classes taught by different methods. A factory compares the mean strength of parts made on three machines. In each case, ANOVA answers the question of whether the groups differ, and post-hoc tests then identify which ones. Another example is a psychologist who compares the mean reaction times of three groups given different instructions, using ANOVA to test whether the instructions matter, and post-hoc tests to find which groups differ.

Real applications: ANOVA is used in agriculture to compare crop varieties and treatments; in medicine to compare doses and treatments; in psychology to compare experimental conditions; in industry to compare machines and processes; and in education to compare teaching methods. It is the standard analysis for any experiment with a categorical factor and a quantitative outcome, and it is the foundation of the more complex models used in modern research, including the analysis of designed experiments and the general linear model. ANOVA also underlies the analysis of clinical trials with several doses, the comparison of teaching methods in education, and the testing of materials in engineering, where the mean strength of parts made by different processes is compared. In each case, it answers the question of whether the groups differ, and the post-hoc tests then identify which ones.

Common questions and misconceptions: People ask why ANOVA is called analysis of variance when it compares means. It compares means by analysing variance: if the groups have different means, the variation between them will be large compared with the variation within them. A common misconception is that a significant ANOVA tells which groups differ; it does not, and post-hoc tests are needed for that. Another question is what to do if the assumptions fail; the assumptions are that the residuals are normally distributed and have equal variance across groups, and if they fail, a transformation or a non-parametric test can be used. Another question is what to do if the F-test is significant but no post-hoc test finds a difference; this can happen when the differences are spread across several groups, and it is a reminder that the F-test and the post-hoc tests ask different questions. Some ask whether ANOVA requires equal group sizes; it does not, but equal sizes make the test more robust to departures from its assumptions.

ID: JAH-STTC-000013 · Ladder: compounded · Status: established · Kind: human knowledge field record

Definition: Nonparametric methods are statistical methods that do not assume the data follow a particular distribution, such as the normal distribution. They are used when the data are skewed, when the sample is small, when the data are ordinal, or when the assumptions of a parametric test are not met. Common nonparametric methods include the sign test, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, the Mann-Whitney U test, and the Kruskal-Wallis test, which are the distribution-free counterparts of the t-test and ANOVA. They are generally less powerful than parametric methods when the assumptions hold, but more reliable when they do not.

History: Nonparametric methods developed in the 20th century as an alternative to the parametric methods of Fisher and his followers. In 1945, Frank Wilcoxon published the signed-rank test and the rank-sum test, which use the ranks of the data rather than the values themselves. In 1947, Henry Mann and Donald Whitney published the U test, which compares two independent samples by ranking all the observations together. In 1952, William Kruskal and Allen Wallis extended the idea to several groups. In the 1970s, the bootstrap, developed by Bradley Efron, gave a general computer-based method for inference without distributional assumptions. Today, nonparametric methods are standard in medicine, psychology, and social science, where data are often skewed or ordinal. Nonparametric methods continued to develop through the 20th century. In the 1950s, the study of rank tests was systematised, and the efficiency of rank tests relative to parametric tests was calculated, showing that the loss of power is usually small. In the 1960s, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was developed for comparing two distributions, and the chi-squared test for categorical data was refined. In the 1970s, the bootstrap gave a general computer-based method for inference without distributional assumptions, and in the 1980s, the study of permutation tests showed that they give exact p-values for almost any test statistic. Today, nonparametric methods are standard in every statistical package, and they are the methods of choice when the data are skewed, ordinal, or from a small sample.

Key concepts: A rank is the position of an observation when the data are ordered from smallest to largest, and most nonparametric methods work with ranks rather than raw values. The sign test uses only the direction of a difference, not its size. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test uses both the direction and the size, through the ranks of the absolute differences. The Mann-Whitney U test compares two independent samples by counting how often an observation from one group exceeds one from the other. History: Bayesian ideas began with Thomas Bayes, whose paper on the subject was published posthumously in 1763, and with Pierre-Simon Laplace, who developed the method in the late 18th century. In the 1980s and 1990s, the Markov chain Monte Carlo method, developed in physics in the 1950s and adapted to statistics, made Bayesian computation practical, and the method returned to the mainstream. Key concepts: The prior distribution expresses beliefs about a parameter before the data are seen, and it can be informative, based on earlier studies, or vague, expressing little knowledge. The posterior predictive distribution is the distribution of a new observation, given the data, and it is used for prediction. The marginal likelihood is the probability of the data averaged over the prior, and it is used to compare models. A hierarchical model is a model in which the parameters themselves have a distribution, which lets information be shared across groups.

How it works: A Bayesian analysis starts by choosing a prior, which is stated openly so that others can judge it. The data are then collected, and the likelihood is computed. The result is reported as the posterior distribution, or as a summary such as the posterior mean and a credible interval. The analysis is checked by trying different priors, to see whether the conclusion depends on the prior, and by checking whether the model fits the data. The computation is done by Markov chain Monte Carlo, which draws thousands of samples from the posterior, and the samples are summarised by their mean, their standard deviation, and their credible interval. The chain is checked for convergence: several chains are run from different starting points, and they should agree. The sensitivity to the prior is checked by trying different priors, and the fit of the model is checked by comparing the predicted data with the observed data. The result is reported with the prior, the posterior, and the checks, so that the reader can judge it.

Definition: Experimental design is the planning of an experiment so that it can answer a question clearly, with the effects of interest separated from other influences. Key concepts: A treatment is the condition applied to an experimental unit, such as a dose of a drug or a type of fertiliser. Statistics in Medicine

Definition: Statistics in medicine is the use of statistical methods to design medical studies, to analyse their results, and to make sense of health data. It covers the design of clinical trials, the analysis of observational studies, the measurement of disease in populations, and the evaluation of diagnostic tests. This record describes the research methods only; it gives no medical advice, no treatment recommendation, no dosage, and no diagnosis. The statistics are the subject, and the medicine is the field in which they are applied.

History: Medical statistics began with the study of populations. John Graunt's analysis of London's bills of mortality in 1662 founded demography, and in the 19th century, William Farr, the first medical statistician of the General Register Office in England, used statistics to study cholera and to reform public health. In 1948, the first randomised controlled trial, of streptomycin for tuberculosis, was run by the Medical Research Council, and it set the standard for testing treatments. In the 1950s, the studies of Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill showed the link between smoking and lung cancer, using case-control and cohort studies. In the 1970s, the meta-analysis — the statistical combination of several studies — was developed, and in the 1990s, evidence-based medicine made the systematic review of trials the basis of medical knowledge. In the 1960s, the randomised controlled trial became the standard for testing treatments, and the first large trials were run by the Medical Research Council. Key concepts: A clinical trial is a study in which participants are assigned to treatments, usually at random, and the outcomes are compared. A cohort study follows a group of people over time and compares those exposed to a factor with those not exposed. A case-control study compares people with a disease to people without it, and looks back at their exposures. The relative risk is the ratio of the risk in the exposed group to the risk in the unexposed group, and the odds ratio is a similar measure used in case-control studies. Sensitivity is the proportion of people with a disease who test positive, and specificity is the proportion of people without it who test negative. A meta-analysis combines the results of several studies to give a more precise estimate. The intention-to-treat principle is the rule that participants are analysed in the group to which they were assigned, even if they did not receive the treatment, because analysing only those who complied would break the randomisation. The number needed to treat is the number of patients who must be treated for one to benefit, and it is a useful way of expressing the size of an effect. A systematic review is a structured summary of all the studies on a question, and a meta-analysis is the statistical combination of their results. Publication bias is the tendency for significant results to be published more often than non-significant ones, which can distort the evidence.

How it works: A medical study is designed by first stating the question and the outcome to be measured. The sample size is calculated to give the power needed to detect a clinically important effect. Participants are recruited and, in a trial, assigned at random to the treatments, with blinding if possible. The data are collected and analysed, usually by comparing the groups and reporting the effect with a confidence interval. The results are interpreted in the context of the study's limitations: who was included, how long they were followed, and whether the outcome is clinically important as well as statistically significant. The study is then reported so that others can judge it and, if possible, replicate it. The study is reported according to a standard — CONSORT for trials, STROBE for observational studies — so that the reader can judge it.
