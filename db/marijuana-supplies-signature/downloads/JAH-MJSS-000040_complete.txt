SCIENCE PROOF
A chillum works by direct flow, and its physics is the physics of a tapered tube. The bore is a frustum, a truncated cone, wider at the bowl end and narrower at the mouthpiece, so the smoke speeds up as the tube narrows, and the draw feels direct because nothing slows the air down: no water, no carb, no chamber. The chillum stone is the one clever part: a small fitted pebble set at the transition between the bowl and the passage, it acts as a screen that stops ash and ground fragments from reaching the mouth, and it forces the air against the tube walls, which cools the smoke more than a straight empty tube would. The traditional safi, a damp cloth wrapped around the mouthpiece end, cools the smoke further by evaporation, and cupped hands around the mouthpiece form a small chamber where smoke can collect and cool before it is inhaled. Fired stoneware clay matters because it tolerates the hot bowl end and the cool mouthpiece without cracking, and because clay is the original chillum material. The bore volume is the frustum formula, one third pi times length times the sum of the two end radii squared and their product, and the bowl section is a shorter frustum at the wide end. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

HISTORY
The chillum is one of the oldest pipe shapes still in everyday use, and its history is older than the bong, the spoon, and the vaporizer put together. It traces back to India, where Hindu holy men, sadhus, used conical clay chillums in religious and communal settings, with the earliest documented use in the eighteenth century and some researchers pushing the first evidence back to the eighth century. The shape spread to Africa and to Rastafarian communities, and it reached the West in the 1960s and 1970s, and the geometry barely changed along the way: a straight tube, wide at the bowl end, narrow at the mouthpiece, with no carb hole, no water chamber, and no moving parts. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape.
