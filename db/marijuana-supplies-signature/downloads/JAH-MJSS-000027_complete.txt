SCIENCE PROOF
The tree percolator works by diffusion, which is the splitting of one flow into many small flows. Smoke enters the central stem and is forced out through the slits in each hollow arm, so instead of one large bubble rising from the downstem, dozens of small bubbles rise at once from every arm. A small bubble has more surface area for its volume than a large one, so more of the smoke touches water, and more heat passes into the water before the bubble reaches the surface. The total slit area sets how easily smoke gets out: eight arms with six slits each give forty-eight streams, and the sum of the slit areas is the opening the smoke must pass through. If the slits clog with resin, the opening shrinks, the draw tightens, and the diffusion falls, which is why tree percs must be cleaned often. The arms are fragile because they are thin hollow glass with slits cut into them, and a knock that a plain tube would survive can snap an arm. The percolator sits above the base water so the smoke is diffused twice: once at the downstem and once at the arms. HISTORY
The percolator bong grew out of the plain water pipe once glassmakers learned to fuse extra chambers and arms inside the tube. The tree percolator, a central stem with branching hollow arms cut with slits, was one of the earliest percolator designs, and trade guides still describe it as the most universal percolator style, usually built with four to twelve arms. The idea is old in outline: breaking one large bubble into many small ones increases the surface where smoke meets water, which is the same diffusion principle used in laboratory glassware and aquarium air stones. Percolator bongs became widely sold in the 2000s and 2010s as glassblowing workshops competed on diffusion designs, adding honeycomb discs, showerheads, and matrix grids alongside the tree. The tree arm remains popular because it diffuses strongly while keeping the draw open. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. Trade guides and manufacturer manuals preserve the steps of that change. """Demo: tree percolator slit area and chamber volumes — Signature Grove Tree Perc."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    arms = 12; slits = 8; w = 1.2; l = 9.0; d = 65.0; h = 400.0; water_h = 55.0
    slit_area = w * l
    total_slit = arms * slits * slit_area
    streams = arms * slits
    area = math.pi * (d / 2.0) ** 2
    water_mm3 = area * water_h
    chamber_mm3 = area * h
    return {"arms": arms, "slits_per_arm": slits, "slit_area_mm2": round(slit_area, 2), "total_slit_area_mm2": round(total_slit, 2), "bubble_streams": streams, "water_volume_mL": round(water_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "chamber_volume_mL": round(chamber_mm3 / 1000.0, 2)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ["arms", "slits_per_arm", "slit_area_mm2", "total_slit_area_mm2", "bubble_streams", "water_volume_mL", "chamber_volume_mL"]:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
