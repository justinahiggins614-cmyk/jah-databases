SCIENCE PROOF
A pipe screen does two jobs at once and they pull in opposite directions: it must block particles and it must let air through. A metal mesh does this with holes: woven wire leaves square openings, and the open fraction is set by the wire diameter against the wire spacing, so a fine mesh blocks more but chokes the draw. The demo computes that balance for a three-quarter-inch stainless screen, 19.05 millimetres across, with 0.3 millimetre wire on a 0.9 millimetre pitch, giving an open fraction of 0.44 and 448 openings across the disc. Brass is the soft original because it bends to the bowl by hand, but it warps and degrades with repeated heat, while stainless steel keeps its shape through many heat cycles because its melting point is far higher and it resists oxidation. A glass daisy screen works differently: it has no mesh at all, and its raised petals create channels that let air pass while the solid glass blocks particles, which is why it never clogs the way mesh does and why it can be rinsed and reused indefinitely unless it chips. Borosilicate is chosen for the glass form because its low thermal expansion resists the shock of repeated heating and cooling. HISTORY
The pipe screen is one of the oldest small parts in smoking: a disc of mesh or a shaped piece of glass set at the bottom of a bowl so loose material stays put while air passes through. Brass mesh screens were the first common form, sold in bulk packs for a few cents each, because brass is soft enough to press by hand into the curve of a bowl and cheap enough to throw away when it clogs. Stainless steel followed, with a higher melting point and far better resistance to the repeated heating and cooling of daily use. Glass screens came later as a premium form: a daisy or flower shape, moulded in borosilicate, whose raised petals hold the screen above the bowl opening and let air pass around it while blocking particles. Titanium and quartz screens sit at the specialty end of the same family. Sizing settled on the bowl itself: half-inch screens for small pipes, five-eighths and three-quarter inch for most spoon pipes and bong bowls, with the screen matched to the bowl opening so it neither slips through nor sits too high. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage of that change.
