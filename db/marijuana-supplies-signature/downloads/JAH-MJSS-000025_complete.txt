SCIENCE PROOF
The beaker works by geometry. Its base is a frustum, a cone with its tip cut off, so its volume grows faster than its height: the wide bottom holds most of the water while the narrow top keeps the neck slim. Water volume is the frustum volume up to the water line, and the water depth matters because the downstem must end below the surface or smoke will not bubble at all. Bubbles rising through water cool the smoke by contact: each bubble is a sphere of hot gas, and the smaller the bubble the larger its surface area for its volume, so more of the heat passes into the water. The wide base also lowers the centre of gravity, which is why the beaker resists tipping: the mass of water sits low and wide, so the tipping moment needed to lift one edge is larger than for a straight tube of the same height. Borosilicate glass is used because it expands very little when heated, so the hot bowl joint does not crack the cold base, and because it does not give flavour to the smoke. The neck above the water is a reservoir: its volume sets how much smoke one draw can hold before the chamber must be cleared. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

HISTORY
The beaker bong takes its shape from the laboratory beaker, the wide-based glass vessel chemists have used since the nineteenth century, and from the water pipe itself, whose word comes from the Thai baung, a wooden cylinder or pipe cut from bamboo. Glass water pipes became common in the twentieth century as borosilicate glass made a heat-resistant, clean-tasting water chamber practical, and a 1981 United States patent (4,253,475) describes the classic form in detail: a single vertical pipe sealed at the bottom, a bowl on a tube leading in near the bottom, and water just above the junction. The beaker base was one of the first variations makers tried, because the flared foot spreads the weight low and wide. Trade guides still call the beaker the most stable shape and the usual first bong, and the wide base holds more water than a straight tube of the same height. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. Trade guides and manufacturer manuals preserve the steps of that change. The history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

import hashlib, math
def compute():
    R = 55.0; r = 27.0; base_h = 75.0; water_h = 40.0; neck_d = 54.0; neck_h = 240.0
    r_water = R - (R - r) * (water_h / base_h)
    water_mm3 = math.pi * water_h / 3.0 * (R * R + R * r_water + r_water * r_water)
    base_mm3 = math.pi * base_h / 3.0 * (R * R + R * r + r * r)
    neck_mm3 = math.pi * (neck_d / 2.0) ** 2 * neck_h
    air_mm3 = (base_mm3 - water_mm3) + neck_mm3
    return {"water_volume_mL": round(water_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "base_volume_mL": round(base_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "neck_volume_mL": round(neck_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "chamber_air_mL": round(air_mm3 / 1000.0, 2), "water_depth_mm": water_h, "base_diameter_mm": 2 * R}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
