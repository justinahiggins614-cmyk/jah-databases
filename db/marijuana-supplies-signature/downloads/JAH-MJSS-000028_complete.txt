SCIENCE PROOF
The recycler works by keeping water in motion. In a plain bong the water sits still and the smoke passes through it once; in a recycler the draw creates a pressure difference that pulls water up the uptake tube into the secondary chamber, where the water and the smoke separate because water is far denser than smoke and falls back under gravity. The drain tube carries that water back to the main chamber, so the percolator never runs dry during a long pull, which is the failure that makes a plain percolator gurgle and splash. The loop time is the loop water volume divided by the draw flow rate: at a draw of 1.5 litres per minute the 230 millilitres of water in the loop takes about nine seconds to make one full circuit. The uptake tube must be wider than the drain tube, because it carries water and smoke together while the drain carries water alone, and a drain that is too narrow will back up and flood the upper chamber. The smoke is not filtered a second time by the loop: the loop's jobs are to keep the percolator covered, to keep water off the lips, and to keep each bubble surrounded by water longer, which is where the extra cooling comes from. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

HISTORY
The recycler bong is a recent design, developed in the 2010s as glassblowers working on concentrate rigs looked for a way to keep water moving instead of letting it sit still. The idea borrows from the Klein bottle, a mathematical shape with no inside or outside, and some recycler designs are still called Klein recyclers for the sealed loop their glass forms. Early recyclers were small dab rigs; the loop layout then spread to flower bongs, with two chambers joined by an uptake tube and a drain tube so water circulates while smoke travels to the mouthpiece. Trade guides describe the loop as doing three jobs at once: it keeps the percolator covered, it keeps water off the user's lips, and it keeps smoke bubbles surrounded by water longer. The design is now a recognised type in its own right, sold alongside beakers and straight tubes. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. Trade guides and manufacturer manuals preserve the steps of that change. The history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.
