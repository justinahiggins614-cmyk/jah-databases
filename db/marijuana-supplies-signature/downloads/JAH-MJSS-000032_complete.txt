SCIENCE PROOF
The metal bowl works by heat conduction and by strength. Brass conducts heat well, so the cup warms evenly and the material at the edges burns at nearly the same rate as the material in the middle, and the stainless-steel sleeve in modern designs regulates that heat further, keeping the load in contact with steel only. The cup is a frustum, wide at the rim and narrow at the bottom, so the material sits in a cone that funnels smoke toward the hole, and the screen over the hole stops fine particles without blocking the airflow. Metal survives what glass does not: a dropped metal bowl dents where a glass bowl shatters, and a metal bowl can be scrubbed with a brush that would scratch glass. The mass of the bowl follows from its metal volume times its density: stainless steel at 8.0 grams per cubic centimetre makes a bowl of about 20 grams, heavy enough to feel solid and light enough to carry. The trade-off is flavour and care: brass can develop a taste over time and needs polishing, aluminium heats quickly and can add a metallic note, and stainless steel stays neutral but costs more. HISTORY
The metal bowl is the oldest bowl form of all: brass pipe bowls appear in museum collections as inverted cones with decorative rims, made long before glass bowls existed. Metal bowls are favoured for resilience and for keeping their shape under repeated heating, and the common metals are brass, aluminium, and stainless steel, each with its own trade-offs. Brass gives a warm golden look and good heat conduction but needs polishing and can develop a taste over time; aluminium is very light and cheap but heats quickly; stainless steel resists rust and heat, cleans easily, and does not alter flavour. Modern designs combine metals, such as an anodised aluminium bowl with a stainless-steel sleeve insert that regulates heat and keeps the material in contact with steel only. Metal bowls fit the same 14 mm and 18 mm joints as glass bowls, and they survive drops that would shatter glass. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape.
