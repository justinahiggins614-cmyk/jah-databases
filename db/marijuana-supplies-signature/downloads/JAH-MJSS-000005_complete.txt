SCIENCE PROOF
A spoon pipe is a direct-flow combustion pipe with a manual air valve, the carb. Lighting the herb starts combustion, and the draw creates a pressure drop along the stem: air at atmospheric pressure enters through the bowl, passes over the burning herb, and the hot gases travel down the stem to the mouth. Covering the carb forces all incoming air through the bowl, which keeps the herb burning and fills the stem and bowl chamber with smoke. Releasing the carb opens a low-resistance path for fresh air, which sweeps the stored smoke into the mouth in one clear. Borosilicate glass matters because it has a low coefficient of thermal expansion, so it tolerates the hot bowl end and the cool mouthpiece end without cracking, and because glass is chemically inert, it adds no flavour of its own. The bowl is a shallow cylinder, so its volume is pi times radius squared times depth, and the stem is a long narrow cylinder whose volume sets how much smoke the pipe holds between the bowl and the mouth. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. HISTORY
The glass spoon is a recent invention in pipe history: sources place its arrival in the mid-to-late 1970s, when glass workers in the United States combined elements of tobacco pipes and older cannabis pipes into a small hand pipe of borosilicate glass. It became the most widely used Western cannabis pipe because it was simple, pocket-sized, and ran cooler than a chillum while taking up far less space than a bong. Borosilicate glass was the material from the start, chosen for heat resistance and a clean flavour, and the form has since spawned sherlocks, hammers, and sidecars.
