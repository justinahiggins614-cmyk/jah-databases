SCIENCE PROOF
A carbon filter works by adsorption: air passes through a bed of activated carbon, and odour molecules stick to the enormous internal surface of the carbon pores instead of passing through. The bed in this record is 38 millimetres thick, the common RC412 grade of virgin Australian carbon rated at 1200 or higher iodine value, which is a measure of how much the carbon can adsorb. The canister is an annulus: air enters through the outer mesh, crosses the 38 millimetre bed, and leaves through the inner mesh to the fan, so the carbon volume is the volume between the outer and inner cylinders, about 2.22 litres in the 4-inch by 12-inch example. Air must move slowly enough for adsorption to happen: at 205 CFM through the outer surface of the canister the face velocity is about one metre per second, which gives the air a real dwell time in the bed, and running the fan far faster than the filter rating pushes air past the carbon untreated. The pre-filter sleeve matters because dust clogs the outer mesh and shortens the bed life, so keeping it clean is the cheapest way to extend the filter. The bed saturates over time no matter how clean the pre-filter is — the sources put continuous-use life at 12 to 18 months for a good canister, and 3 to 6 months where makers quote a conservative replacement interval — and the demo computes the carbon volume, the face velocity, and the hours in the quoted lifespan so the reader can check the arithmetic directly. HISTORY
The carbon filter is a canister of activated carbon that scrubs odour from exhaust air, and it became a standard grow-tent part once indoor gardening moved into shared buildings. Activated carbon has been used to purify air and water for more than a century: its surface is full of microscopic pores, and gas molecules stick to those pores by adsorption, a surface effect, not absorption into the material. The grow-tent form is a steel canister with a flange on one end, a bed of carbon held between two mesh cylinders, and a cloth pre-filter sleeve that catches dust before it reaches the carbon. Common sizes match the common fans: 4 inches by 12 inches for a 2 by 2 or 2 by 4 tent, 6 inches by 16 inches for a 4 by 4 tent, with a bed about 38 millimetres thick of virgin Australian carbon rated at 1200 or higher iodine value.
