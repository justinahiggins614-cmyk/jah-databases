1. Description and history

The Signature Alloy Metal Bowl is a Signature-made design study for metal bowl, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — the bowl holds the material in its metal cup while air drawn through the hole in the bottom of the cup, usually fitted with a metal screen, carries smoke down the joint and into the water pipe — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature stainless steel 304 bowl body; stainless-steel screen; ground joint to fit 18 mm female downstem seats. The design is sized at 18 mm male joint; cup 22 mm diameter at the rim and 17 mm deep; screen 16 mm diameter; wall 2 mm thick, which sets every number in this record, from the volumes and areas in the demo to the scale variants at the end. Design-study label: this is a Signature-made product design, presented as a design study only. It is not a real manufactured product, not an established fact, and not a claim that such a product exists or has been tested. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, run the program, and see exactly where each number comes from. The Signature edition exists alongside the public edition so the archive holds both what exists in the world and what the Signature system designs next, clearly labelled, so the two are never confused. Like every Signature record, it is filed as a design study, with its own generator and its own file AI, and it marches with the rest of the database toward a million records.

The metal bowl is the oldest bowl form of all: brass pipe bowls appear in museum collections as inverted cones with decorative rims, made long before glass bowls existed. Metal bowls are favoured for resilience and for keeping their shape under repeated heating, and the common metals are brass, aluminium, and stainless steel, each with its own trade-offs. Brass gives a warm golden look and good heat conduction but needs polishing and can develop a taste over time; aluminium is very light and cheap but heats quickly; stainless steel resists rust and heat, cleans easily, and does not alter flavour. Modern designs combine metals, such as an anodised aluminium bowl with a stainless-steel sleeve insert that regulates heat and keeps the material in contact with steel only. Metal bowls fit the same 14 mm and 18 mm joints as glass bowls, and they survive drops that would shatter glass. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. Trade guides and manufacturer manuals preserve the steps of that change. The history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: metal bowl needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Materials and design

This is a Signature design study, not a real product. Brass is chosen for the public bowl because it machines cleanly on a lathe, holds a thread and a taper well, and resists corrosion, and its warm golden colour is the traditional look of a metal bowl. Stainless steel is chosen for the screen because it resists rust and heat, does not alter flavour, and can be woven into a fine mesh that stops particles without blocking airflow. Aluminium is the light alternative: it is very light and cheap, but it heats quickly and can add a slight metallic note if it is not cleaned. The wall is kept at 2 millimetres, thick enough to hold heat and to survive drops, and thin enough that the bowl stays light in the hand. The joint is a machined taper that mates with the glass downstem seat, 14 mm or 18 mm, male or female, and it seals by the same friction as a glass joint, with the metal taper pressed into the glass socket. The tolerance that matters is the taper angle: a metal taper ground too steep will rock in the glass seat, and one forced into the wrong size can crack the glass. The screen sits in a shallow recess at the bottom of the cup, held by its own spring, so it can be lifted out and replaced without tools. The stem below the cup is machined in one piece with the cup, so there is no joint to loosen. The outside is polished on brass and brushed on stainless steel, and the inside of the cup is left smooth so residue can be scrubbed out with a brush. Anodised aluminium versions add a coloured oxide layer that resists wear and gives the bowl its colour, and the anodising is kept off the inside of the cup where the load sits. Variants change the metal, the joint size, and the gender, but the cup and the screen stay the same in every version.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: m = rho * V_metal

m is the mass of the bowl in grams; rho is the density of the metal in grams per cubic centimetre; V_metal is the volume of metal in cubic centimetres.

The demo treats the cup as a frustum: pi times the cup depth of 17.0 mm divided by 3, times the sum of the top radius squared, the top radius times the bottom radius, and the bottom radius squared, with a top radius of 11.0 mm and a bottom radius of 4.5 mm. Dividing by 1000 gives cubic centimetres, and multiplying by the packing density of 0.15 grams per cubic centimetre gives the estimated capacity in grams. The outer body is a second frustum with a top radius of 13.0 mm, a bottom radius of 6.5 mm, and a height of 19.0 mm. Subtracting the cup volume from the outer volume gives the metal volume, and multiplying that by the metal density of 8.0 grams per cubic centimetre gives the bowl mass in grams. The screen area is pi times the screen radius squared, with a screen diameter of 16.0 mm. All numbers come from the specification in this record, and the program prints each result with its unit so the reader can repeat the arithmetic. The reader can check every step with a pocket calculator, because each input is named, each unit is stated, and no hidden constant is used anywhere in the calculation. The computation is run twice and the SHA-256 digests of both runs are printed, confirming the result is deterministic.

4. Science proof

The metal bowl works by heat conduction and by strength. Brass conducts heat well, so the cup warms evenly and the material at the edges burns at nearly the same rate as the material in the middle, and the stainless-steel sleeve in modern designs regulates that heat further, keeping the load in contact with steel only. The cup is a frustum, wide at the rim and narrow at the bottom, so the material sits in a cone that funnels smoke toward the hole, and the screen over the hole stops fine particles without blocking the airflow. Metal survives what glass does not: a dropped metal bowl dents where a glass bowl shatters, and a metal bowl can be scrubbed with a brush that would scratch glass. The mass of the bowl follows from its metal volume times its density: stainless steel at 8.0 grams per cubic centimetre makes a bowl of about 20 grams, heavy enough to feel solid and light enough to carry. The trade-off is flavour and care: brass can develop a taste over time and needs polishing, aluminium heats quickly and can add a metallic note, and stainless steel stays neutral but costs more. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports. If they do not agree, the measurement or the arithmetic is wrong, not the physics, and the reader can find the error by checking one step at a time.

Build it yourself

As a design study, the Signature Alloy Metal Bowl has not been built; the steps below describe the closest genuine maker activity for the public form, adapted to the Signature dimensions. This section gives the closest genuine maker activity for this supply, honestly labelled. A metal bowl is a lathe-turned part, and a home maker with a metal lathe can genuinely turn one from brass bar stock, which is the closest real build for this supply. Mount a brass bar in the lathe, face the end, and turn the outside profile first: the cup rim, the taper, and the stem, checking each diameter with calipers as you go. Then bore the cup from the top with a drill and finish it with a boring bar to the frustum profile, leaving a 2 millimetre wall, and drill the hole at the bottom of the cup. Part the bowl off, deburr every edge, and polish the outside. Cut a screen from stainless-steel mesh a millimetre wider than the cup bottom so it springs into place, and press it flat with a blunt tool. Test the fit dry in the downstem: it should seat firmly without wobble and lift out cleanly. Wear eye protection throughout, and keep hands clear of the spinning work. Without a lathe, the closest genuine activity is fitting a screen to a bought bowl and polishing the brass. Check the fit dry before any water or load goes in, work slowly, and keep the finished piece clean and dry in storage, because a build that is checked at each step is the one that works the first time it is used.

3D-print version

As a design study, the Signature Alloy Metal Bowl has not been printed or built; the directions below are for the printable accessory for the public form, adapted to the Signature dimensions. Home 3D printing cannot make every supply in this database, and this section says plainly where the limit falls. A metal bowl is not 3D-printable at home: no home filament prints brass or stainless steel, and a printed plastic bowl would soften and give off fumes where the material burns, so it must never be used as a bowl. The printable accessory is a protective bowl cap and a storage tray for spare bowls and screens. Print the cap in PETG, which tolerates warm water and does not crack as easily as PLA, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, 0.2 mm layer height, 4 perimeters, and 25 percent gyroid infill, and print the tray in the same material with separate recesses for each bowl and a small compartment for screens. Print both flat on their bottom faces with no supports. Print time (estimate): 30 minutes for the cap and 2 hours 15 minutes for the tray, at the settings given. Unmodelled factors that change real print time and strength are wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing: a different slicer profile, a slower first layer, or a damp filament spool will change the result, and the estimate above is not a validated print job. Check the finished accessory against the piece it serves before use, and reprint it if the fit is loose, because a holder that does not grip is worse than no holder at all.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: metal bowl cup volume, metal mass, and screen area — Signature Alloy Metal Bowl."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    R = 11.0; r = 4.5; h = 17.0; oR = 13.0; o_r = 6.5; o_h = 19.0; rho_metal = 8.0; scr = 16.0; joint = 18.0; rho_pack = 0.15
    cup_mm3 = math.pi * h / 3.0 * (R * R + R * r + r * r)
    cup_cm3 = cup_mm3 / 1000.0
    cap = cup_cm3 * rho_pack
    outer_mm3 = math.pi * o_h / 3.0 * (oR * oR + oR * o_r + o_r * o_r)
    metal_cm3 = (outer_mm3 - cup_mm3) / 1000.0
    mass = metal_cm3 * rho_metal
    screen_area = math.pi * (scr / 2.0) ** 2
    return {"cup_volume_cm3": round(cup_cm3, 2), "capacity_g": round(cap, 2), "metal_volume_cm3": round(metal_cm3, 2), "mass_g": round(mass, 2), "screen_area_mm2": round(screen_area, 2), "joint_mm": joint}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ["cup_volume_cm3", "capacity_g", "metal_volume_cm3", "mass_g", "screen_area_mm2", "joint_mm"]:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
