1. Description and history

The Signature Glass Ashtray is a Signature-made design study for glass ashtray, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — ash and spent material fall into the deeper Signature dish; rim notches hold a cigarette or joint by gravity; the heavy base keeps the dish from tipping — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature borosilicate glass with a coloured base band; annealed; no metal or plastic in the ash path. The design is sized at dish 130 mm across and 30 mm deep; wall 5 mm thick; four rim notches; about 279 grams, which sets every number in this record, from the glass ashtray capacity and thermal expansion in the demo to the scale variants at the end. This record is a design study, not a real manufactured product, and nothing here claims the design has been built, tested, or sold. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, and run the program to check every number. The record also gives the materials and design, a build-it-yourself section, 3D-printing directions, and a use program, so the design can be judged as a whole, not only as a set of numbers. Like every Signature record, it sits beside its public twin in the same pair, so the reader can compare the public form with the Signature reworking side by side.

The glass ashtray is one of the oldest ashtray forms, because glass has been the default fire-retardant table material since ashtrays became common household objects in the early twentieth century. The word ashtray came into use around 1920, and the most common design from the start was a shallow cylinder with a flat base, made of glass, pottery, metal, or stone, with notches in the rim for resting a cigarette. Glass won the table because it is non-porous, easy to clean, and does not hold odour, and because a heavy glass dish stays put where a light one slides. Over the twentieth century the form split into round, square, and geometric shapes, into clear, amber, and frosted glass, and into plain and cut-glass decoration, while the job stayed the same. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage, and the history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: glass ashtray needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Materials and design

The glass ashtray is a material-first design, and the choice is between borosilicate and tempered soda-lime glass. Borosilicate is chosen here because its low thermal expansion, 3.3 times ten to the minus six per kelvin, gives it exceptional thermal shock resistance, so it survives a hot ember followed by a cool rinse without cracking, while ordinary glass would not. Tempered glass is the alternative: it is stronger against impact and cheaper to make, but it is more sensitive to sudden temperature change, so it suits a tray that holds cold ash rather than a dish that takes hot embers. The wall is 5 mm thick, which is the working compromise: thinner walls are lighter and clearer but chip at the rim, thicker walls survive drops better but make the dish heavy and slow to cool. The rim notches are cut or moulded into the glass before annealing, and their tolerance is the width of the cut, sized to hold a cigarette without letting it roll. The finish is clear, amber, or frosted glass, annealed to relieve internal stress, and the design varies by shape, round, square, or geometric, by the number of notches, and by decoration, while the working principle stays the same.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: V = pi * r^2 * h

V is the dish capacity in cubic millimetres; r is the dish radius in millimetres; h is the dish depth in millimetres.

The demo treats the ashtray dish as a cylinder and the glass shell as the difference between two cylinders. With a dish diameter of 130.0 mm and a depth of 30.0 mm, the radius is 65.0 mm, so the capacity is pi times 4,225 times 30, about 398,197 cubic millimetres, or 398.20 cubic centimetres. The outer cylinder is 130.0 mm across and 35.0 mm tall, counting the 5 mm base, so its volume is about 464,563 cubic millimetres, and the inner cylinder is 120.0 mm across and 30.0 mm deep, about 339,292 cubic millimetres, leaving a shell of 125.27 cubic centimetres. At a borosilicate density of 2.23 grams per cubic centimetre the mass is 279.35 grams. Thermal expansion over a 200 kelvin rise is the coefficient 3.3 times ten to the minus six times the diameter 130 times 200, about 0.09 millimetres, which is why the dish does not crack when a hot ember lands in it. Every number comes from the specification in this record, the formulas are shown in full, and the program prints each quantity so the reader can check the arithmetic. The computation runs twice with SHA-256 digests printed, so the reader can confirm it is deterministic.

4. Science proof

A glass ashtray works by heat resistance, mass, and surface chemistry. Borosilicate glass has a coefficient of linear thermal expansion of 3.3 times ten to the minus six per kelvin, about a third of ordinary soda-lime glass, so a hot ember on one side and a cool table on the other does not set up enough expansion difference to crack it. Its density is 2.23 grams per cubic centimetre and its specific heat is 0.83 joules per gram per kelvin, so a dish of about 279 grams absorbs heat from embers without its own temperature rising fast, and its thermal conductivity near 1.2 watts per metre kelvin spreads that heat slowly through the wall. Glass is non-porous, so ash and tar sit on the surface instead of soaking in, which is why a glass ashtray washes clean while a wooden one keeps its smell. The dish is a shallow cylinder, so its capacity is pi times radius squared times depth, and the rim notches are simple semicircular cuts that hold a cigarette by gravity. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

There is no genuine home build for a glass ashtray, and this record says so plainly: forming thick glass needs a furnace, an annealing oven, and a trained hand, and glassmaking is a skilled trade, not a home craft. The closest genuine maker activity is making a base and a rest for a bought dish, and it works like this. Start with a bought thick glass ashtray, 130 mm across, and check the rim notches with a cigarette or joint: each notch should hold it without rolling. Then make the accessory a home workshop can do: a cork or felt base pad, cut to a 130 mm circle from 3 mm cork sheet, that sits under the dish and keeps it from sliding or scratching the table. Cut the circle with a craft knife against a plate as a guide, sand the edge smooth, and glue a thin felt ring to the underside if the table is polished. Then make the second accessory a home printer can do: a PETG rim guard that clips over one notch and stops a joint from rolling off when the dish is bumped. That base pad and rim guard are the build; the glass dish itself is bought, not made at home.

3D-print version

The glass ashtray itself is not 3D-printable in glass on a home printer, and this record says so plainly: home printers extrude plastic filament, and no plastic survives contact with hot embers, so a printed ashtray would melt and give off fumes. What is printable is the accessory that goes with it: a PETG rim guard and base ring that fits the 130 mm dish. Print it in PETG, which tolerates warm water and cleaning better than PLA, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, a 0.2 mm layer height, 20 percent gyroid infill, and 3 perimeters, giving a wall about 1.2 mm thick. Print the ring flat on its base with no supports, at a nozzle temperature of 235 degrees C and a bed temperature of 80 degrees C, with the fan at 50 percent. Print time (estimate): about 55 minutes for the ring at standard size. Post-processing is simple: remove any brim, check the 130 mm inner diameter with calipers, and sand the rim lightly if it is sharp. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing all change the real print time and strength, so the estimate is not a validated print job; measure the printed ring before trusting it with a real glass dish.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: glass ashtray capacity and thermal expansion — Signature Glass Ashtray."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    d = 130.0; h = 30.0; wall = 5.0; rests = 4; rho = 2.23; alpha = 3.3e-06; dT = 200.0
    cap_mm3 = math.pi * (d / 2.0) ** 2 * h
    cap_cm3 = cap_mm3 / 1000.0
    outer_mm3 = math.pi * (d / 2.0) ** 2 * (h + wall)
    inner_mm3 = math.pi * ((d - 2.0 * wall) / 2.0) ** 2 * h
    shell_cm3 = (outer_mm3 - inner_mm3) / 1000.0
    mass_g = shell_cm3 * rho
    expand_mm = alpha * d * dT
    return {"capacity_mm3": round(cap_mm3, 2), "capacity_cm3": round(cap_cm3, 2), "shell_volume_cm3": round(shell_cm3, 2), "glass_mass_g": round(mass_g, 2), "thermal_expansion_mm": round(expand_mm, 2), "rim_notches": rests}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ['capacity_mm3', 'capacity_cm3', 'shell_volume_cm3', 'glass_mass_g', 'thermal_expansion_mm', 'rim_notches']:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

8. USE section — use, download, copy

Use the supply

Use, as a program

To use the glass ashtray, set it on a flat heat-resistant surface, rest the cigarette or joint in a rim notch between draws, and tap ash into the dish rather than onto the table. Empty it when the ash reaches a third of the depth, while the embers are out, and wash it with warm soapy water or a vinegar solution to remove tar. Never rinse a hot ashtray with cold water, because even borosilicate glass can suffer thermal shock, and never set it where it can be knocked off a table edge. The program in this record computes what the dish holds and how it handles heat: the capacity from its diameter and depth, the glass shell volume from the outer and inner cylinders, the mass from the borosilicate density, and the thermal expansion over a 200 kelvin rise. Run the demo to see the numbers, then change one input at a time — a wider dish, a deeper bowl, a thicker wall — and watch the capacity and the mass move. The program is the use check: if the expansion it reports is far above a tenth of a millimetre, the glass is not borosilicate, and the reader should re-check the material before trusting it with hot embers.
