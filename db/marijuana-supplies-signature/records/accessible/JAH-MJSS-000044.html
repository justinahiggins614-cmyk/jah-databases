The grow tent is a portable indoor room: a rigid metal frame wrapped in a reflective fabric skin, with zippered doors, duct ports, and a removable floor tray. It descends from the greenhouse and the photographic darkroom, both enclosed spaces built to control light, and it became a standard smoke-shop and garden product as indoor gardening moved into homes and apartments. Early tents were light canvas over thin poles; the modern form uses powder-coated steel poles, corner connectors, and 600D Oxford fabric lined with reflective Mylar, with 1680D canvas as the premium benchmark. The sizes settled on room footprints: 2 by 2 feet for a closet, 2 by 4 for a small room, 4 by 4 as the common mid-size, and larger footprints up to 10 by 10. The record here describes the product — its frame, its fabric, its ports, its tray — and not how to grow anything inside it. Materials and design

A grow tent is a fabric room, and its design is a balance between blocking light, reflecting light, and holding its shape. The skin is 600D Oxford fabric in the standard example, 1680D in the premium version: denier measures the density of the fibres, and higher denier means denser, heavier, more durable fabric that resists pinholes at the seams and blocks outside light better. Inside, the skin is lined with reflective Mylar, rated at 95 percent reflectivity or better, which bounces light back into the space instead of letting the walls absorb it; the premium version uses a diamond-pattern Mylar that spreads the reflected light more evenly. The frame is powder-coated steel poles with plastic corner connectors in the standard example and steel interlocking pins in the premium version, because the frame must hold the skin taut and carry the weight of equipment hung from the roof bars without bending. Zippers are heavy-duty and double-stitched into the skin, because a zipper that splits lets light in and breaks the seal; duct ports are double-cinching so they close tightly around ducting of different sizes. The removable floor tray is waterproof and catches spills, and the observation window lets the inside be checked without opening the door. Tolerances are sewing tolerances: seams are double-stitched, the skin is cut to fit the frame within a few millimetres, and the ports are placed to line up with the roof bars.

4. Science proof

A grow tent works as an enclosed reflective room, and its physics is the physics of light and air in a box. The Mylar interior reflects light back toward the centre of the tent instead of letting the walls absorb it, so the same lamp lights the space more evenly; the sources rate good interiors at 95 percent reflectivity or better. The fabric density, measured in denier, sets how well the tent blocks outside light and resists tearing: 600D Oxford cloth is the common mid-range, 1680D is the premium benchmark, and thinner 210D fabric is more prone to pinholes at the seams. The steel frame carries the load: poles and corner connectors hold the fabric taut and support hanging equipment, and the removable floor tray catches spills so the fabric floor stays clean. Ventilation is sized by volume: the tent in this record holds 104 cubic feet, and the standard rule is one full air exchange per minute, so a fan rated above that volume, with a margin for the resistance of a carbon filter and ducting, is the matching size. Build it yourself

A grow tent is a genuine assembly build, and the kit version is designed to be put together by one person with no tools. Unpack the poles and sort them by length: the four vertical poles, the four roof poles, and the four floor poles for a 4 by 4 tent. Push the floor poles into the corner connectors to make the base rectangle, then push the vertical poles into the same connectors and stand the frame up, then fit the roof poles into the top connectors to close the rectangle. Check that every pole is seated fully in its connector and that the frame stands square before the skin goes on. Pull the fabric skin over the top of the frame like a cover, working it down evenly on all four sides, and fasten the skin to the frame with its internal straps. Close the zippers, fit the removable floor tray inside the base, and pull the duct port drawstrings closed around any ducting. A second genuine maker task is a simple frame from steel electrical conduit for a custom size: cut conduit to the planned lengths, join it with elbow fittings, and hang a fabric cover over it, but the bought kit is the reliable route and the record recommends it. Check the finished tent by standing inside the closed tent in a dark room with a light outside: no light should show through the seams or the zippers.

3D-print version

A grow tent itself is not 3D-printable: its skin is 600D Oxford fabric with a Mylar lining and its frame is steel, and no home printer prints fabric or steel poles, so a printed tent would be a model, not a tent. This record says so plainly and gives directions for printable accessories instead: corner connectors and pole clips. Print the corner connectors in PETG, which is tougher than PLA and holds its shape under the load of the frame: use a 0.4 millimetre nozzle, 0.2 millimetre layer height, 5 walls, and 60 percent gyroid infill, printed with the socket openings facing up so no supports are needed inside the sockets. Print pole clips in TPU at 0.2 millimetre layers and 30 percent infill, so they grip a pole without cracking. Print time (estimate): about 3 hours for a set of eight corner connectors and 40 minutes for four pole clips on a standard desktop printer. After printing, test each connector on a real pole, ream the sockets lightly if they are tight, and check that the frame still stands square. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness changes the socket diameter, supports inside a socket would ruin the fit, nozzle settings change stringing in the sockets, printer motion affects the squareness of the connector, and PETG shrinks slightly as it cools, so print one connector first and test it before printing the set. Printed connectors are spares and replacements, not a substitute for the steel frame.

Print times are estimates, not validated print jobs. Unmodelled real-world factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, material-specific slicing.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: grow tent — Signature Grow Tent."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    l_ft = 5.0; w_ft = 5.0; h_ft = 6.5
    volume_ft3 = l_ft * w_ft * h_ft
    floor_ft2 = l_ft * w_ft
    fabric_ft2 = 2 * (l_ft * h_ft + w_ft * h_ft) + 2 * floor_ft2
    fan_cfm = 600.0
    exchanges = fan_cfm / volume_ft3
    seconds_per_exchange = 60.0 / exchanges
    return {"tent_ft": "5 x 5 x 6.5", "volume_ft3": round(volume_ft3, 1), "floor_ft2": round(floor_ft2, 1), "fabric_ft2": round(fabric_ft2, 1), "fan_cfm": fan_cfm, "exchanges_per_min": round(exchanges, 2), "seconds_per_exchange": round(seconds_per_exchange, 1)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    print(f"tent_ft: {a['tent_ft']}")
    print(f"volume_ft3: {a['volume_ft3']}")
    print(f"floor_ft2: {a['floor_ft2']}")
    print(f"fabric_ft2: {a['fabric_ft2']}")
    print(f"fan_cfm: {a['fan_cfm']}")
    print(f"exchanges_per_min: {a['exchanges_per_min']}")
    print(f"seconds_per_exchange: {a['seconds_per_exchange']}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
