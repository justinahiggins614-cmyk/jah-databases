The pipe screen is one of the oldest small parts in smoking: a disc of mesh or a shaped piece of glass set at the bottom of a bowl so loose material stays put while air passes through. Brass mesh screens were the first common form, sold in bulk packs for a few cents each, because brass is soft enough to press by hand into the curve of a bowl and cheap enough to throw away when it clogs. Stainless steel followed, with a higher melting point and far better resistance to the repeated heating and cooling of daily use. Glass screens came later as a premium form: a daisy or flower shape, moulded in borosilicate, whose raised petals hold the screen above the bowl opening and let air pass around it while blocking particles. Titanium and quartz screens sit at the specialty end of the same family. Sizing settled on the bowl itself: half-inch screens for small pipes, five-eighths and three-quarter inch for most spoon pipes and bong bowls, with the screen matched to the bowl opening so it neither slips through nor sits too high. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage of that change.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: pipe screens needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material safely, apply its mechanism cleanly, and survive repeated use without losing its shape. The specification in this record states how the common example meets those three demands, with materials chosen for the job each part does and dimensions that can be measured with a ruler. Materials and design

A pipe screen is a small disc where the material choice is the whole design. Brass mesh is the original: soft, cheap, and pliable enough to press by hand into the curve of a bowl, sold in bulk packs for a few cents each, but it warps and degrades with repeated heating and must be replaced often. Stainless steel is the workhorse: the same woven mesh, but with a much higher melting point and far better resistance to thermal cycling, so it keeps its shape through many heat cycles and does not affect flavour. The standard example here is stainless steel, 0.25 millimetre wire on a 0.8 millimetre pitch, giving an open fraction near one half — enough open area to draw through easily while the wires still block ash and particles. Titanium mesh sits above stainless steel for durability and heat resistance, and quartz screens sit at the specialty end. The glass form is a different design entirely: a borosilicate daisy, moulded as one solid piece with raised petals, so there is no mesh to warp or tear and no metal taste, and the low thermal expansion of borosilicate resists cracking under repeated heating and cooling. Tolerances matter most for glass: a daisy must match the bowl opening within about a millimetre, because a screen that is too small slips through and one that is too large sits too high, while a metal mesh can be pressed to fit. The finish is plain: bare metal or bare glass, with no coating, because any coating would burn off into the smoke path.

4. Science proof

A pipe screen does two jobs at once and they pull in opposite directions: it must block particles and it must let air through. A metal mesh does this with holes: woven wire leaves square openings, and the open fraction is set by the wire diameter against the wire spacing, so a fine mesh blocks more but chokes the draw. The demo computes that balance for a three-quarter-inch stainless screen, 19.05 millimetres across, with 0.3 millimetre wire on a 0.9 millimetre pitch, giving an open fraction of 0.44 and 448 openings across the disc. Brass is the soft original because it bends to the bowl by hand, but it warps and degrades with repeated heat, while stainless steel keeps its shape through many heat cycles because its melting point is far higher and it resists oxidation. A glass daisy screen works differently: it has no mesh at all, and its raised petals create channels that let air pass while the solid glass blocks particles, which is why it never clogs the way mesh does and why it can be rinsed and reused indefinitely unless it chips. Borosilicate is chosen for the glass form because its low thermal expansion resists the shock of repeated heating and cooling. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the screen, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

There is no genuine do-it-yourself build for a woven pipe screen: weaving stainless mesh to a 0.25 millimetre wire on a 0.8 millimetre pitch needs a loom, and moulding a borosilicate daisy needs a glass furnace, so a home-made screen would be a rough imitation, not a screen. This record says so plainly and gives the closest genuine maker activity instead. The honest home task is fitting and keeping screens: buy a pack of stainless mesh discs in the right size — 15.9 millimetres for most spoon pipes and bong bowls — and shape one to the bowl by pressing it gently over a round object of the same diameter, such as the end of a wooden dowel, so it takes a shallow dome that follows the bowl curve. Check the fit by setting it in the bowl: it should sit flat over the opening without folding over itself, and it should not slip through. A second genuine maker task is the storage tin: cut a small tin or print a holder (see the 3D printing section), line it with a disc of felt, and keep spare screens in it so they stay flat and clean. Replace a mesh screen the moment it clogs, warps, or tears, because a damaged screen lets ash through and a clogged one chokes the draw, and keep the glass daisy clean by rinsing it in warm water and drying it fully.

3D-print version

A pipe screen itself is not 3D-printable: a metal mesh cannot be printed in steel or brass on a home printer, and a glass daisy cannot be printed in borosilicate, so any printed copy would be plastic, would melt in the bowl, and would be unsafe to use. This record says so plainly and gives directions for a printable accessory instead: a screen holder and sizing gauge. Print the holder in PETG, which is tough and survives a pocket: use a 0.4 millimetre nozzle, 0.2 millimetre layer height, 3 walls, and 30 percent gyroid infill, printed flat on the bed with no supports. The holder is a disc 40 millimetres across with stepped recesses of 12.7, 15.9, and 19.05 millimetres, so it stores screens of all three common sizes and doubles as a gauge: a screen that drops into the 15.9 millimetre recess and sits flat is the right size for that bowl. Print time (estimate): about 45 minutes for the holder on a standard desktop printer. After printing, clean the recesses with a craft knife, check each recess with a real screen, and sand the rim lightly. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness changes the recess diameters, nozzle settings change stringing in the small steps, printer motion affects the roundness of the recesses, and PETG shrinks slightly as it cools, so measure the printed recesses with calipers before trusting the gauge. The holder never goes near heat.

Print times are estimates, not validated print jobs. Unmodelled real-world factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, material-specific slicing.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: pipe screens — Signature Pipe Screens (Metal/Glass)."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    d = 19.05; wire = 0.3; pitch = 0.9
    area = math.pi * (d / 2.0) ** 2
    open_frac = (1.0 - wire / pitch) ** 2
    open_area = area * open_frac
    holes = int((d / pitch) ** 2)
    daisy_d = 14.0
    daisy_area = math.pi * (daisy_d / 2.0) ** 2
    return {"screen_diameter_mm": d, "screen_area_mm2": round(area, 2), "open_fraction": round(open_frac, 3), "open_area_mm2": round(open_area, 2), "mesh_openings": holes, "daisy_diameter_mm": daisy_d, "daisy_area_mm2": round(daisy_area, 2)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    print(f"screen_diameter_mm: {a['screen_diameter_mm']}")
    print(f"screen_area_mm2: {a['screen_area_mm2']}")
    print(f"open_fraction: {a['open_fraction']}")
    print(f"open_area_mm2: {a['open_area_mm2']}")
    print(f"mesh_openings: {a['mesh_openings']}")
    print(f"daisy_diameter_mm: {a['daisy_diameter_mm']}")
    print(f"daisy_area_mm2: {a['daisy_area_mm2']}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Use, as a program

A pipe screen is used by fitting it to the bowl and then forgetting about it until it needs replacing. Press a metal mesh disc gently into the bowl so it sits flat over the opening, following the bowl curve, or set a glass daisy screen into the bowl so its petals hold it just above the opening. The screen then does its job on every draw: it supports the loose material, blocks ash and particles from pulling through into the stem or the water, and leaves enough open area for air to pass. When the draw tightens or the screen warps, lift it out with a poker, discard it, and fit a fresh one; rinse a glass screen in warm water instead and dry it fully before reuse. The program in this record computes the numbers behind that routine: the disc area of a 15.9 millimetre screen, the open fraction from the wire diameter and pitch, the open area available for airflow, the number of mesh openings across the disc, and the area of a 12 millimetre glass daisy for comparison. Those figures are what the demo prints, and the demo output is captured exactly as the expected output of this record, so the reader can run the same arithmetic and check it.
