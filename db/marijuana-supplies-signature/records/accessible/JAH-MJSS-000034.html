1. Description and history

The Signature Ash-Catcher Bowl is a Signature-made design study for ash-catcher bowl, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — smoke passes from the deeper Signature bowl into the wider chamber, where it slows and ash falls out into the water; the cleaned smoke continues into the bong — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature borosilicate glass with a coloured trap band; built-in flower bowl fused to the catcher chamber; no metal or plastic in the smoke path. The design is sized at bowl 22 mm across and 18 mm deep; catcher chamber 34 mm across, water depth 25 mm; lower joint 18 mm male at 90 degrees, which sets every number in this record, from the ash-catcher bowl volumes and water fill in the demo to the scale variants at the end. This record is a design study, not a real manufactured product, and nothing here claims the design has been built, tested, or sold. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, and run the program to check every number. The record also gives the materials and design, a build-it-yourself section, 3D-printing directions, and a use program, so the design can be judged as a whole, not only as a set of numbers. Like every Signature record, it sits beside its public twin in the same pair, so the reader can compare the public form with the Signature reworking side by side.

The ash catcher is a recent addition to bong design, because it answers a cleaning problem that only appears once a bong is used often: ash and loose material travel down the downstem and foul the main water chamber, so the whole piece needs cleaning after every few sessions. The first ash catchers were separate glass chambers that sat between the bowl and the bong, and the ash-catcher bowl folds the two parts into one piece by building the bowl into the top of the catcher chamber. The combined form spread through smoke shops in the 2010s as borosilicate glassblowing made the extra chamber cheap to produce, and it is now sold in 14 mm and 18 mm joints at 45 and 90 degree angles to match beaker and straight-tube bongs. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage, and the history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: ash-catcher bowl needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Materials and design

The ash-catcher bowl is a glass-design choice, and the material is borosilicate glass throughout, chosen because it is heat resistant, chemically inert, and transparent enough that the user can see the ash collecting in the chamber. The wall is about 2 mm thick, which is the working compromise: thinner walls cool faster and are lighter but break more easily, thicker walls survive drops better but add weight at the joint and slow the cooling of the smoke. The joints are ground tapers, 14 mm or 18 mm, and their tolerance is what makes the seal: the lower joint must seat in the bong without rocking, and the upper joint must take the bowl slide, or in the built-in form the bowl is fused to the chamber as a single piece of glass, which removes one joint and one leak path entirely. The angle is part of the design, 90 degrees for straight tubes with vertical joints and 45 degrees for beaker bongs with slanted joints, because a catcher that hangs at the wrong angle puts its water line across the smoke path. The finish is clear glass with coloured accents, and the design varies by chamber size, by perc style, and by whether the catcher runs wet or dry, while the working principle stays the same.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: V = pi * r^2 * h

V is the volume of a cylindrical part in cubic millimetres; r is its radius in millimetres; h is its depth or height in millimetres.

The demo treats the bowl and the catcher chamber as cylinders. With a bowl diameter of 22.0 mm and a depth of 18.0 mm, the radius is 11.0 mm, so the bowl volume is pi times 121 times 18, about 6,842 cubic millimetres, or 6.84 cubic centimetres. The chamber cross-section is pi times 289, about 907.92 square millimetres, which is the area that slows the smoke and lets ash fall. 4. Science proof

An ash catcher works by gravity and by impaction, two pieces of physics that need no electronics. Ash particles leaving the bowl are heavier than smoke, so when the smoke stream turns and slows in the wider catcher chamber, the ash falls out of the stream and lands in the water or on the chamber floor, while the gas keeps moving. The chamber cross-section is about 908 square millimetres, far wider than the 18 mm joint, so the smoke slows by roughly the ratio of the areas and the particles have time to drop. Water in a wet catcher adds a second trap: particles that reach the water surface are wetted and stay there instead of bouncing back into the stream. Borosilicate glass matters because it has a low coefficient of thermal expansion, about 3.3 times ten to the minus six per kelvin, so it tolerates the hot bowl end and the cool joint without cracking, and because glass is chemically inert it adds no flavour of its own. The bowl is a shallow cylinder, so its volume is pi times radius squared times depth, and the chamber is a wider cylinder whose water volume is its fill fraction times its full volume. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

There is no genuine home build for an ash-catcher bowl, and this record says so plainly: fusing a bowl into a glass chamber needs a glassblowing torch, an annealing kiln, and a trained hand, and glassblowing is a skilled trade, not a home craft. The closest genuine maker activity is assembling and fitting a bought catcher, and it works like this. Start by measuring the bong joint with calipers or the dime test: a 14 mm joint is slightly smaller than a dime, an 18 mm joint swallows one. Choose a catcher with the matching joint size and the right angle, 90 degrees for a straight tube and 45 degrees for a beaker bong, and check that the catcher hangs upright when seated. If the catcher is a wet type, fill it with water to about 60 percent of the chamber, no higher, so the water line stays below the smoke path. Seat the catcher in the bong joint, seat the bowl in the catcher top, and take a test pull with no herb loaded to feel whether the draw is open and the joints seal. Then make the accessory a home printer can do: a PETG stand that holds the catcher upright on a table so it cannot roll and snap its joint. That stand is the build; the glass itself is bought, not made at home.

3D-print version

The ash-catcher bowl itself is not 3D-printable in glass on a home printer, and this record says so plainly: home printers extrude plastic filament, and no plastic survives the heat at the bowl end, so a printed catcher would melt and give off fumes. What is printable is the accessory that goes with it: a PETG upright stand that cradles the catcher chamber and keeps it from rolling off a table. Print it in PETG, which tolerates warm water and cleaning better than PLA, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, a 0.2 mm layer height, 20 percent gyroid infill, and 3 perimeters, giving a wall about 1.2 mm thick. Print the stand flat on its base with supports under the cradle curve only, at a nozzle temperature of 235 degrees C and a bed temperature of 80 degrees C, with the fan at 50 percent. Print time (estimate): about 1 hour 10 minutes for the stand at standard size. Post-processing is simple: remove the supports from the cradle, check the 34 mm pocket with calipers, and sand the rim lightly if it is sharp. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing all change the real print time and strength, so the estimate is not a validated print job; measure the printed pocket before trusting it with a real glass catcher.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: ash-catcher bowl volumes and water fill — Signature Ash-Catcher Bowl."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    bowl_d = 22.0; bowl_h = 18.0; ch_d = 34.0; water_h = 25.0; fill = 0.60; pack = 0.15; joint = 18.0
    bowl_mm3 = math.pi * (bowl_d / 2.0) ** 2 * bowl_h
    bowl_cm3 = bowl_mm3 / 1000.0
    capacity_g = bowl_cm3 * pack
    ch_mm3 = math.pi * (ch_d / 2.0) ** 2 * water_h
    ch_cm3 = ch_mm3 / 1000.0
    water_ml = ch_cm3 * fill
    trap_mm2 = math.pi * (ch_d / 2.0) ** 2
    return {"bowl_volume_mm3": round(bowl_mm3, 2), "bowl_volume_cm3": round(bowl_cm3, 2), "herb_capacity_g": round(capacity_g, 2), "chamber_volume_cm3": round(ch_cm3, 2), "water_ml": round(water_ml, 2), "water_mass_g": round(water_ml, 2), "trap_area_mm2": round(trap_mm2, 2), "joint_mm": joint}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ['bowl_volume_mm3', 'bowl_volume_cm3', 'herb_capacity_g', 'chamber_volume_cm3', 'water_ml', 'water_mass_g', 'trap_area_mm2', 'joint_mm']:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Use, as a program

To use the ash-catcher bowl, match the joint to the bong, 18 mm to 18 mm, and the angle to the bong, 90 degrees for a straight tube and 45 degrees for a beaker. If it is a wet catcher, add water to about 60 percent of the chamber and no higher. Pack ground herb loosely in the built-in bowl, light the surface while drawing gently, and watch the ash collect in the chamber instead of the bong. Empty the catcher after the session, rinse it, and clean the main bong far less often than before.
