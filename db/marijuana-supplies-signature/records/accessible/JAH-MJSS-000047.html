The carbon filter is a canister of activated carbon that scrubs odour from exhaust air, and it became a standard grow-tent part once indoor gardening moved into shared buildings. Activated carbon has been used to purify air and water for more than a century: its surface is full of microscopic pores, and gas molecules stick to those pores by adsorption, a surface effect, not absorption into the material. The grow-tent form is a steel canister with a flange on one end, a bed of carbon held between two mesh cylinders, and a cloth pre-filter sleeve that catches dust before it reaches the carbon. Common sizes match the common fans: 4 inches by 12 inches for a 2 by 2 or 2 by 4 tent, 6 inches by 16 inches for a 4 by 4 tent, with a bed about 38 millimetres thick of virgin Australian carbon rated at 1200 or higher iodine value. Lifespans vary by use: makers quote 3 to 6 months for heavy use and up to 18 months of continuous use for thick-bed canisters, and the honest rule is to replace the carbon when odour starts to leak. The record here describes the filter as a product — its canister, its carbon bed, its airflow, its lifespan — and not how to grow anything in the space it serves. Materials and design

A carbon filter is a steel canister built around a bed of carbon, and every part of its design serves the airflow through that bed. The canister is galvanised steel in the standard example and stainless steel in the premium version: galvanised steel resists rust and corrosion at a low price, while stainless steel lasts longer in damp air. Inside, the carbon bed is held between two mesh cylinders, an outer mesh and an inner mesh, so air must cross the full 38 millimetre bed to get from the outside to the flange; the bed thickness is the key number, because a thicker bed gives the air a longer dwell time and adsorbs more, and 52 millimetre beds exist for heavier use. The carbon is virgin Australian activated carbon rated at 1200 or higher iodine value, which measures how much the carbon can adsorb, and the sources warn that cheap pelletized carbon performs far worse and lasts months less. The flange is a plain steel ring sized to the duct — 4 inches in the standard example, 6 inches in the premium version — so it fits standard ducting and clamps without an adapter. The pre-filter sleeve is a cloth tube that slides over the canister and catches dust before it reaches the outer mesh, because dust clogs the mesh and shortens the bed life. Tolerances are sheet-metal tolerances: the mesh cylinders must stay concentric so the bed thickness is even all round, or air will take the thin path and bypass the thick part. The finish is plain galvanised or stainless steel, with no paint inside the air path.

4. Science proof

A carbon filter works by adsorption: air passes through a bed of activated carbon, and odour molecules stick to the enormous internal surface of the carbon pores instead of passing through. The bed in this record is 38 millimetres thick, the common RC412 grade of virgin Australian carbon rated at 1200 or higher iodine value, which is a measure of how much the carbon can adsorb. The canister is an annulus: air enters through the outer mesh, crosses the 38 millimetre bed, and leaves through the inner mesh to the fan, so the carbon volume is the volume between the outer and inner cylinders, about 2.22 litres in the 4-inch by 12-inch example. Air must move slowly enough for adsorption to happen: at 205 CFM through the outer surface of the canister the face velocity is about one metre per second, which gives the air a real dwell time in the bed, and running the fan far faster than the filter rating pushes air past the carbon untreated. The pre-filter sleeve matters because dust clogs the outer mesh and shortens the bed life, so keeping it clean is the cheapest way to extend the filter. The bed saturates over time no matter how clean the pre-filter is — the sources put continuous-use life at 12 to 18 months for a good canister, and 3 to 6 months where makers quote a conservative replacement interval — and the demo computes the carbon volume, the face velocity, and the hours in the quoted lifespan so the reader can check the arithmetic directly. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the filter, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

A carbon filter is a genuine maker build in its refillable form, and the sources describe a jar-based version that works on the same principle as the bought canister. Take a mesh canister or a large jar with holes drilled through its walls, and fill it with activated carbon — aquarium-grade granules work — to within a half inch of the rim, so the bed is deep and even. Fit a cloth pre-filter sleeve over the outside to catch dust before it reaches the carbon, and fit a flange or a duct adapter to one end so the canister connects to the inline fan with a hose clamp. Mount the canister inside the tent on straps, connect it to the fan, and run the fan so air is pulled through the bed; the sources note that a jar-based build suits tents up to 3 by 3 feet and works best with a 4-inch fan, while a 4 by 4 tent needs a bought canister with a proportionally larger bed. For a bought canister, the genuine maker task is refilling it: open the end cap, pour out the spent carbon, refill with fresh virgin activated carbon to the same depth, and close the cap tightly so no air bypasses the bed. Replace the carbon when odour starts to leak, and keep the pre-filter clean, because a clean pre-filter is the cheapest way to extend the bed life. Never reuse spent carbon: once its pores are full it adsorbs nothing more.

3D-print version

A carbon filter itself is not 3D-printable: its canister is steel, its bed is activated carbon, and no home printer prints either, so a printed filter would be a plastic tube with no carbon in it and would scrub nothing. This record says so plainly and gives directions for printable accessories instead: a flange adapter and a pre-filter frame. Print the flange adapter in PETG, which is tough and holds its shape in warm air: use a 0.4 millimetre nozzle, 0.2 millimetre layer height, 4 walls, and 40 percent gyroid infill, printed upright with the bore vertical so no supports are needed inside the bore. The adapter steps a 4-inch filter flange up to a 6-inch duct, with a 20 millimetre straight section at each end for the hose clamps. Print the pre-filter frame in PETG at the same settings, a ring that holds the cloth sleeve taut over the canister end. Print time (estimate): about 1 hour 45 minutes for the adapter and 35 minutes for the frame on a standard desktop printer. After printing, test the adapter on the real flange and ducting, ream the bore lightly if it is tight, and check that the frame holds the sleeve without slipping. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness changes the bore diameter, supports inside a bore would ruin the surface, nozzle settings change stringing in the bore, printer motion affects the roundness of the adapter, and PETG shrinks slightly as it cools, so measure the printed bore with calipers before fitting it. The printed parts are accessories, not the filter.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: carbon filter — Signature Carbon Filter."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    outer_d = 150.0; length = 400.0; bed_mm = 38.0; cfm = 402.0; months = 6.0
    outer_r = outer_d / 2.0; inner_r = outer_r - bed_mm
    carbon_vol = math.pi * (outer_r ** 2 - inner_r ** 2) * length / 1000000.0
    outer_area = math.pi * outer_d * length / 1000000.0
    flow_m3s = cfm * 0.000471947
    face_vel = flow_m3s / outer_area
    hours = months * 30.0 * 24.0
    return {"outer_diameter_mm": outer_d, "length_mm": length, "bed_mm": bed_mm, "carbon_volume_L": round(carbon_vol, 2), "outer_area_m2": round(outer_area, 4), "face_velocity_ms": round(face_vel, 2), "lifespan_months": months, "lifespan_hours": round(hours, 0)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    print(f"outer_diameter_mm: {a['outer_diameter_mm']}")
    print(f"length_mm: {a['length_mm']}")
    print(f"bed_mm: {a['bed_mm']}")
    print(f"carbon_volume_L: {a['carbon_volume_L']}")
    print(f"outer_area_m2: {a['outer_area_m2']}")
    print(f"face_velocity_ms: {a['face_velocity_ms']}")
    print(f"lifespan_months: {a['lifespan_months']}")
    print(f"lifespan_hours: {a['lifespan_hours']}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
