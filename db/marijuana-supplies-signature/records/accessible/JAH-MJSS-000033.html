1. Description and history

The Signature Ceramic Bowl is a Signature-made design study for ceramic bowl, a new design in the Signature Marijuana Supplies Database that reworks the familiar public form with one deliberate change. It keeps the job the same — ground herb packed in the deeper Signature cup; the thicker wall stores more heat for an even burn; smoke passes through the cup floor into the downstem; lifting the bowl clears the pipe — but changes how the parts are arranged, using signature glazed stoneware with a heat-band glaze; 18 mm male ground joint; ceramic-coated screen. The design is sized at cup 24 mm across and 18 mm deep; joint 18 mm; wall 3.5 mm thick; about 50 mm tall overall, which sets every number in this record, from the ceramic bowl volume and heat storage in the demo to the scale variants at the end. This record is a design study, not a real manufactured product, and nothing here claims the design has been built, tested, or sold. What it does offer is a complete, checkable design: a full specification, the physics of how it would work, worked arithmetic tied to its own dimensions, a runnable program that computes those numbers, and three boundless scale variants. Readers can follow the design from first principles, repeat the arithmetic by hand, and run the program to check every number. The record also gives the materials and design, a build-it-yourself section, 3D-printing directions, and a use program, so the design can be judged as a whole, not only as a set of numbers. Like every Signature record, it sits beside its public twin in the same pair, so the reader can compare the public form with the Signature reworking side by side.

The ceramic bowl is one of the oldest bowl forms in pipe history, because fired clay predates glassblowing as a craft by thousands of years, and the first smoking pipes in many cultures were clay. When the modern glass bong took its shape in the twentieth century, its bowl was usually glass, but ceramic bowls stayed in use for the same reasons clay pipes had always been used: clay holds heat, it can be shaped by hand, and it takes glaze in any colour. The replaceable slide bowl, sized to a 14 mm or 18 mm ground joint, became the standard fitting, and ceramic makers produced bowls in both sizes with the same joint geometry as glass. Over time the ceramic bowl became a deliberate choice rather than a default, picked by smokers who wanted the heat retention and the hand-made look that glass does not give. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage, and the history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: ceramic bowl needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Materials and design

The ceramic bowl is a materials-science choice, not a styling choice. Glazed stoneware is chosen because fired clay tolerates direct flame without melting, warping, or giving off fumes, and because its low thermal conductivity keeps heat in the cup where the herb is, rather than conducting it down to the joint and the hand. The glaze is a fired glass skin, typically a few tenths of a millimetre thick, that seals the porous clay body so resin and flavour do not soak in; a clear or coloured glaze changes only the look, while a matte or unglazed exterior changes only the grip. The joint is a ground taper, cut to 14 mm or 18 mm, and its tolerance is what makes the seal: a joint ground a fraction of a millimetre too small rocks in the downstem and leaks air, while one ground too large wedges and can crack the glass it sits in. Wall thickness is the design compromise, set here at 3.5 mm: thinner walls heat faster and cool faster but chip more easily, thicker walls store more heat and survive drops better but make the bowl heavy. The finish is a fired glaze inside and out, with the joint left unglazed so the ground glass-to-ceramic contact seals cleanly. Design varies by cup depth, by joint size, and by decoration, while the working principle stays the same.

3. Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: V = pi * r^2 * h

V is the bowl-cup volume in cubic millimetres; r is the cup radius in millimetres; h is the cup depth in millimetres.

The demo treats the bowl cup as a cylinder and the ceramic shell as the difference between two cylinders. With a cup diameter of 24.0 mm and a depth of 18.0 mm, the radius is 12.0 mm, so the cup volume is pi times 144 times 18, about 8,143 cubic millimetres, or 8.14 cubic centimetres. At the stated packing density of 0.15 grams per cubic centimetre the herb capacity is 1.22 grams, inside the 1 to 2 gram range the cited bowl-size guide gives for a 14 mm bowl. The outer cylinder is 31.0 mm across and 21.5 mm tall, so the shell volume is the outer cylinder minus the cup, about 8.08 cubic centimetres, and at a stoneware density of 2.4 grams per cubic centimetre the mass is 19.40 grams. Multiplying that mass by the specific heat of 0.85 and a 200 kelvin rise gives about 3,298 joules of stored heat. Every number comes from the specification in this record, the formulas are shown in full, and the program prints each quantity so the reader can check the arithmetic. The computation runs twice with SHA-256 digests printed, so the reader can confirm it is deterministic.

4. Science proof

A ceramic bowl is a heat-storage combustion cup. Fired stoneware has a density near 2.4 grams per cubic centimetre and a specific heat near 0.85 joules per gram per kelvin, so a bowl of about 19.4 grams stores roughly 3,300 joules when it is heated by 200 kelvin, and that stored heat is what keeps the herb burning evenly between draws instead of cooling between them. Ceramic is also a poor thermal conductor, so the heat stays in the cup wall rather than racing to the joint, which is why a ceramic bowl feels cooler at the joint than a metal bowl of the same size. The glaze matters because it seals the porous clay body: an unglazed interior would soak up resin and flavour, while a fired glaze gives a smooth, non-porous surface that releases residue when soaked. The bowl is a shallow cylinder, so its volume is pi times radius squared times depth, and the joint is a ground taper that seals by contact, not by a gasket. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

There is no genuine home build for a ceramic bowl, and this record says so plainly: making one needs a kiln that reaches stoneware temperatures, and kiln firing is a skilled trade, not a home craft. The closest genuine maker activity is a studio build, and it works like this. Start with a bag of cone 5 or cone 6 stoneware clay, the kind any pottery studio stocks, and wedge it until it is free of air pockets. On a wheel, centre about 150 grams of clay and pull a small cup 24 mm across the inside and 18 mm deep, leaving a 3.5 mm wall and a 3.5 mm floor, then shape the stem below the cup into an 18 mm taper using calipers to check the diameter as you go. Trim the foot when the clay is leather-hard, carve a small hole through the cup floor with a needle tool, and let the piece dry slowly under plastic until it is bone dry, which takes two or three days. Bisque fire it in the studio kiln to cone 04, then glaze the inside and outside with a food-safe clear or coloured glaze, leaving the joint bare, and glaze fire it to cone 5 or 6, the maturity temperature of the clay. Let the kiln cool completely before opening it, then check the joint against an 18 mm downstem and the cup capacity against the demo numbers before using it.

3D-print version

The ceramic bowl itself is not 3D-printable in ceramic on a home printer, and this record says so plainly: home printers extrude plastic filament, and no plastic survives direct flame in a bowl cup, so a printed bowl would melt and give off fumes. What is printable is the accessory that goes with it: a PETG bowl stand and joint protector. Print it in PETG, which tolerates warm water and cleaning better than PLA, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, a 0.2 mm layer height, 20 percent gyroid infill, and 3 perimeters, giving a wall about 1.2 mm thick. Print the stand flat on its base with no supports, the cup pocket facing up, at a nozzle temperature of 235 degrees C and a bed temperature of 80 degrees C, with the fan at 50 percent. Print time (estimate): about 50 minutes for the stand at standard size. Post-processing is simple: remove any brim, check the 18 mm socket with calipers, and sand the rim lightly if it is sharp. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing all change the real print time and strength, so the estimate is not a validated print job; measure the printed socket before trusting it with a real bowl.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: ceramic bowl volume and heat storage — Signature Ceramic Bowl."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    d = 24.0; h = 18.0; wall = 3.5; joint = 18.0; rho = 2.4; cp = 0.85; dT = 200.0; pack = 0.15
    bowl_mm3 = math.pi * (d / 2.0) ** 2 * h
    bowl_cm3 = bowl_mm3 / 1000.0
    capacity_g = bowl_cm3 * pack
    outer_d = d + 2.0 * wall; outer_h = h + wall
    shell_mm3 = math.pi * (outer_d / 2.0) ** 2 * outer_h - bowl_mm3
    shell_cm3 = shell_mm3 / 1000.0
    mass_g = shell_cm3 * rho
    heat_J = mass_g * cp * dT
    return {"bowl_volume_mm3": round(bowl_mm3, 2), "bowl_volume_cm3": round(bowl_cm3, 2), "herb_capacity_g": round(capacity_g, 2), "shell_volume_cm3": round(shell_cm3, 2), "ceramic_mass_g": round(mass_g, 2), "heat_to_200C_J": round(heat_J, 2), "joint_mm": joint}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ['bowl_volume_mm3', 'bowl_volume_cm3', 'herb_capacity_g', 'shell_volume_cm3', 'ceramic_mass_g', 'heat_to_200C_J', 'joint_mm']:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Use, as a program

To use the ceramic bowl, check that the joint matches the downstem, 18 mm to 18 mm, then set a small metal screen in the cup floor if one is used, pack ground herb loosely to the rim, and slide the bowl into the joint until it seats without rocking. Light the surface while drawing gently, then lift the bowl to clear the pipe, and empty the ash only after the ceramic has cooled, because the stored heat lingers in the wall. The program in this record computes what the bowl holds and how much heat it stores: the cup volume from its diameter and depth, the herb capacity from the stated packing density, the ceramic shell volume from the outer and inner cylinders, the mass from the stoneware density, and the stored heat from the mass, the specific heat, and a 200 kelvin rise. Run the demo to see the numbers, then change one input at a time — a deeper cup, a thicker wall, an 18 mm joint — and watch the capacity and the stored heat move. The program is the use check: if the capacity it reports falls outside the 1 to 2 gram range for an 18 mm bowl, the dimensions in the record are wrong, not the arithmetic, and the reader should re-measure the bowl before trusting it.
