The dugout descends from the Prohibition-era hidden stash box: a block of wood hollowed out, or dug out, to carry a small pipe and a pinch of material out of sight. The form survived because it answers a plain need — one measured hit, carried in a pocket, with nothing loose to spill. Early dugouts were hand-carved hardwood boxes with two bored chambers, one for the stash and one for the bat, a short straight pipe the size of a cigarette. The bat itself has older cousins in the chillum and the calumet, straight tubes with a narrow bowl at one end, but the dugout as a kit — case plus bat together — is a twentieth-century smoke-shop form from the United States. Modern makers add a spring-loaded bat chamber, a swivel lid, a poker, and a grinder compartment, and they work in maple, walnut, rosewood, aluminium, and acrylic as well as plain pine. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage of that change.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: dugout one-hitter kit needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material safely, apply its mechanism cleanly, and survive repeated use without losing its shape. The specification in this record states how the common example meets those three demands, with materials chosen for the job each part does and dimensions that can be measured with a ruler. The demo program then checks the numbers, so the reader can see whether the stated dimensions really give the stated capacities.

Materials and design

The dugout case is cut from a close-grained hardwood — maple in the standard example, walnut or rosewood in finer versions — because hardwood bores cleanly, holds a sharp edge at the chamber mouth, and does not split when two parallel chambers are drilled a few millimetres apart. The chambers are bored, not routed: the stash chamber is 16 millimetres across and 70 millimetres deep, and the bat chamber is a plain cylinder that the bat slides into, so the boring tolerance is tight, about plus or minus 0.2 millimetres, or the bat will rattle or jam. The bat is brass, chosen because brass machines to a smooth bore, holds its shape when tapped clean, and does not rust; its copper tip is a separate pressed piece that takes the heat of the flame. The spring in the bat chamber is stainless steel so it keeps its push after thousands of compressions, and the swivel lid is the same hardwood as the case, pinned on a steel pin so it turns without wobbling. The finish is a hard oil or wax, not a thick varnish, because a thick coating chips at the chamber mouths and flakes into the stash. Design varies by maker: some cases add a poker slot or a grinder compartment, some use aluminium or acrylic instead of wood, and some replace the swivel lid with a magnetic cap, but the two-chamber layout and the twist-to-pack bat never change, because they are the whole idea of the kit.

4. Science proof

The dugout works on two small pieces of physics. First, the bat is a straight tube with a narrow bowl, so one draw pulls air through a small charge of ground material and the flame sits below the opening, heating the charge without a large chamber of smoke to clear. Wikipedia records the single hit at about 0.1 to 0.3 grams, which is why the bowl cavity is only a few tenths of a cubic centimetre: the geometry itself sets the dose. Second, the case is a pocket stash box, and its job is containment, not combustion. Hardwood is chosen because it machines cleanly, takes a bored chamber without splitting, and does not pass heat to the hand; the bat is usually brass or aluminium because metal holds a bored bowl and a smooth airway and survives being tapped clean. The twist-to-pack action matters: pushing the bat into the stash chamber and turning it shears a plug of ground material into the bowl, and the plug must be snug enough to stay in place when the bat is held sideways but loose enough to draw air through. That balance is set by the grind and the bowl diameter, and the demo checks the volumes that make it possible.

A careful reader should ask what this record does not prove. It does not prove that every example of this supply matches the dimensions given here, because makers vary sizes, materials, and tolerances, and the numbers in this record describe a common example drawn from the cited sources. It does not prove that the supply performs the same way in every hand, because packing, temperature, and technique change the result, and the demo computes ideal figures from stated dimensions rather than measuring a real piece. It does not prove that a cheaper or thinner version of the same supply is just as good, because the specification states the materials and thicknesses that the numbers depend on. Build it yourself

A dugout case is a genuine woodworking build, and it can be made with a drill press, a block of hardwood, and hand tools. Start with a block of maple 110 millimetres long, 30 millimetres wide, and 20 millimetres thick, and mark the centres of the two chambers on one end face: the stash chamber 16 millimetres across and the bat chamber 9 millimetres across, with at least 4 millimetres of wood between them. Drill the stash chamber first with a 16 millimetre Forstner bit to a depth of 70 millimetres, clearing chips often so the bit does not wander, then drill the bat chamber with a 9 millimetre bit to the same depth. Sand the outside to 220 grit, ease every edge, and sand the chamber mouths lightly so no splinters catch the bat. Fit a swivel lid by drilling a 3 millimetre pin hole through the lid and the case end and pressing in a steel pin, checking that the lid turns freely and covers both chambers. Finish the case with food-safe hard oil, two thin coats, letting each coat dry fully. The bat is bought, not made: a brass tube 76 millimetres long and 8 millimetres across with a bored bowl, which needs a lathe to make safely. Test the finished kit by filling the stash chamber with ground material, pushing the bat in, and twisting: it should pack a firm plug and slide out cleanly.

3D-print version

The wooden dugout case itself is not 3D-printable in wood, and the brass bat is not printable at all, but a printed case and a printed bat holder are both practical accessories. Print the case in PETG, which is tougher than PLA and survives being carried in a pocket without cracking: use a 0.4 millimetre nozzle, 0.2 millimetre layer height, 4 walls, and 40 percent gyroid infill, printed upright with the chambers vertical so no supports are needed inside the bores. Print the bat holder as a separate sleeve in TPU at 0.2 millimetre layers and 20 percent infill, so it grips the bat without scratching it. Print time (estimate): about 2 hours 30 minutes for the case and 35 minutes for the holder on a standard desktop printer. After printing, ream both bores with a drill bit by hand to remove stringing, sand the outside to 220 grit, and wash the parts in warm soapy water. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness changes the fit of the bat chamber, supports inside a bore would ruin the surface, nozzle settings change stringing in the chambers, printer motion affects the roundness of the bores, and PETG shrinks slightly as it cools, so check the bat fit on a test print before printing the full case. The printed case is a functional stand-in, not a wooden dugout, and the record says so plainly.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: dugout one-hitter kit — The Dugout One-Hitter Kit."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    case_l = 102.0; case_w = 28.0; case_h = 18.0
    stash_d = 16.0; stash_h = 70.0; bat_inner_r = 3.0; bowl_h = 12.0
    case_vol = case_l * case_w * case_h / 1000.0
    stash_vol = math.pi * (stash_d / 2.0) ** 2 * stash_h / 1000.0
    bowl_vol = math.pi * bat_inner_r ** 2 * bowl_h / 1000.0
    stash_mass = stash_vol * 0.35
    hit_mass = bowl_vol * 0.6
    hits = stash_mass / hit_mass
    return {"case_volume_cm3": round(case_vol, 2), "stash_volume_cm3": round(stash_vol, 2), "bowl_volume_cm3": round(bowl_vol, 2), "stash_mass_g": round(stash_mass, 2), "hit_mass_g": round(hit_mass, 2), "hits_per_fill": round(hits, 1)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    print(f"case_volume_cm3: {a['case_volume_cm3']}")
    print(f"stash_volume_cm3: {a['stash_volume_cm3']}")
    print(f"bowl_volume_cm3: {a['bowl_volume_cm3']}")
    print(f"stash_mass_g: {a['stash_mass_g']}")
    print(f"hit_mass_g: {a['hit_mass_g']}")
    print(f"hits_per_fill: {a['hits_per_fill']}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Use, as a program

The dugout is used one hit at a time. Grind the material finely, fill the stash chamber, and close the lid. Push the open end of the bat into the stash chamber and twist it gently as you push down; the twist shears a plug of material into the bowl, and the plug should sit snugly enough to stay in place when the bat is held sideways but loosely enough to draw air through. Hold the bat to the lips, light the bowl end, and inhale gently; the charge will glow for a few seconds and give one small hit. Then blow through the bat to clear the ash, tap it against a hard surface if needed, and slide it back into its chamber. The program in this record computes the numbers behind that routine: the case volume, the stash chamber volume, the bowl volume, the mass of material the stash chamber holds at a loose packing density of 0.35 grams per cubic centimetre, the mass of one packed hit at 0.6 grams per cubic centimetre, and how many hits one full stash chamber gives. Those figures are what the demo prints, and the demo output is captured exactly as the expected output of this record, so the reader can run the same arithmetic and check it.
