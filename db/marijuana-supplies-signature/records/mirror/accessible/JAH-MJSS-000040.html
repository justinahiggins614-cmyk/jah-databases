1. Description and history

The chillum is one of the eight supplies built in Wave 2 Lane B of the marijuana supplies pair, a smoke-shop supply with a full description, a specification, a science proof, a math proof, a working demo, and a boundless generator. The chillum works by being the simplest pipe there is: a straight tube, wider at the bowl end and narrower at the mouthpiece, with ground herb packed into the wide end and smoke drawn straight through. It is made from borosilicate glass, and a common example measures about 110 mm long, with a bowl end 18 mm across, a mouthpiece 8 mm across, and a chillum stone, a small fitted pebble or ceramic piece, set inside to stop loose material reaching the mouth. There is no carb hole, so the air path is one straight line, and the draw is direct and quick. The parts fit together in a fixed order, and each part has one job: to hold the material, to apply the mechanism, or to carry the result to the user. Because the parts are simple and the mechanism is mechanical rather than electronic in its basic form, the supply can be understood by inspection, measured with a ruler, and checked by the arithmetic in this record. A chillum is the oldest supply in this lane, and that age is the design, not an accident. This record also gives the materials and design, a build-it-yourself section, 3D-printing directions, and a use program, with scale variants at pocket, standard, and workshop sizes. The chillum is one of the oldest pipe shapes still in everyday use, and its history is older than the bong, the spoon, and the vaporizer put together. It traces back to India, where Hindu holy men, sadhus, used conical clay chillums in religious and communal settings, with the earliest documented use in the eighteenth century and some researchers pushing the first evidence back to the eighth century. The shape spread to Africa and to Rastafarian communities, and it reached the West in the 1960s and 1970s, and the geometry barely changed along the way: a straight tube, wide at the bowl end, narrow at the mouthpiece, with no carb hole, no water chamber, and no moving parts. Over time the same basic form was copied, adapted, and refined by many makers, so the record today covers a family of closely related objects rather than a single patented shape. The sources cited at the end of this record give the reader places to check each stage, and the history matters because it explains why the parts are arranged the way they are: each feature survives because it solved a practical problem for the people who used the supply every day.

The problem it solves

The problem this supply solves is practical and narrow: chillum needs to be prepared or used evenly, reliably, and with ordinary tools, without waste and without guesswork. Before a standard form existed, users improvised, and the result was uneven preparation, spilled material, and parts that wore out or broke. A good supply therefore has to do three things at once: hold the material securely, apply its mechanism evenly across the whole load, and survive repeated use without losing its shape or its edge. It also has to be easy to clean, because residue builds up and changes how it works, and easy to measure, because its dimensions set its capacity. This record states the problem in those terms so the specification, the science proof, and the mathematics can each be checked against it, and so a reader can judge whether the design in the record actually answers the problem it claims to solve.

Materials and design

The chillum is a material-spanning design, and the choice is between clay, glass, wood, stone, and metal. Clay is the original material, chosen by the sadhus because it was everywhere, it tolerates heat, and it can be shaped by hand; glass is the modern choice, chosen here because it is transparent, chemically inert, and easy to clean. Wood is warm in the hand but burns and holds smell, stone is heavy and cool but hard to shape, and metal is tough but conducts heat straight to the mouth. The wall is about 2 mm thick in glass, which is the working compromise: thinner walls are lighter but break more easily, thicker walls survive drops better but narrow the bore. The chillum stone is a fitted pebble or ceramic piece, and its tolerance is the fit: it must sit snugly at the transition between bowl and passage without blocking the air. The bore tolerance is the taper itself: a bore that narrows too fast chokes the draw, while a bore that barely narrows at all gives the smoke no acceleration and no cooling. The finish is clear glass with coloured accents, or unglazed clay with a burnished surface, and the design varies by length, by material, and by whether a safi cloth is used, while the working principle stays the same.

4. Science proof

A chillum works by direct flow, and its physics is the physics of a tapered tube. The bore is a frustum, a truncated cone, wider at the bowl end and narrower at the mouthpiece, so the smoke speeds up as the tube narrows, and the draw feels direct because nothing slows the air down: no water, no carb, no chamber. The chillum stone is the one clever part: a small fitted pebble set at the transition between the bowl and the passage, it acts as a screen that stops ash and ground fragments from reaching the mouth, and it forces the air against the tube walls, which cools the smoke more than a straight empty tube would. The traditional safi, a damp cloth wrapped around the mouthpiece end, cools the smoke further by evaporation, and cupped hands around the mouthpiece form a small chamber where smoke can collect and cool before it is inhaled. Borosilicate glass matters because its low thermal expansion lets the hot bowl end and the cool mouthpiece coexist without cracking. The bore volume is the frustum formula, one third pi times length times the sum of the two end radii squared and their product, and the bowl section is a shorter frustum at the wide end. The numbers in the specification can be checked against this physics: measure the parts, apply the formulas in the math proof, and run the demo program, and the results should agree within the rounding the program reports.

Build it yourself

A clay chillum is a genuine studio build, and this record gives it in full, with one plain limit: it needs a kiln, so it is a pottery-studio build, not a home build. Start with a bag of cone 5 or cone 6 stoneware clay, the kind any pottery studio stocks, and wedge it until it is free of air pockets. Roll the clay into a tapered tube on a flat board, 110 mm long, 18 mm across at the bowl end and 8 mm across at the mouthpiece, using a wooden dowel as a mandrel to keep the bore open: wrap the clay around the dowel, smooth the seam with a damp sponge, and slide the dowel out while the clay is still soft. Shape a small fitted chillum stone from the same clay, a 10 mm ball pressed flat on one side, and set it inside the bore at the transition between bowl and passage while the clay is leather-hard. Let the piece dry slowly under plastic until it is bone dry, which takes two or three days, then bisque fire it in the studio kiln to cone 04, and glaze fire it to cone 5 or 6 if a glaze is wanted, or leave it unglazed and burnished. Let the kiln cool completely before opening it, then check the bore with a pipe cleaner and the bowl capacity against the demo numbers before using it.

3D-print version

The chillum itself is not 3D-printable in glass or clay on a home printer, and this record says so plainly: home printers extrude plastic filament, and no plastic survives direct flame at the bowl end, so a printed chillum would melt and give off fumes. What is printable is the accessory that goes with it: a PETG chillum stand and stone holder. Print it in PETG, which tolerates warm water and cleaning better than PLA, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, a 0.2 mm layer height, 20 percent gyroid infill, and 3 perimeters, giving a wall about 1.2 mm thick. Print the stand flat on its base with supports under the cradle curve only, at a nozzle temperature of 235 degrees C and a bed temperature of 80 degrees C, with the fan at 50 percent. Print time (estimate): about 1 hour for the stand at standard size. Post-processing is simple: remove the supports from the cradle, check the 22 mm pocket with calipers, and sand the rim lightly if it is sharp. Unmodeled factors: wall thickness, supports, nozzle settings, printer motion, and material-specific slicing all change the real print time and strength, so the estimate is not a validated print job; measure the printed pocket before trusting it with a real glass chillum.

6. Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build and its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

"""Demo: chillum bore frustum volume and bowl capacity — The Chillum."""
import hashlib, math
def compute():
    L = 110.0; d_bowl = 18.0; d_mouth = 8.0; od_bowl = 22.0; od_mouth = 12.0; stone_d = 10.0; bowl_len = 30.0; pack = 0.15; rho = 2.23
    R = d_bowl / 2.0; r = d_mouth / 2.0
    bore_mm3 = (1.0 / 3.0) * math.pi * L * (R ** 2 + R * r + r ** 2)
    bore_cm3 = bore_mm3 / 1000.0
    stone_mm3 = (4.0 / 3.0) * math.pi * (stone_d / 2.0) ** 3
    free_mm3 = bore_mm3 - stone_mm3
    free_cm3 = free_mm3 / 1000.0
    Ro = od_bowl / 2.0; ro = od_mouth / 2.0
    outer_mm3 = (1.0 / 3.0) * math.pi * L * (Ro ** 2 + Ro * ro + ro ** 2)
    glass_cm3 = (outer_mm3 - bore_mm3) / 1000.0
    mass_g = glass_cm3 * rho
    d_end = d_bowl - (d_bowl - d_mouth) * bowl_len / L
    bowl_mm3 = (1.0 / 3.0) * math.pi * bowl_len * (R ** 2 + R * (d_end / 2.0) + (d_end / 2.0) ** 2)
    bowl_cm3 = bowl_mm3 / 1000.0
    capacity_g = bowl_cm3 * pack
    return {"bore_volume_mm3": round(bore_mm3, 2), "bore_volume_cm3": round(bore_cm3, 2), "stone_volume_mm3": round(stone_mm3, 2), "free_volume_cm3": round(free_cm3, 2), "glass_volume_cm3": round(glass_cm3, 2), "glass_mass_g": round(mass_g, 2), "bowl_volume_cm3": round(bowl_cm3, 2), "herb_capacity_g": round(capacity_g, 2)}
def main():
    a = compute(); b = compute()
    sa = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(a.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    sb = hashlib.sha256(repr(sorted(b.items())).encode()).hexdigest()
    for key in ['bore_volume_mm3', 'bore_volume_cm3', 'stone_volume_mm3', 'free_volume_cm3', 'glass_volume_cm3', 'glass_mass_g', 'bowl_volume_cm3', 'herb_capacity_g']:
        print(f"{key}: {a[key]}")
    print(f"Run 1 SHA-256: {sa}")
    print(f"Run 2 SHA-256: {sb}")
    print(f"Deterministic: {'MATCH' if sa == sb else 'MISMATCH'}")
if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Use, as a program

To use the chillum, set the chillum stone inside first, then pack the bowl end by pressing it gently into ground herb and turning it a quarter turn each way, until the bowl section is full but not compressed. Hold the chillum at a slight angle, bring the flame just over the edge of the bowl, and start a slow draw, then tap out the ash when the bowl is spent. With no carb, there is nothing to clear: the smoke stops when the herb is gone. The traditional method cups both hands around the mouthpiece to form a chamber where smoke can collect and cool, and a damp safi cloth around the mouthpiece filters the smallest debris. The program in this record computes what the chillum holds and how it draws: the bore volume from the frustum formula, the free volume after the chillum stone, the glass volume and mass from the outer frustum, and the bowl-section capacity from the taper at the wide end. Run the demo to see the numbers, then change one input at a time — a longer tube, a wider bowl end, a bigger stone — and watch the capacity and the free volume move. The program is the use check: if the bowl capacity it reports is far above a gram for a pipe this size, the taper in the record is wrong, and the reader should re-measure the chillum before trusting it.
