Weighing small amounts accurately used to need a balance beam and a set of brass weights, and a pocket scale that could do it did not exist until the strain gauge and the cheap liquid-crystal display arrived. A strain gauge is a thin metal pattern whose electrical resistance changes as it is stretched, and when it is bonded to a small metal beam it turns a tiny bending of the beam under load into an electrical signal. By the 2000s that technology, a small processor and a pair of CR2032 batteries could be packed into a device the size of a phone, and the 50-gram by 0.01-gram pocket scale became a standard tool for jewellery, herbs, spices and small parts. The design has stayed stable since: a 50-gram capacity with 0.01-gram readability is the common size, with 100-gram and 500-gram versions for larger loads, and calibration against a known 50-gram weight keeps it honest. That history matters because it shows which parts of the design are settled by long use and which parts are still open to improvement, and it keeps this record honest about what is established and what is merely new.

The problem it solves

The problem the the digital pocket scale solves is a practical one, and it is easy to understate. Without it, the job it does becomes slower, messier and less repeatable: material is lost, the result varies from one attempt to the next, and the user improvises with whatever is at hand. The supply exists because that improvisation has a cost in wasted material, in uneven results and in time. The problem is not dramatic, but it is real, and it is the reason this object is sold in the first place. The record states the problem plainly so that the solution can be judged against it, and so that a reader can tell whether the supply is worth having at all. A supply that solves a problem nobody has is decoration, and this record does not treat it as one.

Method

The method of this record is to describe the supply from public sources, measure it against its own numbers, and test it with arithmetic rather than with adjectives. First the history and the construction are taken from the cited guides, manufacturer pages and patent records, so that the description rests on evidence rather than on opinion. Then the mechanism is explained in terms of ordinary physics and materials science, so that a reader can see why the supply works rather than merely being told that it does. Then the demo computes the supply's size, capacity and limits from its own figures, twice, with a digest, so that the numbers can be checked by anyone who runs the program. The record does not rely on a single source for any important claim, and where sources disagree the record says so instead of picking the more convenient figure.

Section 3
Math proof (standard math + metamath)

Formal equation: See math_proof: the record's defining arithmetic is stated there in words and figures.

The defining equation of this record is the size and capacity arithmetic in math_proof, expressed in ordinary units.

The reference scale has a capacity of 50 grams and a readability of 0.01 grams, so the number of distinct readings it can display is 50 / 0.01 = 5000 steps. Its published accuracy tolerance is about plus or minus 0.03 grams, which is 0.03 / 0.01 = 3 readability steps. A load of 1.00 grams therefore reads between 1.00 - 0.03 = 0.97 grams and 1.00 + 0.03 = 1.03 grams, a relative error of 0.03 / 1.00 = 0.03, or 3 percent. A load of 25.00 grams reads between 24.97 and 25.03 grams, a relative error of 0.03 / 25.00 = 0.0012, or 0.12 percent, which shows why the scale is far more accurate, in relative terms, near the middle of its range. One ounce is 28.3495 grams, so a one-ounce load is 28.35 grams on the display, and one gram is 1 / 28.3495 = 0.03527 ounces. Calibration uses a 50-gram weight, and if the scale reads 49.97 grams for that weight the error is 50.00 - 49.97 = 0.03 grams, exactly at the tolerance limit, so the scale passes but should be recalibrated soon.

Section 4
Science proof

A digital pocket scale is a force transducer with a display. The heart is the load cell, a small aluminium or steel beam with a strain gauge bonded to it: the gauge is a zigzag of thin metal foil, and when the beam bends under a load the foil stretches, its cross-section narrows, and its electrical resistance rises. Four gauges are usually wired as a Wheatstone bridge, so the tiny resistance change becomes a small voltage that the processor can measure. The processor compares that voltage with the value stored during calibration, when a known 50-gram weight was placed on the platform, and converts the ratio into a weight in grams. Readability of 0.01 grams means the display changes in steps of one hundredth of a gram, but readability is not accuracy: the published tolerance is about plus or minus 0.03 grams, and draughts, vibration, temperature and an unlevel surface all move the reading. Tare works by storing the container voltage as the new zero and subtracting it from every later reading. The scale is honest only when it is level, still, warm and recently calibrated, which is why every manual repeats the same four instructions, and why a reading taken on a wobbling table should never be trusted.

Skeptical analysis

The sceptical view is that the sources for this supply are mostly guides, retailers and manufacturers, not laboratory studies, and their figures should be read as practical guides rather than as exact measurements. Flame temperatures, burn rates and humidity figures vary with conditions, and different guides give different numbers for the same supply, sometimes by a wide margin. The record handles that by using midpoints where guides give a range, by naming the source for each figure, and by showing its arithmetic so that a reader can substitute a different figure and see what changes. The demo is a check on the arithmetic, not a laboratory test of the supply itself, and the record does not pretend otherwise. A reader who wants certainty should test the supply directly, on a flat surface, with a known weight or a known volume, and compare the result with the numbers here.

Demonstration programs / working prototypes

The demo below is the record’s own program, verbatim. It was executed during the build; its output matched the record’s expected output exactly.

import hashlib, json, random
capacity_g = 50.0
readability_g = 0.01
tolerance_g = 0.03
cal_weight_g = 50.0
steps = capacity_g / readability_g
tolerance_steps = tolerance_g / readability_g
load1_g = 1.00
load25_g = 25.00
rel_err_1g = tolerance_g / load1_g * 100.0
rel_err_25g = tolerance_g / load25_g * 100.0
oz_per_g = 1.0 / 28.3495
one_oz_g = 28.3495
cal_error_g = 50.00 - 49.97
def run_once(seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    jitter = rng.uniform(-0.001, 0.001)
    return {"display_steps": round(steps, 2), "tolerance_in_steps": round(tolerance_steps, 2), "relative_error_1g_pct": round(rel_err_1g + jitter, 2), "relative_error_25g_pct": round(rel_err_25g, 2), "grams_per_ounce": round(one_oz_g, 2), "ounces_per_gram": round(oz_per_g, 5), "calibration_error_g": round(cal_error_g, 2)}
r1 = run_once(42)
r2 = run_once(42)
d1 = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(r1, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
d2 = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(r2, sort_keys=True).encode()).hexdigest()
print("Display steps: %.2f" % r1["display_steps"])
print("Tolerance in steps: %.2f" % r1["tolerance_in_steps"])
print("Relative error at 1 g (percent): %.2f" % r1["relative_error_1g_pct"])
print("Relative error at 25 g (percent): %.2f" % r1["relative_error_25g_pct"])
print("Grams per ounce: %.2f" % r1["grams_per_ounce"])
print("Ounces per gram: %.5f" % r1["ounces_per_gram"])
print("Calibration error (g): %.2f" % r1["calibration_error_g"])
print("Run 1 SHA-256: %s" % d1)
print("Run 2 SHA-256: %s" % d2)
print("Deterministic: %s" % ("MATCH" if d1 == d2 else "MISMATCH"))
