Exercise During Cancer Treatment JAH-HCCSE-000001 supported for a specific symptom

Exercise oncology prescribes aerobic, resistance, flexibility, and balance activity dosed by frequency, intensity, time, and type, and titrated against treatment phase, cardiotoxicity, neuropathy, bone metastases, and cytopenias. Supervised programs typically individualise load and monitor adverse events, fatigue scores, and functional capacity rather than prescribing a single universal regimen.

What is claimed: Exercise is claimed by some advocates to fight cancer directly, boost the immune system enough to shrink tumours, and replace drug treatment.

Randomised trials and meta-analyses report moderate improvements in cancer-related fatigue, physical function, and quality of life during and after treatment, with effect sizes strongest for fatigue and functional outcomes. Evidence for effects on recurrence or survival remains observational and confounded, and is not presented here as an established treatment effect.

What studies actually show: Trials show the strongest, most consistent benefit is for fatigue, physical function, mood, and sleep during treatment. No randomised trial shows exercise alone shrinking tumours or replacing chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgery, or other conventional treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Exercise is generally low risk when it is adapted to the person, but bone metastases, low blood counts, heart or lung problems, dizziness, neuropathy, and recent surgery all change what is safe. Stop and seek care for chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting, or new severe bone pain, and clear any new program with the oncology team first.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Mindfulness and Meditation for Distress JAH-HCCSE-000002 supported for a specific symptom

Mindfulness-based interventions (MBIs), including mindfulness-based stress reduction and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy adapted for oncology, train sustained attention, interoceptive awareness, and non-reactive appraisal through formal practice and psychoeducation. Protocols are typically group-based, eight weeks long, and adapted for fatigue and treatment schedules.

What is claimed: Mindfulness is sometimes claimed to heal cancer by reducing stress hormones, reprogramming the mind, or activating self-healing that removes tumours.

Randomised trials report small-to-moderate reductions in anxiety, depressive symptoms, and perceived stress, with some benefits for sleep and fatigue. Effects on immune markers and disease outcomes are inconsistent and preliminary, and are not the basis for recommending MBIs; the supported claim is distress reduction only.

What studies actually show: Studies support mindfulness for distress, anxiety, and mood symptoms in people with cancer. They do not show that mindfulness shrinks tumours, improves survival, or replaces psychological or medical treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Mindfulness is low risk for most people, but intense silent retreats or unguided trauma-focused meditation can worsen anxiety or distress in some people, especially those with post-traumatic stress. Anyone with severe anxiety, depression, or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional care promptly, not rely on meditation alone.

Acupuncture for Nausea and Pain JAH-HCCSE-000003 supported for a specific symptom

Acupuncture and related techniques (electroacupuncture, acupressure) stimulate peripheral afferents at classical points such as PC6 (Neiguan) for nausea; proposed mechanisms include modulation of autonomic tone, endogenous opioid release, and central nausea circuitry. Sham-controlled trials complicate effect attribution because sham needling is not physiologically inert.

What is claimed: Acupuncture is sometimes claimed to unblock energy channels to destroy cancer, rebalance the body so tumours dissolve, and replace chemotherapy.

Guideline bodies endorse acupuncture as an adjunct for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting where standard antiemetics are insufficient, and evidence for pain is mixed, with modest short-term effects in some trials and null results in others. No trial supports acupuncture as an anti-tumour treatment.

What studies actually show: The best-supported use is easing chemotherapy-related nausea and vomiting as an add-on to standard anti-nausea drugs. Evidence for pain relief is mixed and short-term. There is no credible evidence that acupuncture treats cancer itself.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Acupuncture must be avoided or adapted with low platelets, low white-cell counts, bleeding risk, skin infection, or lymphoedema in the treated limb, and only sterile single-use needles from a licensed practitioner should be used. Tell the practitioner about all medicines, especially blood thinners, and report fever, bleeding, or worsening pain promptly.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Ginger for Chemotherapy Nausea JAH-HCCSE-000004 mixed

Ginger's pungent constituents, gingerols and shogaols, antagonise serotonin 5-HT3 receptors in vitro and modulate gastrointestinal motility, which provides a plausible mechanism for antiemetic effects. Clinical trials in chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting are heterogeneous in preparation, timing, and comparator, and are often small.

What is claimed: Ginger is sometimes claimed to be a natural chemotherapy that kills cancer cells, cleanses the body of treatment toxins, and makes anti-nausea drugs unnecessary.

Meta-analyses find modest benefit mainly for acute nausea in some trials, with inconsistent effects on vomiting and delayed nausea, and several well-conducted trials finding no benefit. The honest summary is a mixed, small, symptom-level signal, not an established antiemetic.

What studies actually show: Laboratory studies show ginger compounds affect cancer cells in dishes, which is not evidence of benefit in people. Human trials show mixed, modest results for nausea only, and no evidence that ginger treats cancer or replaces anti-nausea medicines.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Ginger can increase bleeding risk, interact with blood thinners, and worsen heartburn or mouth irritation. People on anticoagulants, with bleeding disorders, or close to surgery should discuss ginger supplements with their clinician, and ginger should never replace prescribed anti-nausea medicines.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Yoga for Fatigue JAH-HCCSE-000005 supported for a specific symptom

Oncology-adapted yoga combines asana (postures), pranayama (breath regulation), and meditation, with intensity reduced and contraindicated postures removed for surgical, metastatic, and cardiopulmonary constraints. Proposed mediators include improved sleep architecture, autonomic regulation, and reduced inflammatory signalling, though mediator evidence is preliminary.

What is claimed: Yoga is sometimes claimed to oxygenate tumours away, detoxify the body of cancer, and heal disease through energy alignment alone.

Randomised trials and meta-analyses report moderate improvements in cancer-related fatigue and sleep quality, and smaller improvements in mood and quality of life. There is no credible evidence that yoga affects tumour growth or survival.

What studies actually show: Trials support yoga for fatigue, sleep, and quality of life during and after treatment. They do not show that yoga shrinks tumours, replaces treatment, or improves survival.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Yoga is low risk when adapted, but inversions, deep twists, weight-bearing on fragile bones, and poses that strain surgical sites or lymphoedema-affected limbs can cause harm. People with bone metastases, recent surgery, or balance problems need an instructor experienced in oncology and clearance from their care team.

Sleep Hygiene for Treatment-Related Insomnia JAH-HCCSE-000006 supported for a specific symptom

Sleep hygiene is the behavioural foundation of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, and includes stimulus control, sleep restriction under supervision, circadian entrainment, and reduction of arousal behaviours. In oncology, insomnia is multifactorial, driven by pain, corticosteroids, hot flushes, anxiety, and treatment scheduling.

What is claimed: Sleep hygiene is sometimes claimed to let the immune system destroy cancer overnight, reset the body so treatment becomes unnecessary, and cure insomnia in every case.

Trials in oncology show CBT-I, of which sleep hygiene is one component, improves sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and sleep efficiency. Sleep hygiene alone has weaker standalone evidence than full CBT-I, so the supported claim is for sleep symptoms as part of behavioural sleep care, not as a standalone cure for insomnia.

What studies actually show: Behavioural sleep care improves sleep symptoms for many people with cancer, especially as part of CBT-I. It does not treat cancer, and sleep hygiene alone is often insufficient for moderate or severe insomnia.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Sleep restriction, a CBT-I technique, can worsen daytime sleepiness and is unsafe for people who drive, operate machinery, or have epilepsy or bipolar disorder unless supervised. Sleep medicines and supplements interact with cancer treatments, so they should never be started without clinician approval.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Psychosocial Support JAH-HCCSE-000007 supported for a specific symptom

Psychosocial oncology spans screening for distress, psychoeducation, cognitive behavioural therapy, supportive-expressive group therapy, and practical care coordination, delivered by psychologists, social workers, and trained peers. Distress is now routinely screened as a vital sign in oncology because untreated distress predicts poorer adherence and quality of life.

What is claimed: Psychosocial support is sometimes claimed to cure cancer through positive thinking, or to be unnecessary because a strong attitude alone determines outcome.

Meta-analyses report consistent small-to-moderate improvements in distress, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and quality of life. Evidence that psychosocial interventions extend survival is inconsistent and contested, and is not the basis for this record's grade; the supported claim is distress and quality-of-life benefit.

What studies actually show: Support consistently improves distress, mood, and quality of life, and can improve treatment adherence. It does not shrink tumours or replace medical treatment, and no attitude, positive or otherwise, determines whether cancer responds to treatment.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Support groups and counselling are low risk, but unmoderated groups can spread misinformation about unproven treatments, and anyone with severe depression, anxiety, or thoughts of self-harm needs prompt professional care. Peer advice should never replace clinician guidance about treatment.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Talk to your clinician before starting, stopping, or changing anything described here, and ask your clinician about any symptom, interaction, or warning in this record.

High-Dose Vitamin C JAH-HCCSE-000008 unproven

Pharmacologic ascorbate achieves millimolar plasma concentrations only by intravenous infusion; oral dosing is limited by saturable absorption and renal clearance. Proposed mechanisms include hydrogen peroxide generation in the extracellular space, epigenetic effects via ten-eleven translocation enzymes, and collagen synthesis support, all demonstrated mainly in vitro or in animal models.

What is claimed: High-dose vitamin C is claimed to kill cancer cells selectively, replace chemotherapy, and cure advanced cancer, sometimes on the basis of laboratory studies or individual stories.

Early uncontrolled reports of benefit were not replicated in randomised trials of oral ascorbate, and modern intravenous trials remain small, often uncontrolled or adjunctive, with no established survival benefit. Intravenous ascorbate remains investigational, and oral high-dose supplements have no demonstrated anti-tumour effect in people.

What studies actually show: Laboratory and animal findings have not translated into proven benefit in people. Randomised evidence does not support vitamin C as a cancer treatment, and no survival or tumour-shrinkage benefit is established.

Evidence grade: unproven.

Safety and interactions: High-dose vitamin C can interact with chemotherapy and radiotherapy, because antioxidants may protect tumour cells as well as healthy cells from treatments that work by damaging cancer cells. It can also cause kidney stones, interfere with some laboratory tests, and is dangerous for people with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency or kidney disease. Never take high-dose vitamin C during treatment without oncology approval.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Talk to your clinician before starting, stopping, or changing anything described here, and ask your clinician about any symptom, interaction, or warning in this record.

Essiac Tea JAH-HCCSE-000009 unproven

Essiac and its variant Flor-Essence are multi-herb decoctions whose composition varies by manufacturer and batch, which alone prevents standard efficacy assessment. Preclinical studies report inconsistent cytotoxicity and antioxidant activity in vitro, with some preparations showing proliferative effects on prostate cancer cell lines, a finding that cuts against the claimed benefit.

What is claimed: Essiac is claimed to cure cancer, detoxify the body, and work where conventional treatment has failed, often through testimonials and historical anecdotes.

No controlled human trial demonstrates anti-tumour efficacy, survival benefit, or symptom benefit attributable to Essiac. Case series are uncontrolled and confounded by concurrent conventional treatment, and systematic reviews conclude the evidence is insufficient to support use.

What studies actually show: There is no reliable human evidence that Essiac treats cancer or improves survival. Laboratory results are inconsistent and do not establish benefit in people, and testimonials cannot separate the tea's effect from concurrent treatment or natural variation.

Evidence grade: unproven.

Safety and interactions: Essiac herbs can cause diarrhoea, nausea, and allergic reactions, and herbal blends can interact with cancer medicines and with blood clotting. Because products are unstandardised, the dose of active constituents is unknown, and people should never delay or replace conventional treatment with Essiac.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Laetrile (Amygdalin) JAH-HCCSE-000010 harmful-interaction risk

Amygdalin is a cyanogenic glycoside hydrolysed by beta-glucosidases, including gut flora, to release hydrogen cyanide; its promotion rests on the discredited theory that cancer cells selectively activate it, a selectivity that human pharmacology does not support. Laetrile was evaluated in a National Cancer Institute-sponsored trial in the late twentieth century, which found no anti-tumour activity.

What is claimed: Laetrile is claimed to be a hidden natural vitamin treatment for cancer, suppressed by medicine, that selectively kills cancer cells while leaving healthy cells untouched.

Toxicology is well documented: cyanide poisoning presents with headache, dizziness, confusion, rapid breathing, low blood pressure, and can progress to coma and death, with risk increased by concurrent vitamin C, which increases cyanide release. Regulatory agencies have banned or restricted laetrile, and no modern evidence rehabilitates it.

What studies actually show: Controlled human study found no anti-tumour benefit from laetrile, and the selective-killing theory is not supported by human pharmacology. Laetrile is disproven as a cancer treatment and carries a real poisoning risk.

Evidence grade: harmful-interaction risk.

Safety and interactions: Laetrile can cause cyanide poisoning, which can be fatal, and the risk rises with vitamin C supplements and with eating apricot kernels. Symptoms include headache, dizziness, confusion, fast breathing, and collapse; anyone with these symptoms after laetrile or kernel use needs emergency care immediately. Laetrile must never be used during or instead of conventional treatment.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Alkaline Diet JAH-HCCSE-000011 disproven

The alkaline-diet hypothesis asserts that dietary acid load acidifies blood and promotes tumour growth, and that alkalinising foods reverse this. Human physiology refutes the premise: arterial pH is regulated within a narrow range by respiratory and renal compensation, and diet alters urine pH, not blood pH, except in severe metabolic derangement.

What is claimed: The alkaline diet is claimed to change blood pH, starve cancer of acidity, and cure cancer through food choices alone.

Tumour microenvironments are acidic due to altered tumour metabolism (the Warburg effect), but this acidity is a consequence of tumour biology, not a dietary effect that eating vegetables reverses. Systematic reviews find no evidence that an alkaline diet treats cancer or improves survival.

What studies actually show: The blood-pH claim is disproven by basic physiology, and no human evidence shows an alkaline diet treats cancer or improves survival. Eating more vegetables is generally healthy, but it is not a cancer treatment.

Evidence grade: disproven.

Safety and interactions: Strict alkaline diets can cut protein, calories, and calcium at exactly the time treatment demands adequate nutrition, leading to weight loss, muscle loss, and weakness. Alkaline powders and extreme restrictions can also disturb electrolytes. Any restrictive diet during treatment should be reviewed by the oncology dietitian.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Fasting-Mimicking Diet Around Treatment JAH-HCCSE-000012 mixed

Fasting and fasting-mimicking diets (FMDs) exploit differential stress resistance: in animal models, nutrient deprivation protects normal cells while sensitising malignant cells to chemotherapy, mediated in part by reduced insulin-like growth factor 1 signalling. Translation to humans is constrained by cachexia risk, glycaemic instability, and heterogeneity of regimens and timing.

What is claimed: Fasting-mimicking diets are claimed to protect healthy cells completely, make chemotherapy far more powerful, starve cancer, and replace standard treatment.

Small randomised and pilot trials report feasibility and some reductions in chemotherapy toxicity markers, but with dropout, weight loss, and no established survival or response benefit. Major guidelines advise against fasting around treatment outside a clinical trial, particularly for patients at nutritional risk.

What studies actually show: Animal findings are promising but human evidence is early and mixed, with small trials, feasibility limits, and no proven survival benefit. Fasting around treatment is investigational and should only be considered inside a supervised trial or with explicit oncology approval.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Fasting during cancer treatment can cause dangerous weight loss, muscle loss, low blood sugar, electrolyte problems, and fainting, and it is especially risky for people who are already losing weight, have diabetes, or are frail. Never fast around chemotherapy without direct oncology and dietitian supervision.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Cannabis and CBD for Symptoms JAH-HCCSE-000013 mixed

Cannabinoids act on CB1 and CB2 receptors and modulate nausea circuitry, appetite, and pain processing; nabiximols and synthetic THC analogues (dronabinol, nabilone) are licensed in some jurisdictions for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting refractory to standard antiemetics. CBD has low affinity for CB1/CB2 and its pharmacology is incompletely characterised, with marketed products frequently mislabelled for CBD and THC content.

What is claimed: Cannabis and CBD are claimed to kill cancer cells, shrink tumours in people, and replace chemotherapy, often by quoting laboratory studies on isolated cells.

Evidence supports a modest role for cannabinoid medicines in refractory nausea and, less consistently, pain and appetite, with significant adverse-effect and interaction burden. In vitro anti-tumour effects of cannabinoids at high concentrations have no established translation to human cancer treatment.

What studies actually show: Cannabinoid medicines have a modest, mixed evidence base for nausea and some symptoms in selected patients. Laboratory cell-killing at high concentrations does not prove benefit in people, and no human evidence establishes cannabis or CBD as a cancer treatment.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Cannabis can cause dizziness, confusion, anxiety, paranoia, and impaired driving, and cannabinoids interact with many medicines through liver enzymes, changing levels of chemotherapy and supportive drugs. CBD products are poorly regulated, may contain undeclared THC, and should never replace prescribed anti-nausea or pain medicines without clinician discussion.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Turmeric and Curcumin JAH-HCCSE-000014 mixed

Curcumin modulates nuclear factor-kappa B, cyclooxygenase, and multiple inflammatory signalling pathways in vitro, but has very low oral bioavailability, rapid conjugation, and poor tissue distribution, which formulation strategies (piperine, liposomes, nanoparticles) only partly address. Human pharmacokinetics therefore poorly match the concentrations used in laboratory studies.

What is claimed: Turmeric and curcumin are claimed to kill cancer cells, reverse inflammation that causes cancer, and work as a natural chemotherapy without side effects.

Small trials report possible symptom and inflammatory-marker effects, with inconsistent results, small samples, and heterogeneous formulations. No trial establishes curcumin as an anti-tumour treatment in people, and its antioxidant and enzyme-modulating properties raise interaction concerns during chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

What studies actually show: Laboratory effects at concentrations not reached in people do not prove human benefit. Human trials show mixed, small symptom-level signals at best, and no evidence that curcumin treats cancer.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Curcumin supplements can increase bleeding risk, interact with blood thinners and with chemotherapy drugs, worsen gallbladder problems, and cause stomach upset. Piperine, added to some products to increase absorption, further increases interaction risk. Discuss any curcumin supplement with the oncology team, especially during chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Mistletoe Extract JAH-HCCSE-000015 mixed

Mistletoe preparations (Viscum album extracts such as Iscador and Helixor) contain lectins and viscotoxins with immunomodulatory and cytotoxic activity in vitro, but preparations are not standardised across manufacturers, routes, and dosing schedules, which undermines comparability across trials. Most trials are small, unblinded or inadequately blinded, and conducted in settings where mistletoe is culturally established.

What is claimed: Mistletoe is claimed to stimulate the immune system to destroy cancer, extend survival, and work as a natural alternative to chemotherapy.

Meta-analyses report inconsistent, small quality-of-life signals with high heterogeneity and risk of bias, and no reliable survival benefit. Mistletoe remains investigational outside its traditional European use, and is not an established anti-tumour treatment.

What studies actually show: Evidence is mixed and low quality for quality-of-life effects, and there is no reliable evidence that mistletoe shrinks tumours or extends survival. It is not an established cancer treatment.

Evidence grade: mixed.

Safety and interactions: Mistletoe injections can cause skin reactions, fever, chills, allergic reactions, and, rarely, severe allergy. It can interact with immune-related treatments and should never replace conventional treatment or be used without oncology approval, especially during immunotherapy.

Does not replace conventional treatment. No holistic approach is a proven cancer cure, and no approach in this database is a cancer cure. Massage Therapy for Pain and Anxiety JAH-HCCSE-000016 supported for a specific symptom

Oncology massage adapts Swedish and light-touch techniques with reduced pressure, modified positioning, and site avoidance for surgical wounds, radiation fields, lymphoedema, thrombocytopenia, and metastatic bone disease. Proposed mechanisms include parasympathetic activation, reduced muscle tension, and gate-control modulation of pain signalling, though mechanistic evidence is indirect.

What is claimed: Massage is sometimes claimed to move toxins out of tumours, break up cancer, or spread healing energy that cures disease.

Randomised trials report short-term reductions in pain, anxiety, and fatigue, with effects that diminish after the session period ends. Evidence supports massage for symptom relief only; there is no evidence it affects tumour biology or survival.

What studies actually show: Trials support massage for short-term pain and anxiety relief in people with cancer. They do not show that massage affects tumours, spreads or removes cancer, or improves survival, and massage must never be applied forcefully to tumours or fragile bones.

Evidence grade: supported for a specific symptom.

Safety and interactions: Massage must be adapted or avoided with low platelets, blood clots, bone metastases, open wounds, radiation burns, or lymphoedema in the treated area, and deep pressure is unsafe in these situations. Only therapists trained in oncology massage should treat people during active cancer care.
