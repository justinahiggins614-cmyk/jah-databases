JAH-R20-SSIM-000001 — Simulation framework — how solar-system collisions are computed

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Uses standard, working physics (Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and momentum) applied to hypothetical collision scenarios. These are computed scenarios, not observed events and not predictions. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

This record defines the simulation framework used by every scenario in this database. The physics is standard and deliberately simple: Newtonian gravity between point masses, conservation of linear momentum and energy, and order-of-magnitude impact-energy scaling. Each scenario states its inputs (masses, velocities, impact parameter), its assumptions (spherical bodies, no fragmentation modeling beyond energy partitioning), and its outputs (released energy, momentum transfer, ejecta estimate). Nothing here uses speculative physics; the Signature concept layer is confined to clearly labeled interpretation sections.

The framework has three scenario classes. Class A: impactor-on-target (asteroid on planet, comet on moon) — kinetic energy of the impactor converts to heat, excavation, and ejecta. Class B: body-body merger (two planets, planet-star) — gravitational binding plus orbital kinetic energy set the scale. Class C: tidal/disruption events (body passing within a Roche limit) — no collision, but the near-miss physics is included because the database covers colliders broadly.

Every scenario record carries the same honesty header: inputs are public astronomical data or stated hypotheticals, the computation is standard physics, and the result is a scenario outcome, not a forecast. The generator extends the database by varying inputs within physically stated ranges — larger impactors, different velocities — never by inventing new physics.

JAH-R20-SSIM-000002 — Scenario: Chicxulub-class asteroid impact on Earth

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Uses standard, working physics (Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and momentum) applied to hypothetical collision scenarios. These are computed scenarios, not observed events and not predictions. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class A (impactor-on-target), modeled on the public geological record of the Chicxulub impact 66 million years ago. Inputs: a stony asteroid about 10 km in diameter (density ~3,000 kg/m^3, mass ~1.6 x 10^15 kg) striking at ~20 km/s — a typical Earth-crossing velocity. Assumption: vertical impact for the energy estimate; real impacts are usually oblique, which changes cratering efficiency but not the order of magnitude.

Computation: kinetic energy E = 1/2 m v^2 gives roughly 3 x 10^23 joules — about 70 million megatons of TNT, or some billion times the energy of the largest nuclear weapon ever tested. Standard impact scaling partitions this into: excavation of a transient crater ~100 km wide, global ejecta, and enough dust and sulfate aerosols lofted into the stratosphere to cool the planet for years — the mechanism behind the end-Cretaceous extinction in the public record.

The simulation's value is comparative: run the same framework with a 1 km impactor and the energy drops by a factor of 1,000 (mass scales with diameter cubed); with a 100-metre impactor, by a factor of a million — city-scale rather than global. The generator produces this ladder of scenarios so the database teaches energy scaling, not fear. Every variant is labeled hypothetical; the Chicxulub parameters themselves are public science.

JAH-R20-SSIM-000003 — Scenario: Theia-class giant impact — making a Moon

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Uses standard, working physics (Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and momentum) applied to hypothetical collision scenarios. These are computed scenarios, not observed events and not predictions. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class B (body-body merger), modeled on the public giant-impact hypothesis for the Moon's formation. Inputs: a Mars-sized body ("Theia", ~6 x 10^23 kg, roughly a tenth of Earth's mass) striking the proto-Earth at ~10-15 km/s at an oblique angle about 4.5 billion years ago. Assumption: the collision is modeled as momentum-conserving merger with a debris disk; the detailed disk physics is simplified to angular-momentum bookkeeping.

Computation: the impactor's kinetic energy is on the order of 10^31 joules — enough to melt both bodies entirely and vaporize vast quantities of rock. Conservation of angular momentum requires the debris disk to carry the system's spin; the present Earth-Moon angular momentum is the check the scenario must pass, and in the standard models it does. The Moon then accretes from the disk over centuries to millennia in the models.

The simulation explores variants the public literature also explores: different impactor masses, different angles, faster or slower collisions — each scored against the same observables (Moon's mass, its iron-poor composition, the system's angular momentum). Variants that fail the observables are kept in the database as failed scenarios, labeled as such: the database teaches by showing what doesn't work, using only standard mechanics.

JAH-R20-SSIM-000004 — Scenario: comet plunging into the Sun

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Uses standard, working physics (Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and momentum) applied to hypothetical collision scenarios. These are computed scenarios, not observed events and not predictions. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class A at stellar scale: a comet falling into the Sun. Inputs: a 10 km icy body (density ~600 kg/m^3) on a near-parabolic sungrazing orbit, reaching ~600 km/s at the solar surface — the Sun's escape velocity, which any infalling body approaches. Assumption: the body is treated as strengthless once inside the corona; real sungrazers (like the Kreutz family observed by SOHO) sublimate and disrupt before reaching the photosphere.

Computation: kinetic energy at impact is ~10^23 joules — comparable to the Chicxulub scenario's energy, but released into a star. Against the Sun's luminosity (3.8 x 10^26 watts), the impact's total energy equals about four minutes of solar output — utterly negligible to the star, though locally violent. Tidal forces disrupt the comet when it crosses the Roche limit (~1-2 solar radii for a strengthless icy body), so the "collision" is really a disruption followed by vaporization.

The scenario teaches scale relativity: the same energy that is globally catastrophic on a planet is a rounding error on a star. The generator extends this to planet-star mergers (a Jupiter into the Sun: ~10^33 J, still small against the star) and to white-dwarf pollution scenarios, all with standard physics and labeled assumptions.

JAH-R20-SSIM-000005 — Scenario: two gas giants colliding

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Uses standard, working physics (Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and momentum) applied to hypothetical collision scenarios. These are computed scenarios, not observed events and not predictions. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class B: two Jupiter-mass planets colliding head-on at their mutual escape velocity (~60 km/s). Inputs: each 1.9 x 10^27 kg, mostly hydrogen-helium. Assumption: treated as fluid bodies; the collision thermalizes orbital kinetic energy into heat, with no nuclear ignition (far below stellar-core conditions).

Computation: kinetic energy ~10^36 joules — a million times the Chicxulub energy, released as heat and light over weeks to months. The merged body is a ~2-Jupiter-mass planet, briefly thousands of kelvin at the surface, radiating the energy away over millennia. Momentum conservation is trivially satisfied in a head-on merger; oblique variants spin up the merged planet, with the spin rate set by the impact parameter.

The public astronomy connection: such collisions likely happened during planetary-system formation (the Nice model's reshuffling), and free-floating planetary-mass objects observed by telescopes may include merger products. The simulation does not claim any specific observed merger — it computes what the physics says such an event looks like, so observers know what to look for: a hot, inflated, rapidly rotating young giant planet. Labeled scenario, standard physics.

JAH-R20-SSIM-000006 — Scenario: Earth's Moon spiraling outward — tidal collider in reverse

SIMULATION — Signature-labeled. Uses standard, working physics (Newtonian gravity, conservation of energy and momentum) applied to hypothetical collision scenarios. These are computed scenarios, not observed events and not predictions. Standard physics only; no speculative axioms.

Scenario class C (tidal/disruption, run in reverse): the Earth-Moon system as a slow-motion un-collider. Public facts: the Moon recedes ~3.8 cm per year, Earth's day lengthens ~2.3 ms per century, driven by tidal friction. Assumption: constant tidal dissipation for the extrapolation (real dissipation varies with continental configuration).

Computation: run backward, the Moon was much closer in the past — consistent with a formation just outside the Roche limit. Run forward ~50 billion years, the system would reach tidal lock with a ~47-day month — but the Sun becomes a red giant in ~5 billion years first, so the scenario is explicitly counterfactual: it shows what tidal physics alone would do, labeled as physics extrapolation, not prediction.

The database includes this because colliders are about gravitational encounters broadly, and tides are the gentlest encounter. The generator produces tidal scenarios for other pairs (Mars-Phobos, which spirals inward toward future disruption; Pluto-Charon, already doubly locked) with the same standard tidal equations and labeled extrapolations.
