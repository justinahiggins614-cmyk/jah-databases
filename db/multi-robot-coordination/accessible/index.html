Auction-Based Task Allocation for Robot Teams

Market bidding that assigns each job to the robot that can do it cheapest.

Auction-Based Task Allocation for Robot Teams is a public record about Market bidding that assigns each job to the robot that can do it cheapest. In market-based allocation each robot bids a cost for each task, and the task goes to the lowest bidder, so the team splits work without a central planner dictating every move. The Contract Net Protocol, published by Reid Smith in 1980, is the classic form: a manager announces a task, contractors bid, and the manager awards the contract to the best bid. Auctions run in rounds, so when a new task appears or a robot fails, the team re-auctions the affected work and keeps going instead of stopping. A bid usually combines travel distance, battery left, and how well the robot's tools fit the task, so the cheapest bid is also the most sensible assignment. Consensus Algorithms for Robot Teams

Agreement rules that let robots settle on one shared value without a leader.

Consensus Algorithms for Robot Teams is a public record about Agreement rules that let robots settle on one shared value without a leader. A consensus algorithm lets every robot in a team converge on the same number, such as a meeting point or a shared map coordinate, by repeatedly averaging what its neighbours believe. The classic result is that a connected communication graph is enough: if every robot can eventually hear from every other robot through some chain of neighbours, the team agrees. Consensus runs in rounds, and each round shrinks the disagreement, so after enough rounds all robots hold values within a small tolerance of each other. Raft and Paxos are the best-known consensus protocols in distributed computing, and robot teams adapt the same idea to moving machines with changing radio links. Formation Control for Mobile Robot Teams

Rules that hold a team of robots in a chosen shape while it moves.

Formation Control for Mobile Robot Teams is a public record about Rules that hold a team of robots in a chosen shape while it moves. Formation control keeps robots in a chosen shape, such as a line, a wedge or a grid, while the whole shape moves toward a goal, so the team can sweep an area or carry a load together. In leader-follower formation, one robot leads and the others hold fixed offsets from it; in virtual-structure formation, the robots hold offsets from a moving imaginary shape instead. Behaviour-based formation gives each robot simple rules, such as stay near your neighbour and avoid collisions, and the shape emerges from those rules without any robot knowing the whole plan. Formations are used by drone light shows, where hundreds of drones hold positions in the sky, and by warehouse robots moving in lanes. Coverage and Patrolling by Robot Teams

Sweeping a whole area so no part is left unvisited or unwatched.

Coverage and Patrolling by Robot Teams is a public record about Sweeping a whole area so no part is left unvisited or unwatched. Coverage means visiting every part of an area, and patrolling means visiting it again and again, so robot teams use both to search fields, inspect warehouses and watch borders. A common method splits the area into cells, gives each robot a set of cells, and lets each robot sweep its cells in a back-and-forth lawnmower pattern. When the area is unknown, robots build a map as they go and mark cells visited, so the team can prove nothing was skipped once every cell is marked. Patrolling adds a freshness rule: each place must be revisited before a set time passes, so the team schedules visits by how long each place has waited. Flocking Rules for Robot Teams

Three simple steering rules that turn a crowd of robots into a flock.

Flocking Rules for Robot Teams is a public record about Three simple steering rules that turn a crowd of robots into a flock. Craig Reynolds described flocking in 1987 with three rules: separation, steer away from neighbours that are too close; alignment, steer toward the average heading of neighbours; and cohesion, steer toward the average position of neighbours. Boids is the name of Reynolds' simulation, and the same three rules steer robot teams, drone shows and animated crowds today. Each robot only watches its nearby neighbours, so the flock needs no leader and no central map, yet the whole group turns, splits around obstacles and reforms. Flocking works best when the neighbour radius is large enough to keep the group together but small enough that each robot only tracks a handful of neighbours. Velocity Obstacles for Collision Avoidance

Geometry that tells a robot which speeds would cause a crash, so it can pick a safe one.

Velocity Obstacles for Collision Avoidance is a public record about Geometry that tells a robot which speeds would cause a crash, so it can pick a safe one. A velocity obstacle is the set of velocities that would make a robot collide with a moving obstacle, so a robot that picks a velocity outside that set stays safe. The method was introduced by Fiorini and Shiller in 1998, and it works by drawing a cone of dangerous velocities from the robot toward each obstacle's future position. Reciprocal velocity obstacles share the avoiding work between two moving robots, so each one only changes its velocity halfway, which stops the two robots from dodging into each other. Modern robot fleets use velocity obstacles because the check is a small piece of geometry, fast enough to run many times a second. Distributed Mapping by Robot Teams

Many robots building one shared map at the same time.

Distributed Mapping by Robot Teams is a public record about Many robots building one shared map at the same time. Simultaneous localization and mapping, called SLAM, means a robot builds a map of an unknown place while working out where it is inside that map, and a team does it together by sharing pieces. In distributed SLAM each robot maps its own area, then the robots exchange map pieces and stitch them into one shared map using landmarks both robots have seen. The hard part is recognising the same landmark from two robots' different viewpoints, which is called loop closure, and a wrong match bends the whole map. Modern teams use lidar, which is a laser scanner that measures distances, and cameras together, so the map holds both shapes and pictures. Communication Graphs for Robot Teams

The radio links between robots, treated as a graph that must stay connected.

Communication Graphs for Robot Teams is a public record about The radio links between robots, treated as a graph that must stay connected. A communication graph treats each robot as a dot and each radio link as a line, so the team's ability to share information becomes a question about the shape of the graph. The graph is connected when there is a path of links between every pair of robots, and most coordination methods fail if the graph splits into separate pieces. Algebraic connectivity is a single number that measures how well connected the graph is, and robot teams steer to keep it above a safe threshold. When a robot moves out of radio range the graph loses links, so teams plan paths that keep the graph connected while still reaching their goals. Grid Path Planning for Robot Teams

Finding the shortest route across a grid, one robot at a time.

Grid Path Planning for Robot Teams is a public record about Finding the shortest route across a grid, one robot at a time. A-star search, written A*, finds the shortest path across a grid by scoring each step with the distance travelled so far plus a straight-line guess of the distance left. A* was published by Hart, Nilsson and Raphael in 1968, and it is still the standard planner for grid maps because it is fast and its result is provably the shortest path. Robot teams plan one robot at a time and then fix conflicts: when two planned paths cross at the same time, the lower-priority robot waits or takes a detour. Grid planning is used in warehouses, where the floor is a grid of aisles and each robot needs a route that does not block the others. Sampling-Based Motion Planning for Robot Teams

Growing random trees through free space until a path appears.

Sampling-Based Motion Planning for Robot Teams is a public record about Growing random trees through free space until a path appears. Rapidly-exploring random trees, called RRT, grow a tree of random samples through free space, so the tree explores quickly and finds a path even in cluttered rooms. RRT was introduced by LaValle and Kuffner in 1998, and RRT* improves it by rewiring the tree to shorten the path as more samples arrive. Robot teams grow one tree per robot and then check the trees against each other, so two robots never plan to occupy the same place at the same time. Sampling planners shine where grids fail, such as robot arms with many joints, because they never need to list every possible position. The Robot Operating System 2 for Robot Teams

The shared software layer that lets robots talk, share sensors and run the same code.

The Robot Operating System 2 for Robot Teams is a public record about The shared software layer that lets robots talk, share sensors and run the same code. The Robot Operating System, called ROS, is a set of software libraries and tools that help build robot programs, and ROS 2 is the newer version built for teams of robots and real-time control. ROS 2 uses the Data Distribution Service, called DDS, as its messaging layer, so robots discover each other on a network and exchange messages without a central server. A ROS 2 program is built from nodes, small programs that publish messages on named topics and subscribe to the topics they need, which keeps each part simple and replaceable. ROS 2 is open source, maintained by the Open Robotics community, and used in research labs, warehouses and self-driving vehicle prototypes. Behaviour Trees for Robot Teams

Tree-shaped plans that switch a robot between jobs as conditions change.

Behaviour Trees for Robot Teams is a public record about Tree-shaped plans that switch a robot between jobs as conditions change. A behaviour tree is a plan shaped like a tree: the leaves are actions such as drive or grasp, and the branches decide which action runs next based on what the robot senses. Behaviour trees tick from the root many times a second, so a robot can abandon a failing action and try another one immediately, which makes them tougher than fixed step-by-step scripts. Sequence branches run their children in order until one fails, while fallback branches try children in order until one succeeds, so a tree can express both careful plans and backup plans. Behaviour trees are used in games, robot soccer and warehouse robots, where plans must change quickly when the world changes. Map Merging for Robot Teams

Stitching the maps of several robots into one shared map.

Map Merging for Robot Teams is a public record about Stitching the maps of several robots into one shared map. Map merging takes the separate maps built by several robots and joins them into one map, so the team can plan on shared ground instead of each robot trusting only its own map. The merge searches for overlapping landmarks or shapes in two maps, then computes the rotation and shift that lines them up, which is the same geometry used to stitch photographs into a panorama. A wrong overlap can fold two different corridors into one, so merges are checked by how well the overlapped landmarks agree after alignment. Merged maps let a team hand work to each other: a robot that mapped a room can send a second robot straight to a shelf inside it. Fault Tolerance in Robot Teams

Keeping the mission going when a robot breaks, stalls or loses radio contact.

Fault Tolerance in Robot Teams is a public record about Keeping the mission going when a robot breaks, stalls or loses radio contact. Fault tolerance means the team still finishes its mission when a robot fails, by noticing the failure, reassigning its work and carrying on with the robots that remain. Robots watch each other with heartbeats, short messages sent on a schedule, and a robot that misses several heartbeats in a row is treated as failed. Redundancy is the price of tolerance: a team that needs four robots to do a job carries five or six, so one failure never leaves a job uncovered. Graceful degradation means the team does less, but still something useful, when too many robots fail, such as mapping half the building instead of none of it. Heterogeneous Robot Teams is a public record about Teams that mix different kinds of robots, each doing what it does best. A heterogeneous team mixes different robot types, such as flying drones that scout from above, wheeled robots that carry loads, and legged robots that climb stairs, so each job goes to the type built for it. The hard part is a shared language: a drone measures the world in the air while a wheeled robot measures it on the floor, so the team needs one shared map and one shared task list. Heterogeneous teams are stronger than uniform teams for mixed missions, such as disaster response, where scouting, carrying and digging all happen at once. The trade is complexity: more types mean more software, more spare parts and more ways for the robots to misunderstand each other. Human-Swarm Interaction

How one person guides, watches and trusts a whole swarm of robots.

Human-Swarm Interaction is a public record about How one person guides, watches and trusts a whole swarm of robots. Human-swarm interaction studies how a single operator guides many robots at once, since a person cannot steer fifty robots one by one. Operators usually command the swarm as a whole, by drawing an area to search or pointing at a goal, and the swarm's own rules turn that command into fifty individual movements. Good interfaces show the swarm as one shape with a health summary, rather than fifty separate dots, so the operator can see at a glance whether the swarm is working. Trust matters: operators who can see what the swarm is doing and why are more willing to let it work without constant checking. Underwater Vehicle Teams is a public record about Teams of underwater robots mapping the sea floor and inspecting pipes together. Autonomous underwater vehicles, called AUVs, are robots that swim on their own, and teams of them map the sea floor, inspect pipelines and track ocean currents. Radio does not travel underwater, so AUV teams communicate with sound, called acoustic modems, which is slow and short-range, so the robots must plan long stretches without talking. Teams split the sea floor into lanes, each AUV maps its lanes with sonar, which is sound-based imaging, and the lanes are stitched into one map when the vehicles surface. Underwater teams are used to find shipwrecks, inspect offshore wind farms and study marine life without disturbing it.
