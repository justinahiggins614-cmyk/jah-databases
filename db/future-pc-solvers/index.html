Cooling a Petascale Desktop: Removing Heat No Fan Can Move Rank 1 · simple

Feasibility: breakthrough-dependent The design needs a specific breakthrough that has not happened: liquid cooling built into the chip package at desktop cost and reliability.

A design for a desktop computer that performs a thousand trillion operations per second and removes its own heat without any fan: liquid channels, phase-change surfaces, and heat spreaders built into the chip itself. This is a design, not a real product.

A desktop that performs a thousand trillion operations per second makes heat faster than any fan can move it. Air cooling fails because air carries too little heat per volume; a fan big enough to move the heat would be louder than a vacuum cleaner and larger than the desk. The heat has nowhere to go: the chip surface is small, the case is closed, and the room is warm. Without a new way to remove heat, the machine must slow itself down to survive, which defeats the purpose of building it fast. The problem is not making the processors faster; it is moving the heat they make, watt by watt, out of a box that sits on a desk.

Worked example: a petascale desktop running a full simulation makes heat at a rate that would melt its own solder in minutes without cooling; the liquid channels carry that heat to the case skin, which stays warm to the touch while the cores stay inside their limit.

History

Cooling faster machines descends from the history of computing, where each generation needed a new way to shed heat, from room-sized air conditioning for early mainframes to heat pipes in laptops; the petascale desktop is the same problem at a density no fan can serve.

Proposed solution

Route liquid cooling channels through the chip package itself, so heat meets coolant at the source; Use phase-change surfaces that absorb heat as liquid turns to vapor; Spread the remaining heat across the whole case, turning the case into a radiator; Throttle only the hottest cores, never the whole machine, when heat spikes; Return the collected heat to the room as warm air, measured and logged.

Dependencies

A coolant that stays liquid at chip temperatures; phase-change materials that survive thousands of cycles; chip packaging that carries liquid channels; a standard for measuring heat removal at the chip.

Feasibility

A Processor That Rewires Itself for Each Task Rank 2 · simple

Feasibility: breakthrough-dependent The design needs a specific breakthrough that has not happened: rewiring a full desktop processor in less time than a task takes to start, with proven correctness every time.

A design for a processor that rearranges its own circuits for each task: video work gets video circuits, number work gets number circuits, and the rewiring happens in less time than the task takes to start. This is a design, not a real product.

A fixed processor wastes most of its circuits on every task: a chip built for video spends its number-crunching circuits idle, and a chip built for numbers spends its video circuits idle. The waste is not small; it is most of the chip, most of the time. A processor that rewires itself for each task would use only the circuits the task needs, arranged the way the task needs them, and would run cooler and faster for it. The problem is the rewiring itself: it must happen fast enough that the task never waits, it must never lose the machine's state, and it must be proven correct every time, because a wrongly wired processor computes wrong answers quickly.

Worked example: a video task loads a video circuit pattern, edits a full-length recording without stutter, and the chip returns to its general pattern when the edit is done, having used only the circuits the task needed.

History

Rewiring a processor for each task descends from field-programmable circuits, which let engineers reshape hardware after it was built, and from the old idea of a machine that changes its shape to fit its work; the desktop version needs that reshaping to happen in milliseconds, not minutes.

Proposed solution

Describe each task as a circuit pattern the processor can become; Store the patterns in fast memory, ready to load in less time than the task takes to start; Rewire the chip between tasks, keeping the machine's state safe while the circuits change; Check the new wiring against the task's requirements before running it; Return the chip to a general pattern when no task is running.

Dependencies

Reconfigurable circuit technology at desktop scale; fast pattern memory; a way to prove a rewiring correct before running it; a standard for describing tasks as circuit patterns.

Feasibility

Memory That Never Forgets and Never Wears Out Rank 3 · simple

Feasibility: breakthrough-dependent The design needs a specific breakthrough that has not happened: a memory material that holds state without power and survives unlimited writes.

A design for computer memory that keeps its contents with no power, and can be written as many times as its owner likes without wearing out. This is a design, not a real product.

Today's memory forces a choice: fast memory forgets when the power goes, and memory that remembers wears out a little every time it is written. The choice costs users their work when the power fails, and costs machines their storage when the writes add up. A memory that never forgets and never wears out would end both costs at once: the machine could lose power at any moment and lose nothing, and it could be written all day, every day, for years, without degrading. The problem is the material: no known memory material holds its state without power and survives unlimited writes, and the design must name the material it needs before it can claim the result.

Worked example: a writer's machine loses power in the middle of a sentence, and when power returns, the sentence is there, the document is whole, and the memory reports zero wear after a year of daily writing.

History

Memory that remembers without power descends from core memory and from modern non-volatile storage, both of which kept their contents without power but wore out with writing; the unworn version is the missing material, not the missing idea.

Proposed solution

Choose a memory material that holds its state with no power applied; Prove the material survives repeated writes without degrading; Build the memory so a power cut loses nothing, not even the last write; Make the memory fast enough to replace both kinds of memory in use today; Test the memory by writing it continuously and reading it back without error.

Dependencies

A non-volatile memory material with no wear limit; error correction for that material; a standard for proving wear-free operation; power-cut testing methods.

Feasibility

Feasibility: research-stage Public research on fault detection, self-healing hardware, and redundant systems exists and is published, but no complete self-repairing desktop exists at the needed scale yet.

A design for a desktop that finds its own hardware faults, isolates them, and repairs or routes around them without a human opening the case. This is a design, not a real product.

When a PC's hardware fails, the owner usually finds out late: a file is already corrupt, a program has already crashed, or the machine will not start at all. Repair means opening the case, finding the faulty part, and replacing it, which most owners cannot do and most machines cannot wait for. A PC that repairs its own faults would find the fault early, isolate it before it spreads, and either fix it or route around it, so the owner keeps working while the machine heals. The problem is trust: a repair the machine makes on its own must be proven correct, must never destroy good data, and must be logged so the owner can see what was changed and why.

Worked example: a failing memory chip is caught while it corrupts only one page, the machine routes around it to a spare, checks the repair against a second copy, and the owner finishes the day's work without noticing anything except a log entry.

History

Machines that repair themselves descend from redundant systems in spacecraft and servers, where spare parts took over when a part failed; the desktop version brings that redundancy home, where no engineer is present to watch it.

Proposed solution

Watch every part continuously, and catch faults while they are small; Isolate a faulty part before its failure spreads to good data; Repair the fault from a spare part, or route around it with a spare path; Check the repair against a second copy before trusting it; Log every fault and every repair, so the owner can see what changed.

Dependencies

Spare parts built into the machine; fault detection that catches small faults early; a second copy of critical data for checking repairs; a logging standard the owner can read.
