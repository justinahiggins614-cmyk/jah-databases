O*NET Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: design-study Design study: this record presents an original Signature design for a profession database in the family of O*NET, describing how the Signature edition would organize, present, and teach the same material. It is a design study, not an established public record.

O*NET is the United States occupational information network: the published record of what American workers do, what they need to know, and what their work demands, occupation by occupation.

A job seeker, a counselor, a teacher, or an employer all ask the same question in different words: what does this job actually involve, what skills does it need, and what does it pay? O*NET answers that question for the whole American labor market, with a single database that describes nearly a thousand occupations in the same terms, so a nurse can be compared with a welder on skills, knowledge, abilities, and work activities. It matters because career decisions are life decisions: a student choosing a trade, a worker retraining after a layoff, and a counselor advising either of them all need facts, not guesses. The database is built from surveys of workers and experts, so its descriptions reflect what people actually do, not what a job title suggests.

History

O*NET replaced the Dictionary of Occupational Titles in the late 1990s, when the United States Employment and Training Administration decided the old book of job descriptions no longer matched a changing economy. The new system was built around a content model that describes every occupation by its tasks, skills, knowledge, abilities, work activities, and work context, and it has been updated ever since, with new occupations added and old ones revised as work changes. The National Center for O*NET Development maintains the database, and the data is released in regular versions so researchers can track how occupations change over time.

Public record

The public record is the O*NET Resource Center at onetcenter.org, free to use, with the O*NET OnLine site for searching occupations, the database downloads in plain files, and the O*NET Web Services for registered users. Bulk data is published free for download, and the site publishes its content model, its data dictionary, and its release notes. The data is a work of the United States government and is published in the public domain, so it may be reused without a license fee. The web services are documented on the site for registered users; no open public endpoint without registration is published, and the record says so plainly.

Signature version

This Signature design builds: The Signature edition adds a teaching layer on top of the same occupational facts: each occupation gets a plain-language explainer of a day in the job, a demo that walks through its tasks, and a ladder that teaches the skills from beginner to expert. The design adds a compare tool that lines up two occupations side by side, a pathfinder that maps the steps from one occupation to a related one, and a skills reader that separates what can be learned quickly from what takes years. Nothing in the design changes a published occupation fact; the Signature layer explains and demonstrates the public record.

Worked example

Look up registered nurse in O*NET and the occupation page returns its tasks, such as assessing patients and administering medication, its skills, such as service orientation and critical thinking, its knowledge areas, such as medicine and psychology, and its work context, such as standing and contact with others. Compare it with a licensed practical nurse and the comparison shows which tasks overlap and which skills differ. The worked example shows why the shared content model matters: two occupations can be compared field by field because both are described in the same terms.

Science proof

Labor-market science gives the project its measures: occupational analysis, job surveys, and the statistics of skill ratings. Workers and experts rate each occupation on scales for importance and level, and the ratings are aggregated and checked for agreement before they are published. The science proof is traceability: a rating carries its scale, its sample, and its method, and a reader can check the data dictionary to see exactly what was asked. Where an occupation is new or changing, the record shows the survey date, so a reader can judge how fresh the facts are. A catalog spot-check works like a quality audit: a fixed-seed sample of entries is drawn from the catalog's categories, the counts per category are compared with the flat expectation, and a chi-square figure measures how far the sample strays from flat. The demo runs that audit twice with the same seed and hashes both results, so a fresh run verifies the arithmetic exactly. The audit proves only that the demo's own arithmetic is deterministic; it does not query the live database and it proves nothing about the live holdings, which must be checked against the published source itself. A small chi-square means the sample came out near flat, which is exactly what a fair seeded draw produces; it says nothing about the live database, which must be checked at its published source.

A chi-square near zero is exactly what a flat seeded draw produces; a large chi-square would mean the sample was lopsided, and either way the figure describes only the demo's own sample, never the live holdings. Working demo program

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'O*NET'
entries = 923
categories = ['Tasks', 'Skills', 'Knowledge', 'Abilities', 'Work activities']
trials = 25
seed = 41
claimed_note = 'Published holdings: 923 occupations described in the same content model; the demo samples 25 entries by content-model section and does not query the live database.'

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    counts = {c: 0 for c in categories}
    for _ in range(trials):
        counts[rng.choice(categories)] += 1
    return counts

expected = trials / len(categories)
counts_a = run_once()
counts_b = run_once()
chi2 = sum((o - expected) ** 2 / expected for o in counts_a.values())
result = {"title": title, "entries": entries, "trials": trials,
          "categories": len(categories), "seed": seed,
          "counts": counts_a, "chi_square": round(chi2, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Catalog: " + title)
print("Entries (published, approximate): %d" % entries)
print("Spot-check sample: %d entries across %d categories (fixed seed %d)" % (trials, len(categories), seed))
print("Category counts: " + json.dumps(counts_a, sort_keys=True))
print("Expected per category: %.2f entries" % expected)
print("Chi-square: %.3f (sum of (observed minus expected) squared, divided by expected)" % chi2)
print("Spot-check note: " + claimed_note)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:
    print("Deterministic: MATCH - both runs produced the same sampled counts.")
else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")
print("Note: this demo simulates a seeded spot-check sample only. It does not query the live database, and a balanced sample is chance, not a verification of the live holdings.")
print("Design study: this record describes a Signature database design, not an established public record.")

A nurse trained in one European country, an employer hiring in another, and a job board matching the two all need the same thing: a shared language for occupations and skills that works across languages and borders. ESCO answers that need with a multilingual classification of occupations, skills, and qualifications, published by the European Commission, so a job in Lisbon and a job in Warsaw can be described in the same terms. It matters because Europe's labor market is a single market in law but many markets in language: without a shared classification, a skill learned in one country is invisible in another. The classification also matters for education, since qualifications can be matched to the skills employers actually ask for.

History

It was designed from the start as a multilingual, openly licensed classification, with occupations, skills, and qualifications linked together, and it has been updated in regular versions since, with new occupations and skills added as work changes. Its history is a European history: the classification grows as the labor market changes, and its translations grow as more languages are added.

import json
import math
import random

title = 'The UK National Careers Service'
entries = 800
categories = ['Job profiles', 'Pay and hours', 'Entry routes', 'Skills', 'Advice']

seed = 43

def run_once():

    counts = {c: 0 for c in categories}
    for _ in range(trials):
        counts[rng.choice(categories)] += 1
    return counts

expected = trials / len(categories)
counts_a = run_once()
counts_b = run_once()
chi2 = sum((o - expected) ** 2 / expected for o in counts_a.values())

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'The German KldB'
entries = 3000
categories = ['Occupational areas', 'Main groups', 'Groups', 'Subgroups', 'Occupations']
trials = 25
seed = 48
claimed_note = 'Published holdings: about 3,000 occupations in the KldB 2010; the demo samples 25 entries by hierarchy level and does not query the live database.'

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    counts = {c: 0 for c in categories}
    for _ in range(trials):
        counts[rng.choice(categories)] += 1
    return counts

expected = trials / len(categories)
counts_a = run_once()
counts_b = run_once()
chi2 = sum((o - expected) ** 2 / expected for o in counts_a.values())
result = {"title": title, "entries": entries, "trials": trials,
          "categories": len(categories), "seed": seed,
          "counts": counts_a, "chi_square": round(chi2, 3)}
digest_a = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
digest_b = hashlib.sha256(json.dumps(result, sort_keys=True).encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()
print("Catalog: " + title)
print("Entries (published, approximate): %d" % entries)
print("Spot-check sample: %d entries across %d categories (fixed seed %d)" % (trials, len(categories), seed))
print("Category counts: " + json.dumps(counts_a, sort_keys=True))
print("Expected per category: %.2f entries" % expected)
print("Chi-square: %.3f (sum of (observed minus expected) squared, divided by expected)" % chi2)
print("Spot-check note: " + claimed_note)
print("SHA-256 run 1: " + digest_a)
print("SHA-256 run 2: " + digest_b)
if digest_a == digest_b:
    print("Deterministic: MATCH - both runs produced the same sampled counts.")
else:
    print("Deterministic: MISMATCH - the two runs disagreed.")
print("Note: this demo simulates a seeded spot-check sample only. It does not query the live database, and a balanced sample is chance, not a verification of the live holdings.")

import hashlib
import json
import math
import random

title = 'The Professional Licensing Registers'
entries = 200
categories = ['Medical', 'Legal', 'Engineering', 'Teaching', 'Other professions']
trials = 25
seed = 58
claimed_note = 'Published holdings: hundreds of regulated professions, each with its own public register; the demo samples 25 entries by profession group and does not query the live database.'

def run_once():
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    counts = {c: 0 for c in categories}
    for _ in range(trials):
        counts[rng.choice(categories)] += 1
    return counts

expected = trials / len(categories)
counts_a = run_once()
counts_b = run_once()
chi2 = sum((o - expected) ** 2 / expected for o in counts_a.values())
