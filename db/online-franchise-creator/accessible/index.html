  1. From One Online Shop to a Franchise Model

    Record 1 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. A franchise model starts with one online business that already works: steady sales, a clear product, and a way of working that can be written down and taught. The creator's first job is to prove the single shop works before asking anyone else to copy it.

    Math proof: The model is proven with one location first: if the original shop earns 50,000 a month in sales and keeps 10,000 after costs, its margin is 10,000 divided by 50,000, which is 0.20, or 20 percent. A second location copying the same model should earn near the same margin, and if it earns far less, the model is not yet ready to copy.

    Science proof: A single working shop is the experiment that proves the model: one shop, measured honestly, shows whether the idea earns money before it is repeated. Copying an unproven shop multiplies its problems instead of its profits, so the proof must come first.

  2. Choosing What Can Be Franchised

    Record 2 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Not every online business can be franchised: the product, the service, and the daily routine must all be teachable to a stranger in a different place. If a step depends on one person's talent or one local connection, it cannot be copied, and it does not belong in a franchise.

    Math proof: Teachability is counted in steps: if a routine has 12 steps and 11 can be written down plainly, it is 11 divided by 12, which is about 92 percent teachable. A routine below about 80 percent teachable needs simplifying before it can be franchised.

    Science proof: People copy what they can see and follow: a written step, a picture, and a short practice run teach faster than a long lecture. A business whose work can be shown step by step can be taught to a stranger, and a business whose work lives only in one person's head cannot.

  3. The Franchise Fee: A Worked Example

    Record 3 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. The initial franchise fee is the one-time payment a new franchisee pays to join: it covers the right to use the brand, the training, the manual, and help with the launch. A fair fee is built from real costs, not guessed: add up what it truly costs to set one new location up, then set the fee to cover it.

    Math proof: The fee is built from costs: training 8,000, plus the manual and brand pack 5,000, plus launch support 12,000, plus legal and admin 15,000, which is 8,000 plus 5,000 plus 12,000 plus 15,000, equal to 40,000. Ten new locations at a 40,000 fee bring 10 times 40,000, which is 400,000 in upfront fees.

    Science proof: A fee tied to real costs is fair to both sides: the franchisor recovers what launching a location truly costs, and the franchisee can see what the money pays for. A fee set by guessing breeds mistrust, because the buyer cannot tell what they are paying for.

  4. Royalties: A Worked Example

    Record 4 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. A royalty is the ongoing payment a franchisee makes, usually a small percentage of sales, in exchange for the brand, the system, and continuing support. A 6 percent royalty on 50,000 of monthly sales is 3,000 a month, and the same percentage keeps the payment fair when sales rise or fall.

    Math proof: The royalty is a percentage of sales: 0.06 times 50,000 is 3,000 per month, and 3,000 times 12 is 36,000 per year from one location. With 10 locations at the same sales, the yearly royalty is 10 times 36,000, which is 360,000. If sales double to 100,000, the royalty doubles to 6,000 a month, so both sides share the gain.

    Science proof: A percentage royalty aligns both sides: when a location sells more, the franchisor earns more, and when it sells less, the payment falls, so the franchisor has a reason to keep helping locations sell. A flat monthly fee does not do this, because it stays the same whether the location thrives or struggles.

  5. Designing a Franchise Territory

    Record 5 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. A territory is the area one franchisee serves, drawn so each location has enough customers without stealing sales from its neighbours. A good territory is drawn from real numbers: how many people live there, how far they will travel, and how much they spend.

    Math proof: A territory is sized by people and spending: if an area has 40,000 people and each spends 25 a year on this kind of product, the area's spending is 40,000 times 25, which is 1,000,000 a year. If one location needs 400,000 a year in sales to thrive, the area can support 1,000,000 divided by 400,000, which is 2.5, so two locations fit and a third would starve.

    Science proof: People shop close to home: a shop that is a short trip away gets visited often, and one that is far away gets visited rarely. Drawing territories around how far customers will actually travel keeps each location busy without crowding its neighbours.

  6. The Operations Manual

    Record 6 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. The operations manual is the franchise in writing: every routine, every standard, and every answer to a question a new owner will ask, in plain steps. A good manual is tested by a stranger following it alone: if they can open and run the location from the manual, it is ready.

    Math proof: A manual is measured by coverage: if the business has 20 daily routines and the manual covers 19 of them in plain steps, it covers 19 divided by 20, which is 95 percent. The missing routine is the one that will go wrong first, so coverage must reach every routine that matters.

    Science proof: Written steps beat memory: a new owner who can look a step up will do it the same way every time, while one who must remember it will drift. A manual turns the founder's habits into instructions anyone can follow, and that is what makes a franchise repeatable.

  7. The Franchise Training Program

    Record 7 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. The training program teaches a new franchisee everything the manual describes, in person and by practice, before their location opens. Training runs in stages: the business itself, then the daily routines, then a supervised opening week, so nothing is learned for the first time in front of a customer.

    Math proof: Training is scheduled in hours: 16 hours on the business, plus 24 hours on daily routines, plus 40 hours of supervised practice, which is 16 plus 24 plus 40, equal to 80 hours. Over a two-week program of 40 hours a week, that is exactly 80 hours, so the plan fits the time allowed.

    Science proof: People learn by doing: a trainee who has practised a routine ten times will do it smoothly on opening day, while one who only read about it will hesitate. Supervised practice turns reading into habit, and habit is what carries a new owner through a busy first week.

  8. Brand Standards That Travel

    Record 8 of 24. simple. Feasibility: proven. Brand standards are the promises every location keeps the same: the name, the look, the quality, the prices, and the way customers are treated. A customer should get the same experience at every location, whether it is the first shop or the fiftieth.

    Math proof: Standards are checked as a score: if a location is checked on 25 standards and meets 24, its score is 24 divided by 25, which is 96 percent. A network that requires 90 percent or better passes that location, and one below 90 percent gets help until it passes.

    Science proof: Customers trust what is the same every time: the same quality, the same welcome, and the same price build a habit of returning. One location that breaks the standard breaks the trust for all of them, so standards are checked everywhere, not just at the first shop.

  9. The Franchise Support System

    Record 9 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Support is the help a franchisor keeps giving after the opening: a helpline, regular visits, new products, shared advertising, and answers when something goes wrong. A franchisee who can get help quickly stays open, sells more, and pays more royalty, so support pays for itself.

    Math proof: Support is priced per location: if the support team costs 120,000 a year and serves 10 locations, each location costs 120,000 divided by 10, which is 12,000 a year to support. If each location pays 36,000 a year in royalty, support costs one third of the royalty and leaves 24,000 per location for everything else.

    Science proof: Help given early prevents failure: a small problem fixed in a phone call stays small, while the same problem left alone can close a location. Regular visits and a ready helpline catch problems while they are still cheap to fix.

  12. Quality Control Across Every Location

    Record 12 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. Quality control is the regular checking that every location meets the brand standards: mystery visits, customer feedback, sales checks, and a plain report each location can act on. Checks are fair when they are the same everywhere, announced in advance, and followed by help, not only punishment.

    Math proof: Control is a pass rate: if 10 locations are checked and 9 pass at 90 percent or better, the pass rate is 9 divided by 10, which is 90 percent. The one that failed gets a written list of fixes and a recheck in 30 days, so the check leads to improvement instead of only a score.

    Science proof: Checking keeps the copy faithful: without checks, each location drifts a little, and after a year no two locations work the same way. Regular checks catch drift early, while the fix is still small and cheap.

  13. The Franchise Launch Plan

    Record 13 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. A launch plan is the dated list of steps from signing to opening day: the site, the fit-out, the stock, the staff, the training, and the first advertising. Each step has a date and an owner, so a delay in one step is seen early and the opening date holds.

    Math proof: A launch is planned in weeks: site 2 weeks, plus fit-out 4 weeks, plus stock and staff 2 weeks, plus training 2 weeks, which is 2 plus 4 plus 2 plus 2, equal to 10 weeks from signing to opening. If any step slips by a week, the opening slips by a week, so the plan is watched every week.

    Science proof: A planned opening beats a rushed one: a location that opens with trained staff, full stock, and its advertising ready earns from day one, while one that opens half-ready spends its first month fixing problems in front of its first customers.

  14. Recruiting the First Franchisees

    Record 14 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. The first franchisees are the hardest to recruit, because there is no network yet to point at: the creator must show the working shop, the honest numbers, and the manual, and choose people with the money, the time, and the will to follow the system. The first three franchisees set the culture for all who follow.

    Math proof: Recruiting is a funnel: if 100 people enquire, 20 are serious enough to meet, 5 can afford the fee and setup, and 3 sign, the signing rate is 3 divided by 100, which is 3 percent. To sign 10 franchisees at that rate needs about 10 divided by 0.03, which is about 334 enquiries.

    Science proof: The first owners teach the franchisor too: their questions show which parts of the manual are unclear, and their struggles show where training is thin. Choosing the first franchisees carefully, and listening to them, makes the system better for everyone who follows.

  15. Pricing the Franchise Package

    Record 15 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. The franchise package price is the full cost of joining: the initial fee, the setup, the stock, and the working money for the first months, added up honestly. A buyer who knows the whole price can plan, while one who learns of hidden costs later feels cheated.

    Math proof: The package is a sum: fee 40,000, plus setup 100,000, plus opening stock 25,000, plus working money 35,000, which is 40,000 plus 100,000 plus 25,000 plus 35,000, equal to 200,000. A buyer needs 200,000 in total, not only the 40,000 fee, so the whole figure is shown, never the fee alone.

    Science proof: Honest pricing builds a network that lasts: buyers who can afford the whole package open strong and stay open, while buyers who could only afford the fee run out of money before the location finds its feet. The true price protects both sides from a bad start.

  16. The Franchise Marketing Fund

    Record 16 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. The marketing fund is the shared advertising pot: every location pays in a small percentage of sales, and the franchisor spends it on advertising that helps all locations. The fund is reported openly, so every franchisee can see what was collected and what it bought.

    Math proof: The fund is a percentage of network sales: with 10 locations each selling 50,000 a month, network sales are 10 times 50,000, which is 500,000 a month. A 2 percent fund collects 0.02 times 500,000, which is 10,000 a month, or 120,000 a year for shared advertising.

    Science proof: Shared advertising reaches further than any single location could afford alone: one strong campaign lifts every location at once, and a fund that is reported openly keeps every franchisee willing to pay into it. A fund spent in secret breeds suspicion, even when it is spent well.

  17. Supply Chains for Many Locations

    Record 17 of 24. working. Feasibility: proven. A supply chain for a franchise buys once for every location: one supplier, one price, one quality standard, and deliveries that arrive on time at each shop. Bulk buying lowers the price, and one standard keeps the product the same everywhere.

    Math proof: Bulk buying is a discount on volume: if one location buys 1,000 units at 10 each, it pays 10,000, but if 10 locations buy together, the price falls to 9 each, so 10,000 units cost 90,000 instead of 100,000, saving 10,000. The saving is 10,000 divided by 100,000, which is 10 percent.

    Science proof: One supplier, checked once, serves all: each location orders the same goods, from the same source, to the same standard, so a customer gets the same product at every shop. Many small suppliers would drift apart in price and quality, and the sameness customers trust would be lost.

  18. Technology Every Location Shares

    Record 18 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. Shared technology is the software every location uses: the shop system, the ordering, the stock, the sales reports, and the training, all in one place. One system means one way of working, one set of numbers the franchisor can trust, and one place to fix a problem.

    Math proof: Shared technology is priced per location per month: if the system costs 5,000 a month for the whole network and serves 10 locations, each location pays 5,000 divided by 10, which is 500 a month. If the network grows to 25 locations, each pays 5,000 divided by 25, which is 200 a month, so growth makes the system cheaper for everyone.

    Science proof: One system keeps the numbers honest: when every location reports sales the same way, the franchisor can compare them fairly, spot a struggling location early, and share what the best locations do differently. Ten different systems would give ten different answers to the same question.

  19. Measuring a Franchisee's Performance

    Record 19 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. Performance is measured with the same few numbers everywhere: sales, costs, royalty paid, customer scores, and standards checks, reported monthly and compared fairly. A franchisee who can see their own numbers against the network average knows exactly where to improve.

    Math proof: Performance is a comparison: if the network average monthly sales are 50,000 and one location sells 60,000, it is 10,000 above average, which is 10,000 divided by 50,000, or 20 percent above. If another sells 40,000, it is 20 percent below, and the franchisor's job is to find out why and help.

    Science proof: Fair numbers make fair judgements: a location is judged against the same measures as every other location, with its territory and age taken into account, so a new location in a small town is not blamed for selling less than an old one in a big city. Measured fairly, the numbers guide help instead of blame.

  20. Renewals, Transfers, and Exits

    Record 20 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. A franchise ends in one of three ways: renewal for another term, transfer to a new owner, or an agreed exit, and each path is written in the agreement before anyone signs. A clean exit keeps the brand intact: the location closes or changes hands without a public dispute.

    Math proof: An exit is priced by the numbers: if a location earns 120,000 a year in profit and similar locations sell for 3 times yearly profit, its value is 3 times 120,000, which is 360,000. A transfer fee of 5 percent of that value is 0.05 times 360,000, which is 18,000.

    Science proof: Planned endings protect the network: a franchisee who can sell their location for a fair price leaves happy and speaks well of the brand, while one trapped with no exit grows bitter and neglects the shop. Renewals, transfers, and exits are planned at the start, not improvised at the end.

  21. Growing From Ten to One Hundred Locations

    Record 21 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. Growing from ten locations to one hundred is a change of kind, not just of size: the support team, the supply chain, the training, and the technology must all grow before the locations do. Growth is staged in waves, with each wave checked before the next begins.

    Math proof: Growth is staged by waves: from 10 locations, waves of 10, then 20, then 30, then 30 add up to 10 plus 10 plus 20 plus 30 plus 30, which is 100. If each wave takes 6 months, the four waves take 24 months, so the network reaches 100 locations in two years of steady, checked growth.

    Science proof: Staged growth survives its own success: each wave tests whether support, supply, and training kept up, and the next wave starts only when they did. Growing all at once overwhelms the support team, and locations opened without support fail in sight of the next wave.

  22. The Franchise Growth Budget

    Record 22 of 24. advanced. Feasibility: proven. The growth budget is the money set aside to reach the next wave of locations: recruiting, training, support staff, and advertising, planned a year ahead. A budget that is written down and checked monthly keeps growth funded, while growth paid for from daily takings stalls at the first slow month.

    Math proof: The budget is a yearly sum: recruiting 60,000, plus training 80,000, plus extra support staff 120,000, plus launch advertising 40,000, which is 60,000 plus 80,000 plus 120,000 plus 40,000, equal to 300,000 a year. With 10 locations each paying 36,000 a year in royalty, royalty income is 360,000, so the budget of 300,000 fits inside it with 60,000 to spare.

    Science proof: Budgeting growth from royalty income keeps the franchisor honest: if the royalty cannot fund the next wave, the network is not yet earning its growth, and the wave should wait. Borrowing to grow faster risks the whole network on a promise that sales may not keep.
