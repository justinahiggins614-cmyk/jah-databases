What Family History Is Rank 1 · simple

Family history is the study of a family's past through records that can be checked: registers, censuses, wills, property papers, service files, and published histories. It is not the collection of stories alone, and it is not the copying of other people's trees. A claim belongs in a family history only when a record supports it, and the record is cited so another researcher can find the same entry and test the same claim. This pair teaches the method in order, from the first home sources to the final check, and every record in it names its source and states what it does not prove.

Starting With What You Know Rank 2 · simple

The soundest start is what is already known and already in the home: names, dates, and places that can be written down before any archive is visited. A beginner writes a short pedigree from memory, marks every entry that is remembered rather than documented, and then works outward one generation at a time. Guesses are labeled as guesses and are never entered as facts, because a guess entered early becomes an error copied late. The first archive visit then has a purpose: to confirm or correct one marked entry, not to gather everything at once.

Interviewing Older Relatives Rank 3 · simple

Interviewing older relatives is a method for gathering memories, not a method for publishing personal data. The interviewer asks open questions, records answers with permission, and writes down exactly what was said, including uncertainty and disagreement between accounts. Memories are then checked against registers and censuses, because memory compresses dates and merges people. No detail about a living person gathered in an interview is published in this database, and the interview record itself stays a private working note unless the person interviewed has given clear permission for a specific use.

Home Sources: Letters, Photographs, and Bibles Rank 4 · simple

Home sources are the papers already in a house: letters, photographs, family Bibles, certificates, school reports, and service papers. Each one is dated, described, and copied before it is handled further, because originals fade and are lost. A photograph with no names on the back is a clue, not an identification, and a Bible entry written years after an event is a later copy, not a same-day record. Home sources are strongest when they are used together: a letter giving a place, a photograph giving a face, and a certificate giving a date, each checking the others.

Building a Family Tree Chart Rank 5 · simple

A family tree chart is a diagram of relationships, not a proof of them. The chart shows parents, children, and spouses, with dates and places beside each name, and every line on it should trace to a cited record. Standard practice draws one generation at a time and leaves a gap visible where no record has been found, rather than filling the gap with a likely-looking name. A chart that shows its gaps honestly is more useful than a complete-looking chart built on guesses, because the next researcher can see exactly where work remains.

Keeping a Research Log Rank 6 · simple

A research log is the diary of a search: what was looked for, where, on what date, and what was found or not found. Negative results are logged too, because a register searched without result is knowledge: it tells the next searcher not to repeat that search and to try a different parish, spelling, or date window. Logs also record variant spellings tried, because a name searched under one spelling only is a search half done. Without a log, the same ground is searched twice and the same error is made twice.

Reading Old Handwriting Rank 7 · compounded

Old handwriting changes by period and by writer, and reading it is a skill learned from dated examples, not from guessing words. The researcher compares letter forms in the same document, reads the whole entry before deciding a word, and checks names against known local spellings. Abbreviations were standard in their time: a shortened Christian name, a Latin term in a parish book, or a clerk's contracted surname. A word that cannot be read is transcribed as unread, with the doubt marked, rather than forced into a familiar name that then enters the tree as fact.

Parish Registers and Church Books Rank 8 · compounded

Parish registers and church books are the oldest systematic family records in many countries: baptisms, marriages, and burials entered by clergy, sometimes for centuries before civil registration began. In England, parish registration was ordered in 1538, which makes English parish books among the longest continuous runs. Registers vary in survival and in detail: some give only names and dates, others give parents, occupations, and places. A baptism is not a birth date, a burial is not a death date, and the gap between event and entry is part of the evidence, not a detail to ignore.

Civil Registration Systems Rank 9 · compounded

Civil registration systems are state-run birth, marriage, and death registers, begun at different dates in different countries: England and Wales in 1837, Scotland in 1855, Ireland in 1864, and France in 1792. A civil certificate is usually fuller than a parish entry, giving parents, occupations, and informants, but it is still a copy of an informant's statement, not a direct observation. Registration law changed over time, so the same event may be recorded differently in different decades, and late registrations exist. The system date matters: no civil certificate can exist before its system began.

Census Returns as Family Evidence Rank 10 · compounded

Census returns are household snapshots taken on one night, and they are among the most useful family records because they place people together in a house, with ages, birthplaces, and occupations. A census age is often approximate, a birthplace may be a county rather than a parish, and a person may be absent from home on census night. Used across decades, censuses build a timeline: the same household appearing, changing, and dispersing. Used alone, a single census entry proves presence on one night only, and every other claim drawn from it needs a second record.

Wills, Probate, and Property Records Rank 11 · compounded

Wills, probate papers, and property records show relationships through inheritance, land, and debt. A will names heirs, executors, and sometimes relationships in words, which makes it strong evidence for family structure, but a will may omit children, favor one heir, or predate later children. Probate inventories list goods and debts, giving a picture of a household's standing. Property deeds trace land through owners, and a deed's witnesses and neighbors are often relatives. These records are strongest for people who held property, and weakest for people who left nothing to probate, which is itself a fact about standing.

Military and Service Records Rank 12 · complex

Military and service records cover soldiers, sailors, and others in state service, and they survive unevenly by country and by war. Attestation papers give age, birthplace, occupation, and next of kin, while service papers trace postings, wounds, and discharge. Pension files may run for decades after service and contain family detail found nowhere else. These records concern people who are long deceased in the historical files used here, and they are used as historical evidence about service and family, never as a route to any living person's information. Destroyed or incomplete files are labeled as gaps.

Migration Trails and Passenger Lists Rank 13 · complex

Migration trails are built from passenger lists, border crossings, naturalization papers, and settlement records, read as a sequence rather than as single arrivals. A passenger list gives a ship, a date, and a stated origin, but origins were recorded by clerks in the port's language, and ages were often approximate. Chain migration means later arrivals followed earlier ones to the same town or district, so a settlement cluster is evidence, not coincidence. The trail is proven only when departure, arrival, and settlement records agree on the same person by more than name alone, with age, origin, and companions matching.

DNA Evidence in Family History Rank 14 · complex

DNA evidence in family history is used here only within strict boundaries: published ancient-DNA research and aggregated population data, as covered in the ancient-dna and haplogroup-webs pairs. A DNA result never replaces a paper record, and a shared segment or a shared haplogroup is supporting evidence, never a proven pedigree by itself. This record states the boundary plainly so that no reader mistakes this database for a DNA testing service: it holds no private test results, no individual modern genomes, and no re-identification of anyone, and it makes no medical claim of any kind.

Checking, Citing, and Correcting a Tree Rank 15 · complex

Checking, citing, and correcting a tree is the final discipline: every claim carries a citation, every citation can be re-found, and every correction is recorded with its reason. A citation gives the archive, the register, the entry, and the date consulted, in enough detail that another researcher reaches the same page. When a new record contradicts an old entry, the old entry is corrected openly, with the old reading preserved as a note, because silent correction hides the error's history. A tree that shows its corrections is trustworthy; a tree that hides them is not.

Sources Index and Open Gaps Rank 16 · complex

This closing record indexes the sources used across the family-history pair: parish and civil registration histories, census method guides, probate and property guides, service-record guides, and migration-record guides, all as published public material. Open gaps are stated plainly: parish survival varies by place and is not complete anywhere; civil systems begin at different dates, so no single system covers all periods; home sources survive only where families kept them; and DNA evidence in this package is limited to published ancient research and aggregates. Future waves should fill exactly these gaps from citable sources and say which source filled each one.
