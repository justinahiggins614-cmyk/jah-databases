How to read it: the teaching ladder

The science of it

An encyclopedia is an information-retrieval system, and its science is the science of finding. A reader arrives with a question and a word; the index turns the word into a location; the article at that location answers the question in sentences. The JAH Wiki works the same way, with one difference that matters: its articles are generated over the network's own records, so the thing being explained and the explanation live in the same system and can be checked against each other. The four-shard split is a retrieval decision, not a decoration: a lookup that knows it wants a word never has to search the spec shard, and a lookup that wants a dossier never has to search the word shard. Sharding by subject keeps each lookup small, and small lookups stay fast no matter how large the total grows.

How to read it: the teaching ladder

Every database in the network teaches the same way, simple first and complex last, and this one is no exception. The first step is the simplest: open one record and read it whole — its title, its description, and its fields — until the shape of a specification is familiar. The second step is to compare two records side by side and notice what changes between them and what stays the same, because the things that stay the same are the structure and the things that change are the content. The third step is to use the index: look a record up by its ID, follow the index row to its chunk file, and see how the database finds one record among tens of thousands without scanning them all. The fourth step is to ask the file AI a question about the database and check its answer against the records themselves, so that the habit of verification is learned alongside the habit of asking. The last step is the generator: ask for a new scale of the record you are reading, watch what the generator makes, and judge it against the original. A reader who climbs that ladder has not merely read about the database; they have learned to use it.

The science of it

A specification is applied science written down before the thing exists: it states what the thing is, what it is made of, how it is manufactured, and how it behaves, in enough detail that a skilled reader could build it. The science in a spec is therefore the science of its subject — the optics in a floodlight spec, the electronics in an ECG patch spec, the networking in an API gateway spec — plus the engineering discipline of writing it so that nothing essential is left to guesswork. The catalog's own science is archival: deterministic IDs, frozen shards, and a state file that records the next free ID, so that no two specs ever share an ID and no spec is ever silently overwritten.

What the file AI does

Every record in this essay's database has its own file AI, and the database as a whole has one too: JAH Signature Essays Librarian. The file AI answers questions about its record from the record's own data, never from outside it — it can explain the record, compare it with its neighbours, and show the working behind its demo and its program. The file AI also runs the boundless generator for its record. The generator works in three scales. Scale A1 makes one thing: a single new variation on the record you are reading, the smallest step beyond it. Scale A2 makes the standard set: the record's natural family, the variations a reader would most often need. Scale A3 makes the wide set: the full sweep of variations the generator can produce for this kind of record. Generator ceiling: up to 1,000,000,000 scales per file, generated on demand by the file AI — not stored files. The generator is throttled to one hundred scales per call, so that it never overloads its host, and it hard-stops at one billion scales per file. How to read it: the teaching ladder

Every database in the network teaches the same way, simple first and complex last, and this one is no exception. The first step is the simplest: open one record and read it whole — its title, its description, and its fields — until the shape of a best-version tracker record is familiar. The second step is to compare two records side by side and notice what changes between them and what stays the same, because the things that stay the same are the structure and the things that change are the content. The third step is to use the index: look a record up by its ID, follow the index row to its chunk file, and see how the database finds one record among tens of thousands without scanning them all. The fourth step is to ask the file AI a question about the database and check its answer against the records themselves, so that the habit of verification is learned alongside the habit of asking. The last step is the generator: ask for a new scale of the record you are reading, watch what the generator makes, and judge it against the original. A reader who climbs that ladder has not merely read about the database; they have learned to use it.

The science of it

Version judgment is measurement science: a version is scored against stated criteria — completeness, correctness, freshness, and usability — and the best version is the one that scores highest. The discipline is in stating the criteria before judging, so that the verdict can be checked instead of merely believed. A tracker that judged without criteria would be an opinion column; a tracker that states its criteria is an instrument. The database's verdicts are useful exactly to the extent that the criteria are public and the scoring is repeatable, and the record's job is to carry both the verdict and the reasons for it.

What the file AI does

Every record in this essay's database has its own file AI, and the database as a whole has one too: JAH Signature Essays Librarian. The file AI answers questions about its record from the record's own data, never from outside it — it can explain the record, compare it with its neighbours, and show the working behind its demo and its program. The file AI also runs the boundless generator for its record. The generator works in three scales. Scale A1 makes one thing: a single new variation on the record you are reading, the smallest step beyond it. Scale A2 makes the standard set: the record's natural family, the variations a reader would most often need. Scale A3 makes the wide set: the full sweep of variations the generator can produce for this kind of record. Generator ceiling: up to 1,000,000,000 scales per file, generated on demand by the file AI — not stored files. The generator is throttled to one hundred scales per call, so that it never overloads its host, and it hard-stops at one billion scales per file. Generated content is labeled simulated for weeding, and the generator's outputs are verified the same way the stored records are: by re-execution, by duplicate checks, and by comparison against the original.
