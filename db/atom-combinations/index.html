Hydrogen and Hydrogen - the H2 Molecule Rank 1 · simple · Verdict: stable

Evidence: established The hydrogen molecule and its bond energy are established textbook physics, measured and reproduced for a century.

Two hydrogen atoms share their single electrons and form H2, the simplest stable molecule in nature.

A single hydrogen atom carries one electron and one proton, and on its own it is a reactive radical that seeks a partner. When two hydrogen atoms meet, each contributes its electron to a shared pair, and the two electrons settle into a molecular orbital that holds both nuclei together. The bond that results is strong: breaking H2 apart takes 436 kilojoules per mole, about 176 times the thermal energy available at room temperature. That margin is the whole story of the verdict. At ordinary temperatures the molecule does not fall apart on its own, it does not decay, and it survives indefinitely in a closed container.

A sealed flask of hydrogen gas at room temperature sits on a laboratory shelf for years and stays hydrogen gas. The molecules collide with the walls and with each other billions of times a second, yet the thermal kicks they exchange are about 2.5 kilojoules per mole, far below the 436 needed to break a bond. Only a spark, a hot filament, or ultraviolet light supplies enough energy to split H2 into atoms.

History

Hydrogen gas was recognised as a distinct substance in the eighteenth century, and the H2 molecule became the first test case of quantum chemistry: Heitler and London explained its bond in 1927, the first time quantum mechanics accounted for a chemical bond. It remains the benchmark every bonding theory must pass.

Method

Bring two hydrogen atoms close enough for their electron clouds to overlap.; Let the two electrons pair into a shared molecular orbital.; Allow the nuclei to settle at the equilibrium separation of 74 picometres.; Carry away the released bond energy so the molecule stays bound.; Confirm the bond by its dissociation energy of 436 kilojoules per mole..

Hydrogen and Oxygen - Water, H2O Rank 2 · simple · Verdict: stable

Evidence: established Water and its bond energy are established textbook physics, measured and reproduced for centuries.

Two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom form water, the most familiar stable molecule on Earth.

Water forms when oxygen shares electrons with two hydrogen atoms, giving each hydrogen a share in a stable pair and giving oxygen a full outer shell. Each O-H bond takes 463 kilojoules per mole to break, about 187 times the thermal energy at room temperature, so a water molecule does not fall apart on its own. The bent shape of the molecule, with the two hydrogens at about 104.5 degrees, makes it polar, and that polarity lets water molecules link to each other through hydrogen bonds. The result is a liquid that is stable across the whole range of temperatures where life exists.

A glass of water left on a table stays water. Individual molecules evaporate from the surface, but the molecules themselves do not split into hydrogen and oxygen: the thermal energy available is about 2.5 kilojoules per mole against 463 needed to break an O-H bond. Only electrolysis, or temperatures above about 2000 degrees, pull water apart.

History

Cavendish showed in the 1780s that burning hydrogen in air makes water, and Lavoisier named the gas hydrogen, the water-former. The bent structure of H2O was settled by spectroscopy in the twentieth century, and the O-H bond energy of 463 kJ/mol is a standard tabulated value.

Sodium and Chlorine - Table Salt, NaCl Rank 3 · simple · Verdict: stable

Evidence: established Sodium chloride and its lattice energy are established textbook physics, measured and reproduced for a century.

A sodium atom gives up its outer electron to a chlorine atom, and the two ions lock into the salt crystal.

Sodium holds its single outer electron loosely, and chlorine holds an almost-full outer shell that wants one more electron. When the two meet, the electron moves from sodium to chlorine, leaving a positive sodium ion and a negative chloride ion. Opposite charges attract, and the ions pack into a cubic crystal in which every sodium is surrounded by chlorides and every chloride by sodiums. Pulling the crystal apart takes 787 kilojoules per mole of lattice energy, about 317 times the thermal energy at room temperature, so salt does not fall apart on its own. Dissolved in water the ions separate, but that is solvation, not collapse: the ions themselves survive intact.

A salt shaker on a kitchen table holds the same crystals for years. The grains do not crumble into sodium metal and chlorine gas, because the lattice energy is hundreds of times the thermal energy. Drop the salt in water and it dissolves, but evaporate the water and the same crystals come back: the ions were never destroyed.

History

Salt has been harvested and eaten since prehistory. The ionic picture was worked out in the early twentieth century from X-ray crystal structures, and the lattice energy of 787 kJ/mol follows from the Born-Haber cycle, a standard textbook calculation.

Method

Bring a sodium atom and a chlorine atom together.; Transfer the outer electron from sodium to chlorine.; Let the resulting ions attract and settle into a cubic lattice.; Carry away the released lattice energy so the crystal stays bound.; Confirm the bond by the lattice energy of 787 kilojoules per mole..
