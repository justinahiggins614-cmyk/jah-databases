Rage: Definition Rank 1 · simple

Evidence: established Established: this facet is documented in the published emotion-science literature (Ekman, Plutchik, Russell, and the appraisal tradition), and the record reports what that literature states.

Rage is the intense form of anger: an overwhelming fury that rises when a wrongdoing or offence is felt as grave, and that floods the body with heat and alarm.

What exactly is rage, and where does it sit among the anger emotions? The question matters because rage is easily confused with its neighbours: it resembles anger, but it is stronger and more overwhelming; it resembles hatred, but it is a state rather than a lasting stance; it resembles indignation, but it needs no moral judgment. The literature treats rage as the intense form of anger on Plutchik's wheel, an emotion that follows the appraisal of a wrongdoing or offence, and that carries the body into heat, alarm, and confrontation. This record sets out the working definition used across the pair, so that every later record, triggers, expression, measurement, can be read against the same sense of the word.

History

The word rage comes from the Old French rage, from the Latin rabies, madness or frenzy, the same root as rabid. It entered Middle English in the thirteenth century, first meaning madness or violent passion, and it kept that sense of overwhelming passion into the modern period. Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics and Rhetoric gave anger its classic philosophical account, and Seneca's On Anger, written around 45 CE, gave rage its most discussed ancient account: anger, Seneca argued, is a short madness, and rage is anger at its height. Darwin took the term into the science of expression in 1872, and Plutchik placed rage as the intense form of anger on his wheel of 1980.

This facet in detail

A person is insulted in front of a crowd, and the feeling reported is rage: the offence is felt as grave, the heat rises, the voice lifts, and the body moves toward confrontation, or turns away in a cold silence. The person does not deliberate; the appraisal is made at once, and the body answers with heat and alarm. The same shape appears in a parent who sees a child wronged, a worker who is blamed for another's fault, and a driver who is cut off at speed: a wrongdoing felt as grave, and a body flooded with heat.

What the record shows

The literature reports that rage is reliably described as an intense, unpleasant, high-arousal emotion, distinct from anger by its intensity and by the sense of being overwhelmed, and distinct from hatred by its being a state rather than a lasting stance. The Geneva Emotion Wheel lists anger as one of its twenty emotion families, with rage named as the intense form. Studies of rage report that it is the anger emotion most often described with the words overwhelming and flooding, and that its reported bodily signs are the strongest of the anger family.

Cautions and limits

The record's cautions are plain. Rage has no single laboratory measure, and most of its evidence is self-report and observation: people describe what they felt, and observers describe what they saw, and neither is a measurement of the feeling itself. The line between anger and rage is drawn by intensity, and the literature does not agree on where the line falls: some writers treat rage as anger at its highest, others as a separate state. The record therefore presents rage as the intense form of anger, following Plutchik, and it states the disagreement beside the definition. Every claim is stated as reported, with its source.

Rage: Triggers Rank 3 · simple

Evidence: established Established: this facet is documented in the published emotion-science literature (Ekman, Plutchik, Russell, and the appraisal tradition), and the record reports what that literature states.

The situations people report as setting off rage: insult, injustice, betrayal, frustration, threat to loved ones, and being blocked, described as reported situations only.

What sets rage off? The literature groups triggers by what is offended rather than by where it happens: the person, the loved ones, and the sense of fairness are the three broad kinds, and each kind has its own shape.
