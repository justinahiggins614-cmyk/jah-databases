Definition: Tooth decay is the destruction of the tooth by acid. The bacteria in the plaque feed on sugar, and they make acid, and the acid dissolves the enamel. At first the damage is a white spot, and then it is a hole, the cavity. If the cavity is not treated, it reaches the dentine, and then the pulp, and the tooth aches, and it can be lost. Decay is the most common disease of the tooth, and it is preventable.

History: Decay was understood late. For centuries, the tooth-worm was blamed: the idea that a worm bored into the tooth. In 1890, Willoughby Miller published the chemico-parasitic theory: the bacteria in the mouth make acid from sugar, and the acid dissolves the tooth. In the 20th century, the science confirmed it: the plaque, the sugar, and the acid are the three causes, and the fluoride is the defence. In the 1940s, the towns with natural fluoride in the water had far less decay, and the prevention was found. Today decay is understood as a disease of the plaque, the sugar, and the time: the acid attacks after every meal, and the fluoride repairs the damage between meals.
