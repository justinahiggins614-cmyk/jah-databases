What Dentistry Is

Dentistry is the branch of medicine concerned with the teeth, the gums, and the mouth. The dentist examines the mouth, prevents disease, and treats it when it appears: the filling, the extraction, the crown, and the cleaning. Dentistry is both the prevention and the treatment: the brushing and the check-up prevent the disease, and the treatment repairs the damage. The mouth is the gateway to the body, and the health of the teeth affects the health of the whole person.

An adult has 32 teeth: 8 incisors, 4 canines, 8 premolars, and 12 molars, which add to 32. A child has 20 teeth: 8 incisors, 4 canines, and 8 molars, which add to 20. The adult has 12 more teeth than the child, because the premolars and the third molars arrive later. A check-up every 6 months means 2 visits a year, and 20 visits in 10 years. Brushing twice a day for 2 minutes is 4 minutes a day, and 1,460 minutes a year, which is about 24 hours of brushing a year.

Teeth and Their Structure

A tooth has a crown, which is the part above the gum, and a root, which is the part below it, held in the jaw by the ligament. The crown is covered in enamel, the hardest substance in the body, and beneath it is the dentine, which is softer. Inside is the pulp, with the nerve and the blood vessels, which keep the tooth alive. The tooth is a living structure: it feels, it grows, and it can heal, in part, by itself.

An adult has 32 teeth: 8 incisors for cutting, 4 canines for tearing, 8 premolars for crushing, and 12 molars for grinding, which add to 32. The incisors have 1 root each, the premolars have 1 or 2, and the upper molars have 3: the roots anchor the tooth in the jaw. The enamel is about 2 millimetres thick on the crown, and it is the hardest substance in the body. The pulp is in the centre: it has the nerve, which feels hot and cold, and the blood vessels, which feed the tooth. A child has 20 teeth, and the adult has 12 more.

A History of Dentistry

The history of dentistry is the history of the tooth: from the extraction to the prevention, from the barber-surgeon to the scientist. It is 5,000 years long, and it is the story of how a painful, feared treatment became a preventive, painless science. The history has five ages: the ancient, the medieval, the scientific, the anaesthetic, and the preventive, and each age changed what the dentist could do.

From Fauchard in 1728 to the first X-ray in 1895 is 167 years, and from the X-ray to the NHS in 1948 is 53 years. From the NHS in 1948 to the first implant in 1965 is 17 years, and from Fauchard in 1728 to 2026 is 298 years. The Egyptians treated teeth about 3000 BCE, which is about 5,000 years ago. The five ages are the ancient, the medieval, the scientific, the anaesthetic, and the preventive, and each lasted centuries except the last, which is still running. Dentistry is 5,000 years old, and the modern, preventive dentistry is less than 100 years old.

Tooth Decay

Tooth decay is the destruction of the tooth by acid. The bacteria in the plaque feed on sugar, and they make acid, and the acid dissolves the enamel. At first the damage is a white spot, and then it is a hole, the cavity. If the cavity is not treated, it reaches the dentine, and then the pulp, and the tooth aches, and it can be lost. Decay is the most common disease of the tooth, and it is preventable.

The acid attack lasts about 20 minutes after eating: the pH falls below 5.5, and the enamel dissolves. With 3 meals and 2 snacks a day, that is 5 attacks a day, and 100 minutes of acid a day. In a year, that is 1,825 attacks, and about 36,500 minutes of acid. Brushing removes the plaque, and the fluoride repairs the enamel between attacks: the balance between the attack and the repair decides whether the tooth decays. The white spot is the first sign: it can still be repaired by fluoride, but the cavity cannot, and it needs a filling.

Gum Disease

Gum disease is the disease of the gums and the bone that hold the teeth. It begins as gingivitis: the gums are red, swollen, and they bleed when brushed, but the bone is not yet damaged. If the plaque is not removed, it becomes periodontitis: the gum pulls away from the tooth, a pocket forms, and the bone is lost, and the tooth loosens and can be lost. Gum disease is the most common cause of tooth loss in adults, and it is preventable.

A healthy pocket is 1 to 3 millimetres deep, and a pocket of 5 millimetres or more means disease. Brushing twice a day and cleaning between the teeth removes the plaque at the gum line, where the disease starts. Gingivitis is reversible: the cleaning removes the plaque, and the gums heal in days. Periodontitis is not fully reversible: the bone that is lost does not grow back, but the cleaning stops the loss. The dentist measures the pockets at the check-up: the numbers are recorded, and the change over time shows whether the disease is controlled.

Brushing and Flossing

Brushing and flossing are the daily cleaning of the teeth: the brush cleans the surfaces, and the floss cleans between the teeth, where the brush cannot reach. Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, for 2 minutes, removes the plaque, and flossing once a day removes the plaque between the teeth. Together they are the foundation of dental prevention: the simplest, cheapest, and most effective treatment in dentistry.

Brushing twice a day for 2 minutes is 4 minutes a day, and 1,460 minutes a year, which is about 24 hours of brushing a year. Flossing once a day is 365 times a year. A toothbrush lasts about 3 months, so 4 brushes are needed a year. The 2 minutes cover the outer, inner, and chewing surfaces of every tooth: the upper teeth and the lower teeth, the front and the back. Spitting without rinsing leaves the fluoride on the teeth, and the fluoride repairs the enamel between brushings.

Fluoride

Fluoride is a mineral that strengthens the enamel and repairs the early damage of decay. It is found in toothpaste, in mouthwash, and in some water supplies, and it is applied by the dentist as a varnish. Fluoride works in two ways: it makes the enamel harder, so the acid dissolves it more slowly, and it helps the enamel to repair itself after the acid attack. Fluoride is the foundation of preventive dentistry, and it is the most effective single measure against decay.

The towns with fluoride in the water had about half the decay of the towns without it: if a town without fluoride had 10 decayed teeth per child, the town with fluoride had about 5. Brushing twice a day with fluoride toothpaste gives 730 fluoride applications a year. The varnish is applied by the dentist, and it stays on the teeth for hours, giving a long fluoride exposure. The balance is the attack and the repair: the acid dissolves the enamel, and the fluoride repairs it, and the fluoride tips the balance towards repair.

Dental Check-ups

A dental check-up is the regular examination of the mouth by the dentist: the teeth, the gums, and the soft tissues are examined, and the disease is found early, when it is small and cheap to treat. The check-up is the heart of preventive dentistry: it is not only the examination, but the cleaning, the advice, and the plan for the next visit. Most people are advised to have a check-up every 6 months, but the dentist decides the interval for each person.

A check-up every 6 months means 2 visits a year, and 20 visits in 10 years. A check-up takes about 30 minutes, so 20 visits are 600 minutes, or 10 hours, in 10 years. The interval is decided by the risk: a person at high risk is recalled every 3 months, which is 4 visits a year, and a person at low risk can wait 24 months, which is 1 visit in 2 years. The check-up finds the disease early: a small cavity is a small filling, and a large cavity is a large treatment, and the early finding saves the tooth and the money.

Fillings

A filling is the repair of a tooth damaged by decay: the decay is removed, the cavity is cleaned, and the filling material is placed, shaped, and hardened, so the tooth can bite and chew again. The filling replaces what the decay took away, and it seals the tooth against further decay. Fillings are the most common dental treatment, and they are the reason most teeth can be kept for life.

A filling takes about half an hour, and it is done in one visit. A tooth has 5 surfaces — the outer, the inner, the chewing, and the two sides — and the filling can cover 1 or more of them. If a person has 2 fillings a year for 10 years, that is 20 fillings, and each one saves a tooth. The composite is matched to the colour of the tooth, so the filling is hard to see. The amalgam lasts for decades, and the composite lasts for many years, and both are checked at the check-up: the filling that is worn or cracked is replaced.

Root Canal Treatment

Root canal treatment is the treatment of the pulp when it is infected or dead: the pulp is removed, the canals are cleaned and shaped, and they are filled and sealed, so the tooth can be kept. It is the alternative to extraction: the tooth is saved, and it can bite and chew again, usually with a crown on top. Root canal treatment is the treatment that saves the tooth when the decay has reached the pulp.

A molar has 3 or 4 canals, and a front tooth has 1: the number of canals is the number of channels to clean. The treatment takes 1 or 2 visits, and each visit takes about an hour. If a tooth has 4 canals, and each canal takes about 15 minutes to clean, that is about an hour of cleaning. After the treatment, the tooth is usually crowned: the crown protects the tooth, which is weaker without its pulp, and the tooth can last for many years. The alternative is extraction, and the root canal treatment saves the tooth.

Crowns and Bridges

A crown is a cap that covers a damaged tooth: it restores the shape, the strength, and the appearance, and it protects the tooth from breaking. A bridge is a row of crowns joined together: it replaces a missing tooth by anchoring to the teeth on either side. Crowns and bridges are the fixed replacements in dentistry: they are cemented in place, and they stay, and they let the person bite, chew, and smile again.

A bridge replacing 1 missing tooth has 3 units: 2 crowns on the abutments, and 1 pontic, the false tooth, in the middle. A bridge replacing 2 missing teeth has 4 units: 2 crowns and 2 pontics. The crown is made in about 2 weeks, and it is fitted in 2 visits: the preparation and the fitting. A crown can last for many years, and it is checked at the check-up: the crown that is loose or cracked is replaced. The computer-made crown can be fitted in a single visit: the tooth is scanned, and the crown is milled, and it is fitted the same day.

Dental Implants

A dental implant is an artificial root: a small titanium screw placed in the jaw, which the bone grows around, and which carries a crown. The implant replaces the missing tooth, root and crown, and it looks and works like a natural tooth. Implants are the most complete replacement in dentistry: they do not need the neighbouring teeth, and they preserve the bone, and they can last for many years.

Healing takes 3 to 6 months: the bone grows onto the implant, and the implant becomes part of the jaw. If healing takes 3 months, and the fitting takes 1 month, the whole treatment takes about 4 months. An implant replaces 1 tooth, and 2 implants can carry a bridge of 3 teeth, and 4 implants can carry a full denture. The implant is placed under anaesthesia, and the patient goes home the same day. The crown on the implant is checked at the check-up, like a natural tooth, and the implant can last for many years.

Orthodontics and Braces

Orthodontics is the branch of dentistry that straightens teeth and corrects the bite: the braces, the aligners, and the retainers move the teeth slowly into place. The teeth are moved by gentle, constant pressure, and the bone around them remodels, so the tooth moves and the bite improves. Orthodontics is both the appearance and the health: the straight teeth are easier to clean, and the even bite works better.

Treatment takes 1 to 2 years: the wire is adjusted every 4 to 6 weeks, so in 18 months there are about 13 adjustments. An aligner is worn for 22 hours a day, and it is changed every 1 or 2 weeks: in a year, that is about 26 to 52 aligners. The teeth move about 1 millimetre a month, so a tooth that must move 6 millimetres takes about 6 months. After the braces, the retainer is worn every night, for as long as the orthodontist advises, to hold the teeth in place.

Wisdom Teeth

Wisdom teeth are the third molars: the last teeth to arrive, at the back of the mouth, usually between 17 and 25. They are called wisdom teeth because they arrive when the person is older, and supposedly wiser. Often there is no room for them, and they become impacted — stuck against the tooth in front — and they can cause pain, infection, and damage to the neighbouring tooth, and then they are removed.

An adult has 4 wisdom teeth: 2 upper and 2 lower, and they arrive between 17 and 25, over about 8 years. If all 4 are removed in one visit, that is 4 extractions, and the healing takes about a week. The wisdom tooth is the third molar: the first molar arrives at about 6, the second at about 12, and the third at about 17 to 25. The jaw has room for 28 teeth, and the 4 wisdom teeth make 32: when the jaw is small, the last 4 have no room, and they impact.

Children's Dentistry

Children's dentistry is the dentistry of the child: the first visit, the baby teeth, the fissure sealants, and the fluoride. The child has 20 baby teeth, and they are replaced by 32 adult teeth, and the children's dentist guides the change. The aim is to prevent decay in the baby teeth, and to make the child comfortable with the dentist, so the adult keeps going. Children's dentistry is prevention, and it is the foundation of a lifetime of dental health.

A child has 20 baby teeth: 8 incisors, 4 canines, and 8 molars, which add to 20. The baby teeth erupt from about 6 months, and they are replaced from about 6 years, over about 6 years, until the child has 28 adult teeth, and then the 4 wisdom teeth arrive. The first visit should be by the first birthday, and the check-up is every 6 months: in 10 years, that is 20 visits. The fissure sealant is placed on the first adult molars at about 6, and it protects them from decay.

Dental Anaesthesia

Dental anaesthesia is the removal of sensation for dental treatment: the local anaesthetic numbs the tooth and the gum, so the treatment is painless. The anaesthetic is injected near the tooth, and it blocks the nerve, and the numbness lasts for a few hours. Anaesthesia is what made modern dentistry possible: without it, the filling, the extraction, and the root canal treatment would be feared, and many would avoid the dentist.

The numbness lasts for a few hours: if the injection is at 10 in the morning, the numbness wears off in the early afternoon. The local anaesthetic is injected near the tooth, and it blocks the nerve: the tooth feels nothing, but the patient is awake, and they can talk. In 1844, Wells used nitrous oxide, and in 1846, ether was used, and in 1943, lidocaine became the standard: the history of anaesthesia is 100 years from the gas to the local, and 80 years from the local to today. The injection takes seconds, and the waiting takes a few minutes, and the treatment takes as long as the treatment takes.

Dental X-Rays

A dental X-ray is a picture of the teeth and the bone, made with X-rays: it shows what the eye cannot see — the decay between the teeth, the root, the bone, and the tooth that has not yet erupted. The X-ray is the dentist's second pair of eyes: it finds the disease hidden below the surface, and it guides the treatment. The dose is small, and the benefit is the early finding of disease.

The first dental X-ray was taken in 1896, one year after the discovery of X-rays in 1895. The bitewing shows the back teeth, and there are 2 bitewings for the left and right, and 2 more for the upper and lower, so 4 bitewings show the back teeth. The panoramic shows the whole mouth in one picture: 32 teeth, 2 jaws, and the joints. The digital X-ray appears at once, and the film X-ray took minutes to develop: the digital is faster, and the dose is smaller. The X-ray is taken only when needed: the dentist justifies it, and the benefit outweighs the dose.

Oral Surgery

Oral surgery is the surgery of the mouth: the extraction of difficult teeth, the removal of the wisdom teeth, the implant, and the treatment of the jaw. The oral surgeon is the dentist who specialises in surgery, and the hospital is where the most difficult surgery is done. Oral surgery is the part of dentistry that cuts, removes, and rebuilds: it is the surgery of the teeth, the gums, and the jaws.

A simple extraction takes minutes, and a difficult one takes longer: the wisdom tooth in pieces takes more time than the loose tooth. The socket heals in about a week, and the bone fills in over months. An implant takes 3 to 6 months to heal, and a jaw operation takes longer: the hospital stay is days, and the healing is weeks. The oral surgeon does the difficult extraction, the implant, and the jaw surgery, and the general dentist does the simple extraction. The surgery is done under local anaesthesia in the surgery, and under general anaesthesia in the hospital.

Cosmetic Dentistry

Cosmetic dentistry is the dentistry of appearance: the whitening, the veneer, and the straightening that make the smile look better. It is the dentistry that is chosen, not needed: the teeth are healthy, but the person wants them whiter, straighter, or more even. Cosmetic dentistry is the newest part of dentistry, and it is the most popular: the smile is the first thing seen, and the cosmetic treatment changes it.

Whitening takes days or weeks: the gel is used at home, and the teeth become whiter gradually. A veneer is a thin shell, about 1 millimetre thick, and it is fitted in 2 visits: the preparation and the fitting. An aligner straightens the teeth in 1 to 2 years, and it is worn for 22 hours a day. The smile has 8 to 10 teeth visible: the upper teeth, and the lower, and the cosmetic treatment changes the visible ones. The cost is the person's, not the health service's: the cosmetic treatment is chosen, not needed, and it is paid for privately.

Dentures

Dentures are the removable replacements for missing teeth: the plate that carries the false teeth, and that sits on the gum. A full denture replaces all the teeth, and a partial denture replaces some, held by clasps on the remaining teeth. Dentures are the oldest replacement in dentistry: they restore the bite, the speech, and the appearance, and they can be taken out, cleaned, and put back.

A full denture replaces all the teeth in a jaw: 14 teeth in the upper jaw, and 14 in the lower, which is 28 teeth. A partial denture replaces the missing teeth: if 3 teeth are missing, the partial carries 3 false teeth, and it is held by clasps on the remaining teeth. The denture is made in about 5 visits, over a few weeks, and it is adjusted at each visit. The denture is cleaned daily: it is taken out, brushed, and soaked, and the mouth is brushed too. A denture lasts for years, and it is remade when the jaw changes shape.

Dental Emergencies

A dental emergency is the dental problem that cannot wait: the knocked-out tooth, the severe pain, the broken tooth, and the swelling. The emergency is treated at once, because the tooth can be saved if it is treated quickly, and the pain can be stopped. The most urgent is the knocked-out tooth: if it is put back within the hour, it can survive, and if it is left, it is lost.

The hour is the rule: the knocked-out tooth put back within 1 hour can survive, and after 1 hour the chance falls. A tooth kept moist in milk survives longer than a tooth left dry: the dry tooth dies in minutes, and the moist tooth survives for hours. Severe pain and swelling must be seen the same day: the abscess is treated, and the infection is stopped. A broken tooth is repaired: the piece is kept, and the dentist bonds it back, or the crown is placed. The emergency number is the dentist's, and the out-of-hours service is the backup, and the hospital is for the severe swelling that affects breathing.

The Dental Team

The dental team is the people who deliver dentistry: the dentist, the hygienist, the therapist, the nurse, the technician, and the receptionist. Each has a role, and the team works together: the dentist diagnoses and treats, the hygienist cleans, the therapist treats children, the nurse assists, the technician makes the crowns, and the receptionist organises the visits. The dental team is the organisation of dentistry, and it is what makes the care possible.

The team has 6 roles: the dentist, the hygienist, the therapist, the nurse, the technician, and the receptionist. A practice has 1 dentist for every 2,000 patients, and a team of 6 for every dentist: the dentist, the nurse, the hygienist, the therapist, the technician, and the receptionist. The hygienist was introduced in the 1940s, and the therapist in the 1960s: the team is about 80 years old. The patient sees the dentist every 6 months, and the hygienist at the same visit, and the team cares for the patient together.

Preventive Dentistry

Preventive dentistry is the dentistry that stops disease before it starts: the brushing, the fluoride, the check-up, the sealant, and the advice. It is the most important part of dentistry, and the cheapest: the prevention costs less than the treatment, and it saves the tooth. Preventive dentistry is the shift from the drill to the brush, from the treatment to the prevention, and it is the dentistry of the future.

Brushing twice a day for 2 minutes is 4 minutes a day, and 1,460 minutes a year. Cleaning between the teeth once a day is 365 times a year. A check-up every 6 months is 2 visits a year, and 20 visits in 10 years. The fissure sealant is placed on the first adult molars at about 6, and it protects them for years. The sugar frequency matters: 5 sugary snacks a day give 5 acid attacks, and 1 gives 1, and the fewer the attacks, the less the decay.

Dentistry in Public Health

Dentistry in public health is the dentistry of the population: the fluoride in the water, the school programmes, the free check-ups, and the campaigns that protect everyone's teeth, not just the patient's. It is the recognition that decay and gum disease are diseases of the population, and they are prevented by the population's measures. Public health dentistry is the dentistry of the many, and it is the most effective dentistry of all.

The towns with fluoride in the water had about half the decay: 10 decayed teeth per child became about 5. The school brushing programme reaches every child in the school, and the varnish is applied twice a year, so 2 applications a year per child. A free check-up every 6 months is 2 visits a year, and 20 visits in 10 years, for every child. The gap is wide: the poor have about twice the decay of the rich, and the public health measures close it, by giving the prevention to everyone, for free.
