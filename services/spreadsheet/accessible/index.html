Spreadsheet

A free spreadsheet. Type numbers and words into a grid of five columns (A to E) and six rows, write a formula starting with =, and watch the totals work out. No sign-in, no payment, free forever. Your numbers never leave the page.

Open the working spreadsheet (needs JavaScript)

How it works

  1. Type in the grid. Each box is a cell, named by its column letter and row number, such as B3. A cell holds one number, one word, or one formula.
  2. Write a formula. Start with =, then name cells and functions: =SUM(A1:A3) adds the three cells, =AVERAGE(A1:A3) averages them, and =A1+B1*2 does plain arithmetic. Brackets set the order.
  3. Press Work it out. The answers appear in a table below the grid, with a total for each column. Change a number and press the button again — every total follows the change.

What it does not do: it does not save your sheet for you, it does not draw charts, and it does not look things up in another sheet. Download your sheet as a CSV file before you leave, or your numbers will be gone when the page closes.

The formulas it knows

  • SUM — adds up every number in a range, such as =SUM(A1:A5).
  • AVERAGE — adds up the numbers in a range and divides by how many there are.
  • MAX — the largest number in a range. MIN — the smallest number in a range.
  • COUNT — how many cells in a range hold numbers.
  • Arithmetic — add (+), take away (-), multiply (*), divide (/), with brackets to set the order, such as =(A1+B1)*2.

What it does

  • What a spreadsheet is — a grid where numbers live in cells and formulas add them up by themselves.
  • Cells, rows and columns — one box, named by its letter and number.
  • Typing numbers and words — numbers line up on the right and can be added; words line up on the left and cannot.
  • Adding with SUM — =SUM(A1:A5) adds up every number in a range in one step.
  • Averaging with AVERAGE — answers the question: what is usual here?
  • The largest and the smallest — MAX finds the biggest number, MIN finds the smallest.
  • Counting cells with COUNT — says how many cells in a range hold numbers.
  • Arithmetic in a cell — add, take away, multiply, and divide, with brackets to set the order.
  • Copying a formula down a column — write a formula once and let the sheet repeat it for every row.
  • Money in a spreadsheet — money is kept as plain numbers, shown with a pound sign and two places after the point.
  • Dates in a spreadsheet — dates are numbers of days, so one date taken from another tells how many days passed.
  • Sorting a list — putting rows in order by a chosen column, smallest to largest or A to Z.
