How it works

Pick any square. Every square is one element: its number, its symbol, its name, and its mass. The square's place says how it behaves.
Read the details. Below the table you get the element's category, group, period, and atomic mass — and a plain-words note about what it does.
Search or filter. Type a name, a symbol, or a number, or pick a category such as Noble Gas, and the table narrows to the matches.

What it cannot do: it cannot show what an element looks like in a laboratory, it cannot predict a reaction, and the masses shown for elements made only in laboratories are the whole numbers of their longest-lived forms, such as 294 for oganesson, not measured averages. All 118 names, symbols, atomic numbers, and categories come from the live Periodic Elements Database, which holds 118 full records.

Good to know

Six things worth knowing about the table.

Hydrogen, element 1

The lightest element, one proton, one electron — and the most common element in the universe. It makes water with oxygen.

Read the full record →

Iron, copper, zinc

The metals of daily life: iron builds bridges, copper carries electricity, zinc coats steel against rust.

Read the full record →

Gold and silver

They do not tarnish and do not rust. Gold never corrodes; silver is the best carrier of electricity of any metal.

Read the full record →

Silicon, element 14

The second most common element in the Earth's crust — and the heart of every computer chip and solar cell.

Read the full record →

The noble gases

Helium, neon, argon, krypton, xenon, radon, and oganesson — full outer shells, so they react with almost nothing.

Read the full record →

Made, not found

Elements past uranium do not occur in nature. They are made one atom at a time — oganesson, 118, exists only as a handful of atoms.
