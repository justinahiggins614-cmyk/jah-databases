Section 1 — Description and history

Signature Calendar is a mirror of the calendar kind of website: a website whose job is to hold dates, events, and reminders in date order, so a visitor can see what falls on one day and find events of one kind. The calendar kind of website began as lists of days with notes beside them, then as grids of dates with events written in. This mirror follows that same shape: dates, event titles, kinds, and a search over the events.

What this mirror lists is the network's own material. Its sample set is ten Signature sample events made for this mirror, about the network's own subjects. They are labeled plainly as Signature sample events, not as real events on real dates. The network itself holds 63,826 databases and 12,367,686 records across the 39 websites and the 2,700 per-artifact websites.

Section 2 — Math proof (standard math + metamath)

The mirror's filter rule is a deterministic function. For a query Q of words q1 … qn and an event E with a title, a date, and a kind, the event matches when:

match(Q, E) is true when every word of Q appears in E's title or kind

Results are ordered by date, earliest first; ties are broken alphabetically by title, so the order is the same every time for the same query. An event on a date D is found by the rule: eventsOn(D) returns every event whose date equals D, in date order.

Worked example: the query "walk" against the sample set. Two events carry the word "walk" in their title: "Sample rain walk" (2026-12-09) and "Sample map walk" (2026-12-11). Both match, and the earlier date lists first. The query "cooking" matches two events: "Sample bread baking morning" (2026-12-06) and "Sample soup lunch" (2026-12-12), and again the earlier date lists first.

Section 3 — Science proof

The science behind this mirror is how time is measured and how events are placed on it: days follow each other in order, weeks and months group the days, and an event sits on one date. A calendar works because dates have a fixed order: any two dates can be compared, and the earlier one always comes first. The mirror uses the simplest honest form of that idea: a fixed sample set of events, each carrying a title, a date, and a kind, with a filter that keeps only the events carrying every word asked for.

Two measures describe how good such a filter is. Precision is the share of returned events that actually match what was asked. Recall is the share of matching events in the set that the filter actually returned. This mirror's rule — every query word must appear — favours precision: it returns fewer events, but every event it returns contains every word you asked for. The dates are the dates of the sample events; they are not dates of real events.
