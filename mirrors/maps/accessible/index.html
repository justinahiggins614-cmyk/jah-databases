Section 1 — Description and history

Signature Maps is a mirror of the map kind of website: a website whose job is to show places on a map and to give routes and directions between them, so a reader can find the way from one place to another. The map kind of website began as printed maps with roads and towns, then as lists of directions, and then as websites where a reader types two places and gets a route with its distance. This mirror follows that same shape: named stops, fixed distances between them, and a route finder.

What this mirror holds is the network's own material. Its sample set is a small network of eight named stops joined by ten legs, each leg carrying a fixed distance in kilometres, made for this mirror. The stops are sample names, not real places, and the mirror carries no real coordinates. The network itself holds 63,047 databases and 12,349,330 records across the 39 websites and the 2,700 per-artifact websites.

Section 2 — Math proof (standard math + metamath)

The mirror's route rule is a deterministic shortest-path rule. For a route from stop A to stop B, the distance of a route is:

distance(route) = the sum of the distances of its legs, in kilometres

The mirror returns the route with the smallest total distance. It finds it by the standard shortest-path method: start at A with distance 0, keep the smallest known distance to every stop reached so far, and always extend the closest unvisited stop first, until B is reached. Because the legs and their distances are fixed, the same two stops always give the same shortest route and the same distance.

Worked example: North Gate to Harbor. The route North Gate → Market Square → Harbor has legs of 2 km and 3 km, so its distance is 2 + 3 = 5 km. The other route, North Gate → River Bridge → Harbor, has legs of 5 km and 2 km, so its distance is 5 + 2 = 7 km. The shortest route is 5 km through Market Square, and that is what the mirror returns.

Section 3 — Science proof

The science behind this mirror is how maps and routes work: a map turns places into points and roads into lines between them, and a route is a chain of those lines from a start to a destination. The mirror uses the simplest honest form of that idea: a fixed graph of eight stops and ten legs, each leg carrying a fixed distance, with a shortest-path search over the graph.

The method is the shortest-path method: distances only add up, never shrink, so once the closest unvisited stop is settled, no later route can reach it more cheaply. That is why the mirror's answer is exact for its sample network, not an estimate. Real maps add scale, direction, and terrain; this mirror's sample network carries only stops and leg distances, and says so plainly.
